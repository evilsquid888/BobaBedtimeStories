# Story 82: Iron Egg & the Lost-and-Found Lighthouse

**Main Character:** Tiě Dàn (Iron Egg) — a tiny, dark, chewy salt-and-soy braised egg from Tamsui, gruff and crusty on the outside
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** The old Tamsui (Danshui) harbor and its red-brick lighthouse at dusk
**Theme:** What looks tough on the outside can be the gentlest helper of all
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 燈塔 (dēngtǎ) = lighthouse

---

At the bottom of a paper bag at the very end of the Tamsui boardwalk lived the grumpiest little snack in all of Taiwan, and his name was Iron Egg.

He was no bigger than a thumb, dark as a chestnut, and so wrinkled that he looked like a tiny raisin who had argued with a prune and lost. He had been braised in salt and soy and star anise so many times that his shell had gone hard and chewy and shiny, and — between you and me, dear reader — he was rather proud of being difficult to bite.

"Nobody wants me," Iron Egg grumbled, which was the first thing he said every single morning. "I'm too tough. I'm too dark. I'm too *chewy*. Children want pillowy things. Soft things. Squishy things." He gave a hard little harrumph. "I'm a snack you have to *work* at."

All day long he watched the boardwalk shoppers stroll past his bag. Their eyes slid right over him and landed on the marshmallowy mochi, the fluffy pineapple cakes, the wibbly-wobbly grass jelly. "See?" Iron Egg would mutter, sinking deeper into the crinkles of his paper bag. "A soft world wants soft snacks. And I am about as soft as a doorknob." He was so good at grumbling that the seagulls had started using him as an alarm clock.

And down the boardwalk, the warm orange sun began to sink into the sea.

Now, you should know that just above Iron Egg's paper bag stood the most important building on the whole harbor: a tall, round, red-brick **燈塔** (dēngtǎ) = lighthouse, with a glass lantern at the very top that swung on a long rope and pulley. Every evening, the lighthouse keeper — a kindly old **阿嬤** (āmà) = grandmother named Auntie Pomelo, who was herself a fragrant green pomelo with reading glasses — climbed the spiral stairs to light the lamp so the little fishing-snacks out at sea could find their way home.

"Without the lamp," Iron Egg muttered, "those silly snacks would bonk right into the rocks. Good thing it's not *my* job. Soft jobs need soft helpers."

But that very evening, something went wrong.

Auntie Pomelo did not climb the stairs.

She sat at the bottom of the lighthouse with a blanket round her rind, her leafy top drooping, sniffling into a hankie the size of a sail. "Oh dear, oh dear," she wheezed. "I've caught the harbor sniffles. My legs have gone all wobbly-jelly. I cannot climb. And worse — *much* worse —" she pointed a trembling green finger out at the water — "the **霧** (wù) = fog is coming in."

Iron Egg followed her finger. And his little soy-dark heart did a flip.

Rolling across the sea was a fog so thick and gray it looked like somebody had spilled a giant bowl of soy milk across the whole world. It swallowed the waves. It swallowed the sunset. It swallowed the far-off shape of the rocks. And out *there*, somewhere inside all that gray, bobbed a tiny fishing boat crammed full of little snacks — fish balls and squid crackers and sugared peanuts — who had gone out to play at sea and now could not, for the life of them, find the shore.

"Help!" came a faraway, muffled, wobbling voice. "We can't see the lighthouse! We've gone round in three circles and a squiggle! Which way is hooooome?"

"Goodness, goodness," Auntie Pomelo fretted, her glasses sliding right off her round green nose. "Those poor little snacks. If they bonk the rocks they'll go all soggy, and a soggy squid cracker is the saddest sight in all of Tamsui."

"Somebody must light the lamp!" cried Auntie Pomelo. "And somebody must call them in! But I'm too poorly to climb, and my voice is too soft and sneezy to carry over the water." She blew her nose with a sound like a tugboat. "*Hooonk.*"

Iron Egg looked left. He looked right. There was nobody else on the whole boardwalk. Only him: one small, hard, wrinkled, chewy egg who had spent his entire life being absolutely certain he was no good for soft, kind, important jobs.

"Oh, *coconuts*," he grumbled. And he started to climb.

Up the spiral stairs he went, bumpity-bumpity-bump, because he had no legs and had to roll up each step like a tiny dark bowling ball with opinions. It was the tallest, twistiest, dizziest climb of his life. "Whose idea — *oof* — was it — *oof* — to put the important light — *oof* — at the *top*?" he wheezed, rolling over a step and clonking his wrinkly head on the next one. "When this is over I am writing a very stern letter." By the time he reached the top he was puffing so hard his star-anise smell came out in little clouds, and a passing moth got so cozy from the smell it fell asleep mid-flutter.

There stood the great glass lamp — already lit, glowing warm and golden — but it would not stay *up*. It hung on a rope that ran over a pulley, and at the other end of the rope was a counterweight pouch, and the pouch was *empty*. So the heavy lamp kept slipping down, *creeeak*, sinking below the window, its beautiful light vanishing behind the bricks where no boat could see it.

"The counterweight!" Iron Egg gasped. "There's nothing to hold the lamp steady! I need something small and heavy. Something dense. Something dark and hard and — "

He stopped.

He looked down at his own small, heavy, dense, dark, hard, wrinkled little body.

"...Oh," said Iron Egg, very quietly. "*Oh.*"

And reader, I am delighted to tell you that the grumpiest snack on the boardwalk did the bravest thing of his whole life. He rolled himself right into the empty counterweight pouch and went *plop*.

The rope went taut. The pulley gave a happy little *ding*. And the great glass lamp rose smoothly up, up, up to the very top of the window — and held there, perfectly steady, because the perfect weight to anchor it was one small braised egg who had been braised so many times he was practically a pebble.

The golden light shot out across the fog like a sword of warm honey.

"It's working!" Iron Egg shouted — and then he remembered the second job. The calling. The snacks at sea still couldn't *hear* which way home was. They needed a foghorn. A big, low, booming voice to roll across the water.

Iron Egg had spent years being told his voice was too gruff. Too rough. Too gravelly and deep and grumbly for polite snack company.

He grinned a hard little grin.

He took the biggest breath a thumb-sized egg has ever taken. And from the top of the **燈塔**, anchored in his pouch, glowing gold, he let his gruff voice roll out low and slow and warm across the sea:

"*HELLOOOO out there. This way, little ones. Foooollow my voice. Hooome is right heeere.*"

It was not a scary sound. It was the coziest, deepest, kindest rumble you ever heard — like a cat purring inside a drum, like your grandfather humming you to sleep. It rolled over the waves and curled right into the fog.

Far out at sea, the little boat of snacks lifted their heads.

"Did you hear that?" whispered a fish ball.

"It came from *that* way," said a squid cracker.

"No, *that* way," argued another fish ball, who had absolutely no sense of direction and was, in fact, pointing at a fish.

But then the grumbly voice rolled out again — "*Steady, steady, I'm right here*" — and this time there was no mistaking it. It was the warmest sound in the whole cold fog, and every snack on board turned toward it at once, the way a sunflower turns toward the sun.

"Follow the warm grumbly voice!" cheered the sugared peanuts. "It sounds like a hug that learned to talk!"

And so Iron Egg called and called, low and steady, while the golden lamp held firm above him. "*This way. Steady now. Almost home. I've got you.*" He didn't stop, not once, not even when his throat went scratchy, because somewhere down in his hard little middle he had discovered something soft after all — the warmest, gentlest, most patient thing in the world, which had been hiding inside his crusty shell the whole time.

Slowly, out of the soy-milk fog, a shape appeared. Then a sail. Then the dear bobbing nose of the little boat, following the warm gold light and the warm low voice like a duckling following its mama.

One by one, out of the fog, the boat came sliding home. Bump. It nudged the dock. The fish balls bounced ashore. The squid crackers did a happy crinkly dance. The sugared peanuts rolled in a giggling pile right onto Auntie Pomelo's blanket, who said "*oof*" and then laughed so hard her leaves wiggled.

"We're home! We're home! The grumbly beacon saved us!"

Auntie Pomelo looked up the spiral stairs, her glasses fogged with happy tears. "Iron Egg," she called softly. "Come down, you wonderful, terrible, marvelous little egg."

Iron Egg rolled back down the stairs, bumpity-bump, and landed in a small dark heap at the bottom, completely out of puff and entirely covered in pulley dust.

Every snack on the harbor gathered round him. And the fish ball — the squishiest, softest, pillowiest snack of all — leaned over and said the thing Iron Egg had spent his whole life being sure no one would ever say:

"You're the best helper on the whole boardwalk. You held the light *and* you brought us home. Who knew the toughest little snack would turn out to be the warmest one?"

Iron Egg's hard, wrinkled, soy-dark face went all crinkly. It was, if you looked very closely, a smile.

"Hmph," he said. But it was a happy hmph. The happiest hmph the harbor had ever heard.

Auntie Pomelo wrapped her blanket around him, sniffles and all, and the snacks brought him a thimble of warm tea and a tiny seat of honor right by the lighthouse door. And do you know what Iron Egg realized, sitting there all wrapped up and warm? He realized his hard shell had never been a *wall* keeping the world out. It had been a *boat* — sturdy enough to carry everything gentle and kind that lived inside him, all the way across the sea to where it was needed most.

From that night on, whenever the fog rolled in, the snacks of Tamsui would look up at the red-brick **燈塔** and see the lamp glowing steady and gold — anchored by one small, dense, brave little egg — and they would hear his low, kind, grumbly voice rolling over the water, calling everybody safely home.

And nobody, not ever again, called Iron Egg too tough.

They called him the Beacon.

## Goodnight Blessing

May you know, little one, that the crusty outside of things is only a shell — and that the gentlest helpers often look the toughest of all. May your light hold steady when the fog rolls in, and may a warm, low voice always call you home through the dark. You are stronger than you feel and softer than you know, and somebody out there is so very glad you exist. Now snuggle down, let the lighthouse keep watch, and drift off on the warm gold sea of sleep. Goodnight.
