# Story 55: The Little Tiger God Under the Table

**Main Character:** Xiǎo Hǔ Yé (Tiger God)
**Guest Characters:** Lǔ Ròu (Braised Pork Rice)
**Setting:** Under the offering table of a cozy neighborhood temple
**Theme:** Even guardians need a friend and a nap.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 老虎 (lǎohǔ) = tiger

---

Underneath the offering table of the coziest little temple on the whole block, there lived a tiger who was about the size of a teapot.

He was made of painted clay — orange and cream, with crooked black stripes that someone had clearly drawn in a hurry, one curly tail like a question mark, and a round belly you simply wanted to poke. His grin was so goofy that nobody had ever once been frightened of him, which was a small problem, because being frightening was, technically, his entire job.

His name was **小虎爺** (Xiǎo Hǔ Yé) = Little Tiger Master, and he was a real, genuine **老虎** (lǎohǔ) = tiger. He would tell you so himself.

"RAWR," said Xiǎo Hǔ Yé to the empty temple, very fiercely. "Rawr means hello!"

Nobody answered. So he flopped onto his back and stared up at the bottom of the table, the way he did most days, and most nights, and most of the in-between-ish times too.

Being a guardian, it turned out, was lonely work.

---

You see, the Tiger God lived under the table of **土地公** (Tǔdì Gōng) = the Earth God, the jolly little white-bearded grandfather spirit who blessed the market. And ordinarily that would be marvelous company. But the Earth God had gone off on a blessing-trip three temples over, to bless a baby, a bridge, and a particularly nervous mango tree, and he'd be away for a week.

"Mind the place, Tiger," the Earth God had chuckled on his way out, popping a lucky candy under the table. "Guard it well."

So Xiǎo Hǔ Yé guarded.

He guarded the morning, when the incense curled up like sleepy cats.

He guarded the afternoon, when sunbeams crossed the floor and there was honestly nothing to guard them from.

He guarded the evening, practicing his fiercest faces in a puddle of reflection. "Behold," he growled, "the mighty lǎohǔ!" Then he yawned, an enormous, jaw-cracking, ear-folding yawn, which is the thing tigers his size do most of all. His roars, if we are being completely honest, were really just big yawns wearing a costume.

By the seventh lonely day, Xiǎo Hǔ Yé had run out of faces to practice. He had counted the floorboards (eleven). He had named the dust bunnies (Steve, mostly). He had even tried guarding his own tail, which only resulted in him going in three dignified circles before getting dizzy and sitting down hard.

He had also, somewhere around the fifth day, invented games to keep himself busy. There was Pounce-the-Sunbeam, which he always lost, because sunbeams are notoriously slippery and also not really there. There was Statue, which he was *excellent* at, on account of being a statue. And there was his favorite, a deeply important game called Guard the Offering — in which he sat perfectly still beside whatever snack someone had left on the table, protecting it bravely from all dangers, right up until the precise moment he ate it.

"A guardian's duty," he would explain solemnly to Steve, licking crumbs off his clay paw, "is to make *absolutely certain* the offering is good enough for the spirits. And the only way to be certain... is to test it. Thoroughly. *All* of it."

Steve the dust bunny, being a dust bunny, offered no opinion. This was both the best and the worst thing about Steve.

"It's not that I'm lonely," he announced to Steve the dust bunny. "Tigers are not lonely. Tigers are *independent.*"

His tummy rumbled like distant thunder.

"...Tigers are also," he admitted in a much smaller voice, "extremely hungry."

---

Now, just as the temple bell gave a soft and sleepy *bonggg*, the front step creaked, and in walked the warmest little bowl you ever saw.

He was a humble round bowl of rice, crowned with glistening, shiny minced pork that shone like polished wood, and balanced on top — like a perfect little hat — was a single marbled tea egg. He smelled of five-spice and soy sauce and somebody's kitchen on a cold night. He smelled, in a word, like *home.*

This was **滷肉** (Lǔ Ròu) = Braised Pork Rice, and he had come to leave an offering, the way folks do, setting a steaming bowl on the table to say thank you to the spirits.

"Evening," said Lǔ Ròu, in his slow, homey voice. He set himself gently on the offering table. "Simple things, done with love. That's all I've got. Hope somebody up there likes pork."

Under the table, the Tiger God's nose went *twitch.*

Then *twitch twitch.*

Then his whole face went after the smell like it was being reeled in on a fishing line, and before he could stop himself, a voice rumbled up from beneath the tablecloth:

"...*Somebody* down here likes pork."

Lǔ Ròu blinked. He peeked under the table.

And there, in the dusty dark, was a teapot-sized clay tiger with a guilty grin, one paw already halfway toward the offering bowl, frozen like a kid caught reaching into the cookie jar.

For a long moment, nobody moved.

"RAWR," said Xiǎo Hǔ Yé weakly. "...Rawr means I wasn't doing anything."

---

Lǔ Ròu did not run. Lǔ Ròu did not gasp. Lǔ Ròu, being the kind of bowl who had warmed a thousand cold tables, simply chuckled and scooted himself off the offering table, *down* onto the floor, and right under into the dark, until he was sitting cozily nose-to-nose with the startled little Tiger God.

"Well," said Lǔ Ròu. "I came to feed the guardian of this temple. Reckon I found him."

"You're not scared?" asked Xiǎo Hǔ Yé, astonished. "I'm a *tiger.* A fierce one. People are *supposed* to be scared."

"Son," said Lǔ Ròu warmly, "you've got rice on your whiskers and you named the dust bunny Steve. I heard you."

Xiǎo Hǔ Yé turned a deeper shade of clay-orange, which is the tiger version of blushing. "...He's good company," he mumbled. "But he doesn't say much."

Lǔ Ròu nudged the bowl closer between them. "Then let's fix the company. Eat. A guardian shouldn't guard on an empty belly. Slurp first, worry later — though I think that one belongs to my big brother Niú Niú."

And so the fiercest little lǎohǔ on the block and the humblest little bowl of rice in Taiwan shared a midnight supper under a temple table, passing the tea-egg hat back and forth (they each ate exactly half, which is the politest way), while the incense smoke drew slow lazy curls above them.

---

The tea egg, by the way, was magnificent — marbled all over with beautiful cracked-tea patterns, like a tiny map of someplace warm. Xiǎo Hǔ Yé held his half up to the lantern light and admired it.

"You know," he said, "I once met a tea egg who said every crack tells a story. Dàn Dàn, I think his name was. Very wise. Wandered off to read someone's fortune in a convenience-store cooker."

"Sounds like a fella worth knowing," said Lǔ Ròu, chewing thoughtfully. "Most folks are, once you sit down and share a bowl with 'em."

"That's just it," said Xiǎo Hǔ Yé, and his voice went small again. "Nobody sits down. They leave the offering, they bow, they hurry off into the night. They never *stay.* I've been guarding this corner since before the paint on my stripes was dry, and tonight is the first time in a very, *very* long while that anyone has sat with me long enough to learn that I named the dust bunny Steve."

He took a slow, sad bite of rice.

"I think," he confessed, "I'm very good at my job and very bad at not being lonely."

Between more bites, Xiǎo Hǔ Yé told Lǔ Ròu everything. How he loved his job, truly he did. How he kept the corner safe and the bad-luck nibblers away and the temple cozy. How he chased off the gloomy moths and frightened away the squeaky floor-creaks (or tried to — the floor-creaks were stubborn). But how the days went *so* slow with the Earth God off blessing nervous mango trees, and how a guardian, it turns out, can guard a place beautifully and *still* feel like the loneliest tiger in the world.

Lǔ Ròu listened the whole way through, the way only a really good bowl of rice can listen.

"Here's a thing my grandmother-pot used to say," Lǔ Ròu said at last. "A guard who guards alone gets cold. But two by the fire stay warm *and* still watch the door." He patted the tiger's round belly with a little grain of rice. "You don't have to choose between doing your duty and having a friend, kiddo. The trick is finding a friend who'll sit *under the table* with you."

The Tiger God's pink-painted eyes went very wide and very shiny.

"...Will you?" he whispered. "Sit under the table? With me?"

"I deliver offerings every single night," said Lǔ Ròu. "Cold rice helps nobody. From now on, I bring the bowl, you keep me warm with all that fierce tiger heat, and we guard this little corner together. Deal?"

Xiǎo Hǔ Yé did not say *deal.* Xiǎo Hǔ Yé did the most enormous thing a teapot-sized clay tiger can do.

He pounced — gently — into a hug, knocking the empty bowl spinning like a top, and let out the loudest, happiest, most rib-rattling **ROAR** of his entire life.

It was, of course, just a very big yawn. But it was a *joyful* one, and joyful yawns are extremely contagious, so Lǔ Ròu yawned too, and the incense smoke seemed to yawn, and even Steve the dust bunny appeared to settle a little deeper into the floor.

And so it began — the coziest arrangement on the whole block.

Every single night, just as the temple bell gave its sleepy *bonggg*, Lǔ Ròu would come padding up the front step with a fresh warm bowl, and the two of them would tuck themselves under the offering table and have their supper together. Lǔ Ròu taught the Tiger God to share properly (one bite for you, one bite for me, and *only* one bite of testing the spirits' portion). Xiǎo Hǔ Yé taught Lǔ Ròu his fierce faces, which Lǔ Ròu pronounced "the most terrifying things I have ever seen, truly, I am quaking," in a voice so warm and unquaking that they both fell over laughing.

They guarded together, too. When a real gloomy moth came fluttering in one night, looking to dim a lantern, Xiǎo Hǔ Yé puffed up his round belly and let out his biggest yawn-roar, and Lǔ Ròu rattled his tea-egg hat like a tiny war-drum, and the startled moth flapped right back out into the dark where it belonged. They high-fived. (Lǔ Ròu, having no hands, high-fived with a grain of rice. It still counted.)

"See?" said Lǔ Ròu, settling back down. "Two by the fire. Warm *and* watching the door."

"Two by the fire," agreed Xiǎo Hǔ Yé, and for the first time in a very long while, the slow nights didn't feel slow at all. They felt like the best part of the day.

---

When the Earth God came home a few days later — smelling of tangerines and freshly-blessed bridges — he peeked under his own offering table and found the most heart-warming sight in all the market.

There, curled together in a cozy nest of tablecloth, were a little clay tiger and a little bowl of braised pork rice, fast asleep, the tea egg tucked between them like a shared pillow. The Tiger God was snoring his soft, rumbly, not-at-all-scary snore, and Lǔ Ròu had a contented soy-sauce sheen, and the corner had never, ever looked so well-guarded.

"Well, bless this little corner of the island," the Earth God whispered, and he tiptoed away so as not to wake them, leaving two lucky candies instead of one.

From that night on, the temple had not one guardian but two — the coziest guardian duo on the whole block. And if you ever pass a little neighborhood temple at suppertime and catch the warm smell of five-spice drifting up from somewhere low to the floor, peek under the offering table.

You might just spy a fierce little lǎohǔ and his very best friend, keeping the whole world cozy, one shared bowl at a time.

---

## Goodnight Blessing
May you, like the little Tiger God, learn that being brave and being snuggled can happen at exactly the same time. May there always be someone willing to sit under the table with you, share their warm bowl, and call your loudest yawn a mighty roar. Tonight, let the incense curl soft, let the lucky candies wait until morning, and let your worries nap somewhere far away. You are guarded. You are loved. You are warm as fresh rice on a cold night. Goodnight.
