# Story 73: Almond Tofu & the Cloud-Sorting Job

**Main Character:** Xìngrén Dòufu (Almond Tofu) — a soft, pale, jiggly almond pudding who is a worrier about order and neatness
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A misty mountain teahouse perched in the clouds above Maokong
**Theme:** Not everything needs to be tidy to be lovely
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 雲 (yún) = cloud

---

Xìngrén Dòufu was the wobbliest worrier on the whole mountain.

He was a small square of almond pudding, pale as moonmilk and so jiggly that when he hiccupped, his whole body shimmied like a bowl of nervous moonlight. He smelled faintly of toasted almonds and had a habit of nudging things into perfectly straight lines — pebbles, teacups, his own four jiggly corners, which simply would not stay square no matter how firmly he patted them.

So when a job notice fluttered down the mountain that read *WANTED: ONE TIDY SOUL TO SORT THE CLOUDS*, Almond Tofu wobbled so fast he nearly bounced off the path.

"A sorting job!" he gasped, clutching the notice. "For *me!*"

He had never wanted anything so much in his entire jiggly life. You see, Almond Tofu's secret, most private dream was to walk into a room that was *already* messy and leave it *tidy* — and to be *thanked* for it. A whole job made of sorting! It was almost too much happiness for one small pudding to hold.

Up he climbed, through the misty tea terraces of Maokong, where the gondola cables hummed somewhere far below in the fog. He passed tea bushes beaded with dew (he straightened a few crooked leaves on the way, just out of habit) and climbed a winding stone stair until he reached a teahouse that perched in the sky like a sleepy lantern. And there, drifting all around it, were the clouds.

They were an absolute *disaster.*

---

The 雲 (yún) = clouds were everywhere, and not one of them was behaving.

They puffed. They smeared. They flopped over the railings and squashed themselves against the windows and bonked gently into one another like a hundred lazy lambs who'd forgotten how legs worked. One enormous cloud had draped itself across the whole front porch like a spilled bowl of soup.

Almond Tofu's corners went all aquiver.

"Oh, *no,*" he whispered. "Oh, this is *terrible.* This is the messiest mess I have ever messily seen."

A round little teapot sat steaming on the porch railing — old and chipped and brown as a chestnut, with a lid that tilted at a cheerful, crooked angle. This was Lǎo Hú, the teahouse's ancient teapot, and she had watched the 雲 drift over this mountain for two hundred years.

"You must be the new sorter," she said, puffing a curl of jasmine steam. "Welcome, little pudding. The clouds are yours to arrange. Make them lovely for the guests."

"Lovely!" Almond Tofu repeated, and a determined wobble ran from his top to his toes. "Don't you worry, Madam Teapot. I'll have these clouds in tidy little rows before the tea even cools. Straight rows. Even spaces. *Sorted.*"

Lǎo Hú's crooked lid tilted just a smidge more. "Mm," she said, which is the noise wise old teapots make instead of arguing.

---

Almond Tofu got to work.

He scooted up to the big soup-spilly cloud on the porch and pushed it with both jiggly arms. *Squidge.* The cloud rolled obediently into place beside its neighbor — and then, the very instant he turned his back, it sighed and oozed right back to where it had started.

"No, no, *no,*" said Almond Tofu. "You go *there.* In the row. With the others."

He pushed it again. It oozed back again. He pushed it a third time, lost his footing on a slippery patch of mist, and went rolling head-over-corners right *through* the cloud, popping out the other side wearing a fluffy white hat of fog.

"Pffbt," he said, spitting out cloud.

This was going to be harder than he thought.

He tried herding next. He found a long bamboo tea-whisk and used it like a tiny broom, sweeping clouds into a line — *swish, swish, swish* — neat as you please, a perfect parade of puffs all in a row. He even gave them numbers under his breath. "One. Two. Three. Four. Stay, stay, stay, *stay.*" He stepped back to admire them, his almond heart swelling with pride. They were so straight! So even! So gloriously *organized!*

And then a breeze came along and stirred the whole row together into one big swirly blob.

"AH," said Almond Tofu. "No no no, come *back,* you've lost your place in line!"

He scrambled after them with the whisk, *swish-swish-swishing* twice as fast, but the faster he swept the more they tangled, until at last he was spinning in a damp little circle, whisking madly at nothing, while the clouds tumbled over each other behind him in fits of what could only be described as cloud-giggles.

He tried tying them. He found a length of soft red string left over from a mooncake box and looped it around two clouds, knotting them gently together so they couldn't wander. The clouds simply drifted in opposite directions, dragging the string taut, then *boing* — sprang back and bonked into each other with a noise like two pillows shaking hands. The string slipped right through them as if they were made of breath. Which, of course, they very nearly were.

He tried stacking them. He scooped one small cloud onto another, then a third on top of that, building a tidy fluffy tower, holding his wobbly breath, balancing ever so carefully — and the whole stack went *poof* and spread out flat like spilled milk the moment he let go. Almond Tofu was left holding nothing but a fistful of damp.

"Hmph," he said, shaking the mist off his arm. "Rude."

He tried very politely *asking* them, too. He climbed up on a tea stool, cleared his throat with a small jiggly *ahem,* and addressed the entire sky in his squarest, most serious voice.

"Excuse me. *Excuse* me. If the clouds in the back could please find their assigned spaces, and the clouds in the front could stop *lounging,* we'll have everything sorted in no time. Thank you. *Thank* you."

The clouds did not appear to speak Pudding. One of them yawned an enormous, slow, foggy yawn directly in his face, and Almond Tofu toppled backward off the stool with a soft *bonk.*

So then — and this was his very best idea, he was sure of it — he drew little chalk boxes on the sky. Well, on the cool damp air where the sky should be, which took a great deal of patience and an even greater deal of standing on tiptoe. He labeled them in careful, wobbly letters: CLOUD ONE and CLOUD TWO and CLOUD THREE, all in a tidy grid, and then tried to scoot each cloud into its proper box like an usher seating very fluffy theatergoers.

The clouds did not read chalk. The clouds did not, it turned out, read *anything.* They blended cheerfully past every line he drew, mingling and mooshing and trading edges until he couldn't tell where Cloud One ended and Cloud Three began — and somewhere in all the mooshing, Cloud Two appeared to have eaten Cloud Four entirely. By now a dozen clouds had melted together into a single rolling tide that lapped at the teahouse porch like a slow, lazy white sea, erasing his chalk boxes one by one with soft little *whoosh*es, as if politely cleaning up after a child who had drawn on the walls.

Almond Tofu chased the last unsorted puff clear around the porch, lunged for it with both arms wide, missed completely, and skidded on the slick mist all the way to the railing, where he came to rest upside down with his rounded corners pointing at the sky.

Almond Tofu sat down on the porch with a defeated *splat.* His corners drooped. A bit of mist had soaked clear through his middle, so he sloshed faintly when he sniffled.

"I can't do it," he wobbled. "They won't line up. They won't *stay.* They just keep — keep —" He flapped a soggy arm at the swirling, mooshing, gloriously disobedient cloud-sea. "—*mixing.*"

---

Lǎo Hú let out a long, warm puff of steam.

"Come here, little worrier," she said gently. "Let me pour you something. And let me show you a secret."

Almond Tofu sloshed over. The teapot tipped her crooked lid toward the western railing, where the whole sky had begun to do something extraordinary.

The sun was setting.

And the clouds — the messy, mingled, never-once-tidy clouds — were catching the light. They glowed peach and rose and a soft sleepy gold. Where they blended into one another, the colors *swirled,* pink melting into amber melting into the gentle lilac of evening, like someone had stirred a sunset into cream and forgotten to stop. The whole cloud-sea rolled and shimmered, no two puffs alike, no edge where one ended and the next began, all of it pouring together into one enormous, breathing, beautiful glow.

Almond Tofu forgot to wobble.

He just *looked.*

"Oh," he whispered. "Oh, my."

"You see?" said Lǎo Hú softly. "Two hundred years I've watched these 雲. Guests climb the whole mountain — past the tea trees, past the temple bells — just to sit on this porch at sundown. And do you know what they come to see?"

"...The tidy rows?" Almond Tofu guessed, very quietly, though he already knew it wasn't true.

The old teapot chuckled, a sound like bubbles in a kettle. "They come to see *that.* The mess. The mingling. The great soft swirl of it. A cloud in a neat little row is just a cloud, dear one. But a thousand clouds tumbled together into a sunset — *that* is the most beautiful sight on the whole mountain." She nudged her crooked lid. "Some lovely things can't be kept in lines. And the wonderful part is — they don't *need* to be."

Almond Tofu watched a fat pink cloud go waltzing into a gold one, the two of them swirling round and round, perfectly imperfect.

He thought about all the pebbles he'd lined up. All the teacups he'd squared. All the worrying he'd done over edges and rows and corners that wouldn't stay corners. He thought about how he had spent the whole long day trying to teach a sunset to stand in a queue.

And something deep in his jiggly middle went soft — softer than usual, and that was saying something for a pudding who was already mostly soft to begin with.

"I think," he said slowly, "I've been trying to fold a sunset into a napkin."

"Mm," said Lǎo Hú, which this time meant *exactly so.*

---

So Almond Tofu put down his bamboo whisk. He rubbed away every chalk box. He let Cloud One and Cloud Two and Cloud Three forget their numbers entirely and become, simply, the *sky.*

Then he climbed up onto the warm porch railing beside the old teapot, wobbled himself comfy, and did the very last thing a tidy little worrier ever expects to do.

He let the mess be beautiful.

The clouds rolled and mingled and glowed. They drifted right up over the railing and curled around Almond Tofu like a soft, untidy blanket, and he didn't push a single one back into place. He just giggled — a small almond giggle — as a puff of rose-gold cloud bonked gently against his cheek and left a smudge of sunset there.

"That's all right," he told it. "You can stay wherever you like. I'm not the boss of you. Nobody is the boss of a cloud."

Lǎo Hú poured him a tiny cup of warm jasmine tea, slightly crooked, with the steam curling up to join the sky. And do you know — it was the most delicious thing Almond Tofu had ever tasted, and not one drop of it was tidy.

"You know," he said dreamily, "I don't think I'm a cloud sorter at all."

"No?" said Lǎo Hú.

"No." He snuggled deeper into the swirl. "I think I'm a cloud *watcher.* And I think that might be the very best job on the whole mountain."

The sun sank low. The cloud-sea turned the deep, soft purple of a sleepy plum. And high above Maokong, in a teahouse made of lantern-light, a little square of almond pudding finally let his corners go round — and found that round was lovely too.

## Goodnight Blessing

Sleep now, small one, and let your day go soft at the edges.
You needn't fold every worry into a tidy little square.
Let your thoughts mingle and swirl like sleepy 雲, glowing gold and rose,
and know that the loveliest things are not the neatest ones —
they are the warm, the gentle, the wonderfully blended.
Tuck the messes of the day beneath a cloud-soft blanket.
You are exactly enough, just as jiggly and just as you are.
Goodnight.
