# Story 32: Red Bean and the Winter Solstice

**Main Character:** Hóng Dòu (紅豆) — Red Bean
**Guest Characters:** Tāng Yuán (湯圓) — Tangyuan (a brief warm cameo)
**Setting:** A chilly Taipei night market on 冬至 (Winter Solstice), the longest, coldest night of the year
**Theme:** Warmth shared in the cold — food is love
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 冬至 (dōngzhì) = Winter Solstice

---

It was the coldest night of the entire year, and Hóng Dòu was so happy she could barely stand still.

This was a problem, because Hóng Dòu was a very small bean — a plump, deep-burgundy little thing with a neat white stripe down her middle, no taller than a thumb — and when a very small bean gets excited and starts bustling, she tends to knock over things much larger than herself. Which is exactly what happened, because the very first thing Hóng Dòu did on the night of **冬至** (dōngzhì) = Winter Solstice was drag her enormous copper soup pot into the middle of the **夜市** (yèshì) = night market and immediately trip over her own ladle.

*Clang.*

"I'm fine!" she announced to nobody, popping back up and dusting herself off. "Everyone needs a little sweetness, and tonight EVERYONE is getting some, even if I have to fall over a hundred times to do it."

She did not fall over a hundred times.

She fell over a hundred and three times. But who's counting? (You are. I can tell. Stop it.)

Now, you should understand what 冬至 means, little dreamer, because it is the whole reason Hóng Dòu had been awake since dawn. 冬至 is the longest, darkest, coldest night of the entire year — the night when the world tips over the very bottom of winter and starts, ever so slowly, leaning back toward the sun. In Taiwan, families gather on this night and eat warm, round **湯圓** (tāngyuán) = glutinous rice balls floating in sweet soup, because round things mean *together*, and warm things mean *love*, and nobody — NOBODY — should be cold and alone on the longest night of the year.

Hóng Dòu took this very, very seriously.

"Tonight," she said, stirring her great pot with a ladle nearly as big as she was, "nobody in this market eats alone. Nobody shivers. Nobody goes to bed with a cold belly." She tasted her soup, smacked her lips, and added a fat handful of rock sugar. "Not on MY 冬至."

The soup in her pot was **紅豆湯** (hóngdòu tāng) = red bean soup — thick and dark and ruby-red, sweet as a hug, steaming so generously that the steam fogged up the windows of every stall for three carts in either direction. It smelled like winter blankets. It smelled like grandmothers. It smelled, frankly, like the inside of a happy memory.

And it was working. Because the cold things were beginning to creep out of the dark.

The first to wander up was a shivering little dumpling vendor's cat, soaked from the drizzle, looking thoroughly sorry for itself.

"**來來來**!" (lái lái lái) said Hóng Dòu — "come come come!" — and ladled a tiny saucer of warm soup. The cat drank it, blinked, and promptly fell asleep against the warm side of the pot like a furry comma. "There," beamed Hóng Dòu. "One down."

Next came a delivery scooter driver, hunched and frozen, his nose glowing red as a stoplight. He hadn't even ordered anything — he was just driving past — but Hóng Dòu chased him down the lane, ladle in hand, shouting "**吃飽了嗎**?" (chī bǎo le ma) = "have you eaten yet?" until he gave up, parked, and accepted a bowl. He had three. He cried a little into the second one. He said it was the steam. (It was not the steam.)

"Two down!" said Hóng Dòu.

And here is the thing about Hóng Dòu, the thing you must know if you are ever cold and lonely in a Taipei night market on the longest night of the year: she will *find* you. You cannot hide from a grandmother with soup. It is one of the fundamental laws of the universe, right up there with gravity and the fact that the last dumpling always belongs to whoever wants it least.

She found the old man who sold lottery tickets and had no family to go home to. She found the two college students who'd missed the last bus and were sharing one thin scarf between them. She found a homesick exchange student from very far away who didn't know what 冬至 was, and didn't know why everyone seemed so happy, and was trying not to feel like the only cold person in a warm country.

Hóng Dòu marched right up to her with a steaming bowl.

"You're far from home," she said gently. It was not a question.

"How did you—"

"I'm a bean," said Hóng Dòu, as if that explained everything. (It did not. But she said it with such warmth that the student decided not to argue.) "Tonight is **溫暖** (wēnnuǎn) = warm. That's the whole point of it. The night is long so we have more time to be warm *together*." She pressed the bowl into the student's cold hands. "Eat. You're home now. This is home now. I've decided."

The student took one sip — sweet, hot, ruby-red — and felt something in her chest unfreeze.

"Three down," whispered Hóng Dòu. "And many to go."

By now the whole market had noticed. A crowd was gathering around the great copper pot, drawn in by the smell and the steam and the small burgundy bean who would not let a single soul stay cold. There was laughter. There was slurping. There were at least four people who had no intention of eating soup tonight and were now on their second bowl, wondering how this had happened to them.

There was, however, a tiny problem.

"It's wonderful soup," said the lottery-ticket man, scraping his bowl. "But it's missing something."

Hóng Dòu froze, ladle dripping. "Missing something?" she gasped, scandalized. "My 紅豆湯? Missing *something*?"

"On 冬至," the old man said gently, "you've got to have the round ones. You've got to have **湯圓**."

And Hóng Dòu's whole little face fell, because he was right. It was the longest night of the year, the night of *together*, the night of round things floating in sweet warm soup — and she had a glorious pot of red bean soup and not a single tangyuan to put in it.

"Oh no," she whispered. "Oh no oh no oh no. What kind of 冬至 has no 湯圓?"

"Did somebody say 湯圓?" boomed a voice.

The crowd turned.

And there, rolling jauntily up the lane through the cold and the drizzle, was the roundest, jolliest, most perfectly spherical fellow you have ever seen — a glutinous rice ball, soft and white and glowing, with rosy cheeks and a grin that took up half his face.

**Tāng Yuán** had arrived.

"Family!" he cried, beaming around at the whole shivering, slurping crowd as if he'd known every single one of them since birth. "We heard there was a soup situation. We came as fast as we could roll!" (Tāng Yuán always said *we*, even when he was, as far as anyone could tell, just one fellow. "We're never really just one," he'd explain. "We're a whole family in here." Nobody knew exactly what he meant, but it was comforting, so they let him have it.)

He took one look at the steaming copper pot of ruby-red 紅豆湯, and his rosy cheeks went rosier.

"Oh, Hóng Dòu," he said, getting a little misty. "Oh, this is *beautiful*. Red bean soup on 冬至! Do you know what this soup needs?"

"湯圓," said Hóng Dòu hopefully.

"湯圓," Tāng Yuán agreed, nodding so hard he nearly tipped over. "Round ones. Soft ones. Sweet ones. Ones that bob. We're all in this soup together, family — quite literally, in about thirty seconds!"

And then he started to laugh.

Now, you have to understand: when Tāng Yuán laughs, things happen. Specifically, the sweet black sesame paste inside him — warm and gooey and dark as midnight — comes oozing out the top of his head in a slow, happy little fountain. The harder he laughs, the more it oozes. And right now, surrounded by warmth and friends and the best red bean soup in all of Taipei, Tāng Yuán was laughing so hard that black sesame was practically *erupting* out of him like the world's most delicious volcano.

"Tāng Yuán!" giggled Hóng Dòu. "You're leaking!"

"I always leak when I'm HAPPY!" he howled, oozing magnificently. "It means the inside matches the outside! Now QUICK — before I laugh myself empty —"

And with one last joyful roll, Tāng Yuán went tumbling, spinning, *cannonballing* straight into the great copper pot.

*SPLOOSH.*

A wave of ruby-red soup splashed up and rained back down, warm and sweet, and when it settled, there he was — bobbing happily in the middle of all that red bean soup, perfectly round, perfectly content, his sweet black sesame swirling out into the soup in lovely dark ribbons.

"AH," sighed Tāng Yuán, floating on his back like a man in a warm bath. "*This* is 冬至. This is exactly right."

The crowd cheered. Hóng Dòu burst into the proudest tears a bean has ever cried. The exchange student laughed out loud for the first time all winter. The cat woke up, looked at the floating tangyuan, and decided this was the greatest night of its small wet life.

"NOW," announced Hóng Dòu, hoisting her enormous ladle high, steam billowing around her tiny burgundy body like a cape, "the soup is complete! 紅豆湯 *and* 湯圓! Sweet *and* round! Hot *and* together! On the longest, coldest night of the year — **nobody** goes home cold!"

She ladled and ladled and ladled. Bowls went out into a hundred cold hands. The market filled with the sound of slurping and laughing and that particular happy quiet that happens when a lot of people are warm at the same time. The longest night of the year stopped feeling long. It started feeling like the coziest night there had ever been.

And Tāng Yuán, bobbing gently in the warm red soup as the ladle came round and round, looked up at Hóng Dòu and the crowd of glowing, full, no-longer-cold faces, and he sighed the most contented sigh in the history of sighs.

"Eat up, family," he murmured, as a bowl rose toward a smiling old man's hands, sweet black sesame swirling, warm red bean cradling him close. "We were always meant for this. The best thing a 湯圓 can ever be... is *shared*."

And he was — happily, proudly, perfectly. Bowl after bowl, the warm 紅豆湯 and the round sweet 湯圓 found their way into grateful hands and grateful hearts, exactly as they were always destined to, on the night the whole world tips back toward the light.

By the time the pot ran low, every single person in that night market was warm. Every belly was full. Nobody — not the cat, not the scooter driver, not the lonely old man, not the student so very far from home — nobody was cold. Nobody was alone.

Hóng Dòu scraped the very last sweet ruby spoonful into her own tiny bowl, sat down on the edge of her warm copper pot, and looked out at the glowing, sleepy, soup-fed market with the satisfied smile of a grandmother whose whole family has finally eaten.

"There," she whispered to the longest night of the year. "Everyone got a little sweetness."

The steam rose. The market hummed. And slowly, ever so slowly, the longest night of the year turned its face back toward morning.

## Goodnight Blessing

Tonight, little dreamer, on whatever night you find yourself in — long or short, warm or cold — may there always be a small burgundy bean somewhere stirring a great pot of sweetness, just for you. May your belly be full and your blanket be warm and your heart be round as a 湯圓 bobbing in red bean soup. The night is long so that we have more time to be warm together, and you are not alone, and you are not far from home — because home is wherever someone has saved you a bowl. Eat up, little one. You're home now. Goodnight, and stay warm.
