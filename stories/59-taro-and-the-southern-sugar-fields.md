# Story 59: Taro & the Southern Sugar Fields

**Main Character:** Taro (芋頭) — chunky dusty-purple, wise & patient, reading glasses, speaks in proverbs
**Guest Characters:** Hēi Táng (Brown Sugar)
**Setting:** Sugarcane fields and a vintage sugar-railway in Tainan
**Theme:** Good things take time to steam — and to grow.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 南部 (nánbù) = the south

---

The little blue train coughed twice, hiccuped a puff of steam, and then refused to move at all.

"Oh dear," said Taro, peering over his reading glasses at the tiny rusty engine. It was the famous **五分車** (wǔ fēn chē) = the half-gauge sugar-railway, an old narrow train that once carried sugarcane across the **南部** (nánbù) = the south of the island. Now it carried two passengers: one calm purple lump, and one extremely impatient ribbon of syrup.

"WHY ARE WE STOPPED?" Hēi Táng demanded, sloshing dramatically across the wooden bench. He left a glistening tiger-stripe streak everywhere he flopped. "We've been on this train for THREE WHOLE MINUTES and we have not arrived ANYWHERE!"

Taro chewed slowly on a sunflower seed.

"Trains," he said, "are like taro. Both take time to cook through."

Hēi Táng oozed off the bench and onto the floor in a puddle of impatience. "I don't WANT to take time. I want to be sweet NOW. I want to be IMPORTANT NOW."

Taro smiled. This, he decided, was going to be a very good road trip.

The sugar-train belonged to a wrinkly old conductor-spirit named Old Whistle, who waved a green flag and apologized that the engine simply "got sleepy in the heat." So while it napped, Taro stepped down into the cane fields — and Hēi Táng dripped after him, grumbling.

The fields of **南部** stretched out forever, tall green sugarcane swaying in rows like a sea of soft swords. The sun was warm and honey-gold. Somewhere a frog said *guo-guo*. The air smelled of cut grass and faraway caramel.

"It's so SLOW here," Hēi Táng complained. "Up north everything is fast. Bubble tea shops! Neon signs! Whoosh, whoosh! But down here? The clouds are barely even bothering to move."

"Mm," said Taro. "That is the secret of the south. **慢慢來** (màn man lái) = take it slowly."

"I HATE slowly," said Hēi Táng. And to prove it, he tried to make himself sweeter, RIGHT THEN, by squeezing his middle as hard as he could. He turned a slightly darker amber and made a sad little *sploot* sound, and that was all.

Taro patted him gently with one chunky purple hand. "You cannot rush a syrup, kiddo. Brown sugar becomes sweet by being cooked low, and slow, and long. Same as me. I am a root. I grew underground in the dark for many quiet months before anyone ever saw me."

"That sounds BORING," said Hēi Táng.

"It was wonderful," said Taro. "Quiet is where the flavor hides."

They walked between the cane rows. Hēi Táng tried very hard to walk fast, but syrup does not walk fast — it sort of *advances*, like the world's most determined puddle. By the time they reached a little wooden sugar shed, he was exhausted and cross.

Inside the shed was an enormous black iron pot the size of a bathtub, and in the pot, slowly, *slooowly*, a river of cane juice was bubbling. *Blub. Blub. Bloop.* It was thin and pale and watery.

"Behold," said Taro. "A brown sugar baby."

Hēi Táng pressed right up to the rim. "THAT? That's not brown sugar! It's barely even tan! It's practically clear! It looks like dishwater!"

"Give it time," said Taro.

"How much time?"

"Hours and hours and hours."

"I'll be old by then! I'll have a beard!"

"You're a syrup," Taro pointed out. "You can't grow a beard."

"I'll grow one out of SPITE," said Hēi Táng.

The juice went *blub, blub, blub*. And here is the surprising thing, dear reader: it did not look like much was happening. But things were happening. Wonderful, invisible, patient things. The water was tiptoeing away as steam. The sugar inside was huddling closer and closer together, getting darker, getting richer, getting *deeper*.

Hēi Táng watched. And watched. And — to his enormous shock — got a little bit interested.

"It's... changing color," he admitted. "A teensy bit. Maybe. Like, a freckle of color."

"That's the south working its magic," said Taro. "Out here, nobody hurries the pot. They've been making sugar this way for a hundred years. **古早味** (gǔ zǎo wèi) = the old-time flavor. You can't buy it fast. You can only earn it slow."

Just then there came a tremendous *whoooo-WHEEEET!* from outside. The little blue train had woken up from its nap, and Old Whistle was waving his green flag and calling, "All aboard, all aboard, the engine's feeling chipper!"

"Wait — we can't go yet," said Hēi Táng. "The sugar isn't done!"

Taro raised one purple eyebrow, very slowly, the way only a root vegetable in reading glasses can. "Why, Hēi Táng," he said. "I do believe you want to *stay* and *wait.*"

"I do NOT," said Hēi Táng. Then, quietly: "...I just want to see how it turns out. That's different."

"That," said Taro warmly, "is not different at all. That is patience growing inside you. Like taro. In the dark. Quietly."

Hēi Táng made a *harrumph* noise, but he stayed put. He watched the pot. The afternoon stretched long and golden across the cane fields, and Old Whistle decided his engine could use "one more little snooze" anyway, so the train settled down on the tracks like a cat in a sunbeam.

The two friends sat by the great iron pot as the light turned from honey to amber to rose. And slowly — *màn man lái* — the juice in the pot grew thick. It grew dark. It grew that glorious, glossy, tiger-striped brown that Hēi Táng knew in his own swirly heart, because it was the color of *him*.

"It looks like me!" he gasped.

"It IS you," said Taro. "Or what you'll be, when you're all grown and sweet and sure. Now smell."

Hēi Táng leaned in. The steam curled up and wrapped around him — toasty, smoky, sweet-as-a-grandmother's-hug, with that little bittersweet edge that only comes from being cooked a long, long, loving time.

"Oh," said Hēi Táng, very softly. "Oh, that's... that's the best smell in the whole **南部**."

"In the whole island," Taro agreed. "And it took all afternoon. Things worth being don't happen with a *whoosh*. They happen with a *blub, blub, blub.*"

Hēi Táng was quiet for a long, long minute — which, for Hēi Táng, was a small miracle of its own. Then he dipped one swirly tip into the finished sugar and tasted what he was becoming.

It was rich. It was deep. It was patient and warm and *worth it.*

"Taro," he whispered, "I think I get it now. I don't have to be sweet RIGHT NOW. I just have to keep cooking. The sweet part comes if I don't run off."

"Now you sound like a proper southern syrup," said Taro, and he was so pleased that his glasses fogged up. "Remember: 心急吃不了熱豆腐 — a hasty heart can't eat hot tofu. Cool your hurry. Trust the slow."

That evening, when the engine finally felt "chipper for real this time," the two friends climbed back aboard the little blue train. Old Whistle gave them each a jar of the fresh-cooked brown sugar, still warm, glowing like a captured sunset.

And as the sugar-train rattled gently home through the dark cane fields, *clackety-blub, clackety-blub*, Hēi Táng did something he had never once done in his whole speedy life.

He sat still.

He watched the stars come out over the south, one patient pinprick at a time, and he didn't ask a single time when they would arrive. Because he had learned the sweetest lesson of all: the very best things — sugar, and taro, and stars, and friendship — all come to those who wait, and watch, and let the steam rise slow.

"Slurp first, worry later," murmured Taro, who had picked that one up from a friend up north.

"That's not your catchphrase," said Hēi Táng sleepily.

"No," Taro admitted. "But it's true everywhere. Even in the south."

And the little train rolled on, sweet and slow, all the way to morning.

## Goodnight Blessing
May your day, like sugar in the pot, be cooked low and warm and unhurried. May you learn that the dark and quiet times are simply where your sweetness is growing. There is no rush, little one — the stars will wait for you, and so will every good thing. Curl up now, slow as steam, and let the cane fields sway you to sleep. Goodnight.
