# Story 51: Aiyu & the Lantern Riddle Night

**Main Character:** Aiyu (Aiyu Jelly)
**Guest Characters:** Taro
**Setting:** A temple courtyard riddle-lantern festival at night
**Theme:** Quiet cleverness shines just as bright.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 燈謎 (dēngmí) = lantern riddle

---

On the night of the festival, the temple courtyard hung so heavy with lanterns that the sky forgot to show any stars — it figured the lanterns had it covered. Red ones bobbed like fat tomatoes. Gold ones glowed like cracked-open eggs. And dangling from every single one was a slip of paper with a **燈謎** (dēngmí) = lantern riddle scribbled on it, waiting to be solved.

Aiyu arrived at the very edge of all that glow, holding her own small light, and immediately did what she did best: she tried to become invisible.

This was easier for Aiyu than for most. She was a translucent golden-amber cube of aiyu jelly, wibbly and see-through as honey held up to a candle. When she stood still in front of a lantern, the light shone *right through her,* and you could see the riddle paper glowing on the other side of her tummy.

"Oh good," she whispered to herself. "Nobody will notice me at all."

A popcorn-chicken nugget bounced clean off her head. "Sorry! Didn't see ya!" it giggled, and rejoined its hopping crew.

Aiyu sighed a tiny, see-through sigh. Being invisible, it turned out, also meant being bumped into.

---

The festival was the loudest, brightest, most wonderful chaos. Snacks crowded the courtyard shoulder to shoulder, all of them shouting answers up at the lanterns. Whoever shouted the right answer to a riddle got to keep that lantern's light — and the more riddles you solved, the brighter your night glowed.

Shaved Ice had already collected nine lanterns and wore them stacked on top of his head like a wobbly crown. "I AM A GENIUS!" he announced to everyone, and also to a lamppost, just in case the lamppost was listening.

A scallion pancake named Cōng Cōng cartwheeled past, flippy and flappy. "Riddle me this!" he hollered, reading off a slip. "*What gets wetter the more it dries?* Easy — a TOWEL! Let's flip the script!" The lantern flared bright, and Cōng Cōng caught it on a crispy edge, beaming.

Aiyu watched, and her see-through heart did a small squeeze. Because here was Aiyu's secret, and it was this:

She *loved* riddles. She loved them the way some snacks love sugar. When she heard a tricky one, the answer would unfold in her quiet mind like a paper flower dropped in water — slow, sure, and beautiful. She almost always knew. She knew the towel one before Cōng Cōng did. She'd known three others before that.

The trouble was the *shouting.*

Aiyu could no more shout an answer across a crowded courtyard than she could turn herself purple. Every time she opened her mouth to call out, all those eyes might swing toward her, and her whole jiggly body would go cold and quivery, and the words would melt back down her throat like ice in warm tea.

So she stood at the edge, knowing all the answers, and saying none of them. Quiet as a held breath. Clear as a window nobody looks through.

---

"Hmph. There you are."

The voice was low, patient, and smelled faintly of old books and steamed sweetness. Aiyu turned to find Taro — chunky, dusty-purple, and dignified, his little reading glasses perched halfway down his nose so he could peer over them at her.

"You're hiding," said Taro. It was not a question.

"I'm not hiding," whispered Aiyu. "I'm... blending."

"Mm." Taro nodded slowly. "A clear pond hides many fish," he said, which was the sort of thing Taro said, "but the fish are still there, child. Just because nobody can see them does not mean they aren't swimming." He adjusted his glasses. "What was the towel answer, then?"

Aiyu blinked. "...A towel."

"And you knew it."

"...Before he did," she admitted, very softly.

Taro's whole purple face crinkled into a smile. "I thought so. I have been watching you mouth the answers all night, like a little fish opening and closing." He patted her wibbly shoulder with one chunky hand. "Come. Walk with me. You don't have to shout for anyone. Just walk."

So they walked together through the river of light, the patient purple square and the shy golden cube, and Aiyu found that walking beside Taro made the courtyard feel less like a crowd and more like a garden.

---

They came at last to the very center of the courtyard, where the biggest lantern in all of Taiwan hung waiting.

It was enormous — round and pale as the moon's shyer cousin, painted with cranes and clouds, and *completely dark.* It had hung dark for the whole festival. Because dangling from its bottom was the **燈謎** that nobody — not one single snack — had been able to solve all night. The Grand Riddle. Solve it, and the giant lantern would light, and the whole courtyard would glow gold.

A crowd had gathered, calling up guesses that all bounced off the dark lantern like pebbles off a roof.

The Earth God himself stood beneath it — Tǔdì Gōng, the jolly little white-bearded grandfather spirit, no taller than a teapot, in his tiny red temple-hat. He read the Grand Riddle aloud once more in his chuckly, slow voice so the latecomers could hear:

"*I have no throat, yet I can speak.
I have no eyes, yet others through me peek.
I am loudest when I am most clear.
What am I, my dears?*"

The courtyard erupted with guesses.

"A TRUMPET!" boomed Shaved Ice. (Wrong. Trumpets have no eyes to peek through, the Earth God noted gently.)

"A bell!" tried a pepper bun. (Wrong.)

"A SCALLION PANCAKE THAT IS ALSO VERY CONFIDENT!" Cōng Cōng suggested. (Bless him. Wrong.)

The guesses got wilder and the lantern stayed darker, and slowly the crowd's hopeful noise sagged into disappointed grumbling. It seemed the grandest lantern of the festival would stay dark after all.

But at the edge of the crowd, in front of the dark giant lantern, stood Aiyu — translucent, golden, see-through. And the riddle had unfolded in her quiet mind like a paper flower in water, almost the moment she'd heard it.

She *knew.*

She turned to Taro, her whole little body trembling like a struck jelly. "I know it," she breathed. "But I can't — Taro, there are so *many* of them, I can't shout it across all those—"

"Then don't shout," said Taro calmly. "You needn't fill the whole courtyard with your voice, child. You need only fill one small ear." He leaned his chunky purple head down close. "Whisper it to me. Just to me."

And so Aiyu — who could not shout to save her wibbly life — leaned up on her tiptoe corner and whispered the answer, soft as moonlight landing, right into Taro's ear.

Taro's eyebrows lifted clear off the top of his glasses. "*Oh,*" he said. "Oh, that's lovely. That's exactly it." He straightened, turned, and called out to the Earth God in his deep, carrying voice: "The answer comes from my friend here. She says—" and then he stopped. He looked back at her, and his eyes were kind. "No. *You* say it, little one. I'll stand right beside you. You only have to say it once, and only as loud as a whisper. The night will carry the rest."

Aiyu's see-through heart hammered. The whole courtyard had gone quiet — really, genuinely quiet — waiting. All those eyes. But Taro stood at her side, solid and purple and warm as a stone in the sun, and somehow that made the silence feel less like a cliff and more like a cupped pair of hands.

She looked up at the great dark lantern. And in a voice barely louder than steam curling off tea — honest, clear, and small — Aiyu said:

"...A window."

For a moment, nothing.

Then the Grand Lantern *bloomed.* Light poured up through it like sunrise inside a paper moon, and it glowed so gold and so bright that the whole courtyard turned the color of warm honey, and every snack's face shone, and Shaved Ice's nine lanterns looked like nightlights next to it.

"A WINDOW!" the Earth God cried, clapping his tiny hands, his white beard bouncing. "*No throat, yet it carries voices through. No eyes, yet everyone peeks through it. Loudest — clearest — when its glass is most clear!* Ho ho — and who solved it? The quietest, *clearest* one of all!"

The whole courtyard turned to look at Aiyu.

And here is the wonderful part. Aiyu — translucent, see-through Aiyu, the one who'd spent all night trying to disappear — stood in the light of the lantern she had lit. And because she was clear as glass, the great golden glow shone *right through her,* so that she lit up from the inside, glowing like a little lantern of her own.

She didn't disappear at all. She was the brightest thing in the courtyard.

"Bless this little corner of the island," chuckled the Earth God, pressing a lucky candy into her wibbly hand, "and bless the quiet ones in it. The loudest voice does not always carry the truest answer, my dear. Sometimes the truest answer arrives on a whisper."

Aiyu, glowing, looked up at Taro. "I didn't even shout," she whispered.

"No," Taro agreed, beaming. "You whispered. And the whole night heard you anyway." He nudged his glasses up his purple nose. "A still pond reflects the clearest moon, child. You needn't make waves to shine."

---

For the rest of the festival, Aiyu didn't try to be invisible even once. She drifted from lantern to lantern, and when someone got stuck on a tricky **燈謎**, she'd lean in close and whisper the answer to *them,* so they could call it out and keep the lantern's light. By the end of the night, half the snacks in the courtyard had won a lantern thanks to a quiet golden cube and her gentle little voice.

And high above, the Grand Lantern glowed on — lit by the softest answer of the night, shining through the clearest heart in all of Taiwan.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little dreamer, you do not have to be the loudest light in the room to be the warmest. Some of the brightest things in this world glow quietly, from the inside, where no one has to shout to see them. Tonight, when you close your eyes, let your thoughts settle clear and still as a pond holding the moon. The clever, kind, quiet ones are always heard in the end — and you are one of them. Sleep soft, shine gentle, and know you are seen. Goodnight.
