# Story 27: Three Tadpoles and the Glass-Bottom Gondola

**Main Characters:** Janie, Jayna, and Cat — three traveling tadpoles
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** Maokong Gondola and Taipei Zoo, Taipei
**Theme:** Big adventures in small bodies
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 貓空 (Māokōng) = Maokong, literally "cat sky"

---

Three tadpoles were sitting in a teacup, and the teacup was sitting on a bench, and the bench was sitting at the very front of a very long line at the **貓空纜車** (Māokōng lǎnchē) = Maokong Gondola station in Taipei.

The teacup was filled with water. The water was filled with tadpoles. And the tadpoles were filled with opinions.

"I cannot BELIEVE we're doing this," said Janie, wiggling her tail so hard the water sloshed. Janie was the biggest of the three, with a glossy dark head and bright black eyes, and she was the kind of tadpole who had already decided that today was going to be the best day of her life and was prepared to fight anyone who disagreed.

"I can absolutely believe we're doing this," said Jayna, who was slightly smaller and slightly more nervous and had two tiny back legs just starting to poke out, which she was VERY proud of. "I have, however, several questions. First: are we going to die? Second: how high is this gondola? Third: are tadpoles even ALLOWED on gondolas?"

"Jayna," said Cat, "you ask those three questions every single morning."

"And I have YET to receive satisfying answers."

Cat was the third tadpole, and Cat was named Cat because when she had hatched, her grandmother had looked at her wiggling around and said, "She moves like a cat chasing a string," and the name had stuck even though Cat was, definitively, not a cat. Cat was a tadpole. A small, round, dark-grey tadpole with an extremely calm expression and an extremely dry sense of humor.

"And THE most important question of all," Cat continued, "is whether the gondola has a glass bottom."

"It does!" Janie squealed. "It DOES! I checked! Three of the cars are see-through! You can look STRAIGHT DOWN at the mountains and the trees and the tea fields and the ZOO!"

"Hold on hold on hold on," said Jayna. "Glass bottom? GLASS? Between us and the SKY?"

"Between us and the GROUND, sweetie," said Cat. "The sky is above us. Glass is below us."

"That's WORSE."

"I dunno, I find it kinda calming," said Cat. "If the glass breaks, we're tadpoles. We just splash."

"CAT."

"What."

"That is not a calming statement."

The line moved forward. Their teacup slid along the bench. A very patient human grandmother was carrying them — a sweet old lady named Auntie Mei who had agreed to bring the three tadpoles on a Taiwan adventure as long as they promised to A) not jump out of the teacup, B) not get eaten by any of the zoo animals, and C) not start any arguments while she was trying to enjoy her bubble tea.

So far they were 1 for 3.

"**歡迎光臨**!" (huānyíng guānglín) said the gondola attendant — "welcome!" — and Auntie Mei held up her ticket, and the attendant glanced into the teacup and blinked.

"Ma'am," said the attendant, "are those tadpoles?"

"Yes," said Auntie Mei.

"...are they going on the gondola?"

"Yes."

"...okay."

The attendant did not get paid enough to ask follow-up questions.

They were directed to a car. And not just any car. A **GLASS-BOTTOM CAR**. The floor was a thick, shiny window, and beneath it was nothing but empty air and very far-away mountains. Auntie Mei stepped in. The teacup was placed gently on the seat beside her. The doors slid shut with a soft *whoosh*.

And then the gondola lifted off.

"OH MY GOD," shrieked Jayna. "OH MY GOD WE'RE FLYING."

"WE'RE FLYING!" cheered Janie.

"We are technically dangling," said Cat. "Flying implies engines."

"CAT WE ARE GOING TO DIE."

"Probably not, statistically."

The gondola climbed. The station fell away beneath them. The city of Taipei stretched out like a tablecloth made of buildings and roads and tiny tiny cars, and the three tadpoles pressed their faces against the side of their teacup and stared.

Then Janie remembered the floor.

"GUYS," she said. "GUYS LOOK DOWN."

They looked down.

Through the bottom of the teacup, through the glass floor of the gondola, through a thousand feet of empty mountain air, they could see —

The whole world.

Green hillsides covered in tea bushes growing in tidy curling rows. Tiny temples with red roofs tucked between trees. A river snaking through the valley. Hawks gliding on currents BELOW them, which is a deeply strange thing to see — birds flying UNDERNEATH you, like you were the sky.

"It's so..." Jayna whispered. "It's so..."

"BIG," said Janie.

"Beautiful," said Cat.

Jayna's tiny back legs were trembling. But not, she realized, from fear. From something else. From the feeling of being a very small tadpole in a very big world and somehow getting to SEE the bigness instead of just hearing about it.

"I'm not scared anymore," she said.

"GOOD," said Janie. "Because LOOK!"

She pointed her little nose. Below them, getting closer, was a sprawling green campus with curving paths, lakes, and enclosures the size of small parks.

The **台北市立動物園** (Táiběi Shìlì Dòngwùyuán) = Taipei Zoo.

"Oh," breathed Cat. "Look at all of them."

From above, through the glass, they could see —

Elephants. Actual elephants. Splashing in a pond. Pandas munching bamboo in a cool grotto. A herd of giraffes walking in slow motion across a savannah enclosure, their necks like moving towers. A pygmy hippo wallowing happily in mud. Penguins zipping around an underwater tunnel. And — most exciting of all — a tiger pacing along the edge of his big leafy yard, his orange fur glowing in the sunlight.

"That's a TIGER," whispered Jayna reverently.

"That's a BIG tiger," said Cat.

"That tiger would eat us in one slurp," said Janie, who was absolutely delighted by this fact.

The gondola glided onward. The zoo passed beneath them like a living storybook. And then the mountains rose up around them, terraced with rolling tea plantations, and the cable car climbed and climbed and climbed —

— until at last it reached the very top of the mountain. **貓空** (Māokōng) station. Cat-Sky.

"WE'RE ALSO IN THE PLACE NAMED AFTER ME," Cat noted, sounding genuinely moved for the first time in her young tadpole life.

Auntie Mei carried them out into the sunshine. Maokong was a quiet mountain village of tea houses and dessert shops, with a view that stretched all the way back to the gleaming silver finger of Taipei 101 in the distance.

And at the most famous tea house at the very top of the mountain, Auntie Mei sat down at an outdoor table, lifted the teacup gently, and said, "All right, my little ones. I think you've earned this."

A waiter brought out three tiny bowls.

**冰淇淋** (bīngqílín) = ice cream.

Three flavors. Mango. Taro. And tea — green tea ice cream made from the very leaves growing on the mountain beneath them.

"Tadpoles can't eat ice cream," whispered Jayna nervously.

"This is a magical Taiwan story," said Cat. "We can eat whatever we want."

"**好吃**!" (hǎochī) Janie shouted — "delicious!" — and dove face-first into the mango scoop.

They feasted. They wriggled with joy. Janie covered her whole head in mango. Jayna took tiny, careful nibbles of taro and made a face that was equal parts shock and bliss. Cat licked at the green tea ice cream slowly, savoring every cold sweet bite, and announced that this was, in fact, the best moment of her entire life and she would be willing to fight anyone who said otherwise.

Auntie Mei sipped her oolong tea and watched the three tadpoles eat ice cream on a mountain with the entire city of Taipei spread out beneath them like a quilt.

"Look at them," she murmured to nobody in particular. "Three little nothings. And they came all this way."

The wind moved through the tea leaves. The afternoon light turned gold. Somewhere far below, the gondolas kept gliding silently up and down the mountain, carrying other travelers to and from the sky.

After ice cream, they took the gondola back down. Even Jayna, who had been so scared on the way up, pressed her nose against the glass floor and watched the world rush by beneath her. The tiger was still pacing. The giraffes were still walking. The hawks were still flying below.

"You know what I learned today?" said Janie sleepily, as the gondola swayed gently through the late afternoon.

"What?" said Cat.

"Being small doesn't mean you can't go big."

"That was almost wise," said Cat.

"I have my moments."

Jayna's tiny back legs gave a happy twitch. "I'm not scared of glass floors anymore."

"You will be tomorrow."

"Yeah. But for today, I'm not."

The gondola descended through the slanting golden light. The teacup rocked gently. The three tadpoles, full of ice cream and sky and tiger sightings, curled into a tiny tadpole pile at the bottom of their cup and drifted into the kind of nap that only happens when you've had a truly excellent day.

Auntie Mei looked into the teacup and smiled.

"Sweet dreams, little adventurers," she whispered. "**台灣很大** (Táiwān hěn dà) = Taiwan is big. But you saw a lot of it today."

And here is the thing about three tadpoles in a teacup, riding a glass-bottom gondola over the rooftop of Taipei:

They didn't NEED to be big. They didn't NEED to be fierce. They didn't need to be tigers or elephants or giraffes or even fully-grown frogs.

They just needed to show up. To wiggle a little. To press their noses against the glass and look down at the world.

The world, it turns out, will show itself to anyone brave enough to look.

Even tadpoles.

ESPECIALLY tadpoles.

## Goodnight Blessing

You don't have to be big to have a big adventure. You don't have to be fierce to see the whole world. You just have to wiggle a little, and look down, and let the sky carry you. Goodnight, brave tadpole. Goodnight.
