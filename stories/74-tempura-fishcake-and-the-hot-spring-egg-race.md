# Story 74: Tempura Fishcake & the Hot-Spring Egg Race

**Main Character:** Tián Bù Lǎ (Taiwanese Tianbula / fishcake) — a chewy, ridged, golden fried fishcake who is patient and methodical
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A volcanic hot-spring village in Beitou where eggs cook in the steaming pools
**Theme:** Doing one careful thing well beats doing ten things in a hurry
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 溫泉 (wēnquán) = hot spring

---

High up in the misty hills of Beitou, where the mountains burped warm clouds all night long, there lived a fishcake named Tián Bù Lǎ — and on the night our story begins, every single snack in the village was about to lose their minds over an egg.

Now, Tián Bù Lǎ (let's call him Tian for short, because his full name takes about as long to say as a yawn) was golden and chewy and ridged all over, like a little fried wave that had decided to take a nap and never woke up. He bobbed gently in his bamboo basket, smelling of toasted fish and the sea. And he did everything — *everything* — slowly.

Which is why, when the village bell went *BONG*, Tian was the only one who didn't immediately fall over.

"EGG TIME!" shrieked a Pineapple Cake, leaping out of bed.

"EGG TIME!" screamed a Mochi, bouncing off the ceiling.

And just like that, the great Hot-Spring Egg Race began.

---

You see, Beitou had a secret, and the secret was its **溫泉** (wēnquán) = hot springs. Steaming pools of milky, mineral-blue water bubbled right out of the mountain, hot enough to cook an egg without a single flame. Cook an egg gently in a wēnquán and you got the most wonderful thing in the whole world: a **溫泉蛋** (wēnquán dàn) = hot-spring egg. Silky white. A yolk like soft gold custard. So jiggly it wobbled when you so much as *looked* at it.

The whole village had been talking about it for a week. And tonight — finally — the springs were warm enough.

So the snacks ran.

They ran in their pajamas. They ran carrying baskets and buckets and one extremely confused Taro who had grabbed a hairbrush instead of an egg. They thundered down the wooden steps to the steaming pools, elbowing and giggling and shouting, "*First!* I'm gonna be *first!*"

Tian watched them go. Then he picked up his own egg — one single egg, cool and smooth and white — and began to walk down the hill. One. Step. At. A. Time.

"You're gonna miss it, Tian!" called the Mochi, already cannonballing toward the water.

"I don't think so," said Tian, in his slow, warm, gravelly voice. "An egg can only cook so fast. The mountain isn't in a hurry. Why should I be?"

The Mochi did not hear this, because the Mochi was already underwater.

Now, you might be wondering: why was everyone in such a hurry? It wasn't even about being *hungry*. It was about being *first*. Somewhere along the way, the whole village had gotten it into their heads that the very first snack to eat a hot-spring egg would be — well, nobody was quite sure. The Coolest? The Luckiest? The Most Egg-cellent? The Pineapple Cake swore she'd heard that the first egg of the season granted a wish, though when you pressed her on it, she admitted she'd heard it from a Steamed Bun who was famous for making things up.

But it didn't matter if it was true. Once a crowd decides to race, the race becomes the only thing that's real. And so down the hill they tumbled, faster and faster, while Tian came along behind them at the speed of a snail who had stopped to admire the moon.

---

At the pools, it was chaos.

Snacks were flinging eggs into the steaming wēnquán like they were skipping stones. *Plop! Plop-plop! Sploosh!* Eggs everywhere. Nobody was counting. Nobody was timing. Everybody was just *waiting to be first*, hopping from foot to foot, going, "Is it ready? Is it ready? IS IT READY?"

About nine seconds later, the Pineapple Cake could not stand it.

"MINE'S DONE!" she announced, and yanked her egg out, cracked it open, and—

*Splrrrt.*

It poured out completely raw, like a cold and disappointed handshake, all over her feet.

"Bleurgh!" she yelped. "It's all *runny!*"

"That means it's not ready, genius," said the Taro, who then yanked *his* egg out — and got an even runnier one, which somehow ended up in his hair, right next to the hairbrush he still did not know why he'd brought.

One by one, the whole crowd pulled their eggs out too soon. And one by one they got the same gloppy, slimy, sloshy mess. The pool's edge grew slick with spilled egg. A Tapioca Pearl slipped in it, skidded fifteen feet, and announced from flat on his back, "I regret everything."

But here is the funny part — and it really is the funny part. Instead of waiting *longer*, the crowd decided the problem was that they hadn't gone *faster*. So they did it all again. They flung *fresh* eggs into the springs, danced around even more impatiently, and yanked them out even *sooner*, certain that this time the egg simply hadn't understood how important the race was.

It went, as you can imagine, extremely badly.

The second batch was somehow runnier than the first. The Mochi got an egg so raw it tried to crawl back into its shell. The Steamed Bun cracked three eggs in a row and wore all three of them, until he looked less like a snack and more like a very surprised omelette. The Taro, in a fit of frustration, attempted to cook *two* eggs at once by holding one in each hand, sloshing them around, and shouting "GO GO GO" — which produced precisely nothing except a Taro covered in twice as much egg as before.

"The spring is broken!" wailed the Mochi.

"The spring is *not* broken," called Tian, finally arriving at the bottom of the hill, not even a little out of breath.

---

The crowd turned to look at him.

"Then why are all our eggs WRONG?" demanded the Pineapple Cake, dripping.

"Because," said Tian gently, "you all did ten things in a hurry. Watch me do one thing slowly."

And here is the thing about a fishcake: when a fishcake decides to do something carefully, it is a beautiful sight to behold.

Tian set down his single egg. He found the warmest, calmest corner of the wēnquán — not the bubbling middle where everyone was splashing, but the quiet edge where the water just barely shivered. He tested it with one ridged golden finger. "Mm," he said. "Hot, but not angry. **剛剛好** (gāng gāng hǎo) = just right."

Then, instead of flinging his egg, he *lowered* it. Slowly. On a little string, the way you'd lower a sleepy baby into a cradle. The egg sank into the milky water without so much as a splash.

"Now," said Tian, "we wait."

"For how long?!" the snacks cried.

"Long enough to sing one song," said Tian. "A slow one."

So — because they had nothing better to do, and because their feet were covered in raw egg — they sang. A slow, sleepy, silly song about a mountain that snored. The Tapioca Pearl, still flat on his back, hummed along. The steam curled up around them all like a warm gray blanket.

And the whole time, Tian just watched his one egg. Calm. Patient. Methodical. He even closed his eyes and *listened*, the way you might listen to soup.

"You're not even *looking* at it," whispered the Mochi.

"I don't need to grab it," Tian whispered back. "It's not going anywhere. Neither am I. That's rather the point."

---

The song ended.

Tian opened his eyes. He lifted his egg out of the wēnquán on its little string — gently, gently — and let it cool for exactly three breaths. (He counted them. Out loud. The crowd counted with him. It was, honestly, the most relaxed anybody in Beitou had been all week.)

Then he cracked it open.

The whole village leaned in.

The white slid out soft and silky and perfectly set. And the yolk — oh, the yolk — wobbled out like a tiny golden sunrise, jiggling, glowing, holding its shape but only *just*, the way a good hot-spring egg always does. It quivered when Tian breathed near it.

"That," said the Pineapple Cake, in a hushed voice, "is the most beautiful egg I have ever seen."

"It's the *same egg* you all had," said Tian, with a slow smile. "The spring gave you the exact same thing. You just kept snatching yours out before the mountain finished its work."

The Taro looked at the hairbrush in his hand. The hairbrush looked back. Neither of them had any idea what to say.

---

So they tried again. But this time, Tian's way.

It was hard, at first. Slowing down always is, when your feet have spent all night learning to run. The Mochi lowered her egg and then immediately reached to grab it back. "Wait," said Tian softly, and she froze, fingers hovering over the steam. "It's still cooking. The mountain is still working. Trust it."

"But what if I do it wrong?" the Mochi whispered.

"You're not doing anything," said Tian, with a slow chuckle. "That's the secret. The doing is the *not* doing. The mountain does the cooking. You just have to be brave enough to leave it alone."

One by one — *one at a time* — each snack lowered a single egg into the quiet warm corner. No flinging. No racing. No "is it ready, is it ready." Tian taught them his song, and they sang it, and they waited, and they counted three breaths, and they did not grab. And the strangest, loveliest thing happened: the waiting, which they'd been so afraid of, turned out to be the *best part*. The steam. The song. The hush. The little shiver of excitement before the crack. They'd been racing so hard to skip it that they'd nearly missed the nicest minute of the whole night.

And when they cracked their eggs open — *every single one* came out perfect. Silky. Jiggly. Golden.

"We had it the whole time!" gasped the Mochi, staring at her wobbling yolk like it was made of treasure. "We just kept running *past* it!"

"That happens a lot," said Tian kindly. "Running past the good part. The good part doesn't run. It waits for you to slow down enough to find it."

They piled all the perfect eggs into one big bamboo basket and shared them, sitting in a circle in the steam, passing them around so everyone got to taste everyone else's. Nobody was first. Nobody cared anymore. The Tapioca Pearl declared that being last, on a full stomach, in a cloud of warm spring steam, was actually the best place to be.

And as for Tian? He ate his one perfect egg slowly, savoring every silky bite, ridges glistening gold in the lantern light — the most patient fishcake in all of Beitou, who had won the race by not running it at all.

---

## Goodnight Blessing

May your night be like a hot-spring egg, little one — soft, and golden, and worth the wait. Don't rush past the warm parts. Let the gentle mountain of sleep do its quiet work, one slow breath at a time. There's no race to win here. There's only this: the steam, the hush, and you, settling down just right. **剛剛好.**

Goodnight.
