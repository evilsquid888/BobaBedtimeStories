# Story 52: Turnip Cake & the Lucky-Word Banner

**Main Character:** Cài Tóu Guǒ (Turnip/Radish Cake) — a humble, steamy, square savory cake, shy with words
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A family kitchen and front doorway during Lunar New Year's Eve preparations
**Theme:** A wish written for your home is a wish for everyone in it
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 春聯 (chūnlián) = Spring Festival couplet banners

---

On the very last evening of the old year, a small square fellow named Cài Tóu Guǒ sat on the warmest spot of the kitchen counter, steaming gently and trying very hard to look invisible.

He was a turnip cake — soft and pale gray, speckled all over with little flecks of dried shrimp and bits of toasty fried shallot, with four flat sides so neat you could have used him as a coaster. When he got nervous, which was often, he steamed harder, and right now a tiny cloud was rising off his head like a thought he couldn't quite finish.

"Cài Tóu Guǒ!" called Grandmother Lotus Seed from across the kitchen, waving a fat red brush dripping with ink. "We've decided. *You* will write this year's banner for our front door."

The little cake nearly slid off the counter.

"*Me?*" he squeaked, releasing such a burst of steam that he briefly vanished. "But — but I can barely write my own name without smudging it!"

---

To understand why this was a catastrophe of the highest order, you must understand the front door.

Every Lunar New Year, families hang **春聯** (chūnlián) = Spring Festival couplet banners beside their doorways: long strips of bright red paper brushed with golden wishes for the year ahead. They are the very first thing a guest sees. They are, Cài Tóu Guǒ believed with his whole steamy heart, a kind of promise to the entire street — and if the words came out crooked, then surely the whole year would come out crooked too.

"The banner sets the tone for everything," he whispered, horrified. "What if my brushstrokes wobble? What if I jinx us? What if a single bad squiggle and — and the dumplings burn, and the oranges go sour, and Uncle's socks never match again for *twelve whole months?*"

"His socks already never match," said Grandmother Lotus Seed cheerfully. "Now hush and pick up the brush."

She set before him a long sheet of red paper, a saucer of gold ink that shimmered like a tiny lake of sunlight, and the brush, which was nearly as tall as he was.

"And don't look so glum," she added. "Last year your cousin the Scallion Pancake wrote our banner, and he was so flaky he kept shedding little crispy bits onto the ink. We hung up a wish for prosperity that was *also* lightly seasoned. The street talked about it for a month."

This did not help. Cài Tóu Guǒ only steamed harder, because being talked about for a month was precisely the thing he feared most in all the world.

He gulped. He wrapped his stubby arms around the brush, lifted it high, took aim at the empty red paper —

— and froze.

The paper looked back at him, enormous and blank and patient. The whole kitchen seemed to lean in. The radish in the soup pot stopped bubbling. Even the steam coming off his own head paused politely, waiting to see what he would do.

He did a blot.

It was not a beautiful blot. It was a fat, round, golden blot, right in the center, like a sneeze made of treasure. The first character was supposed to be **福** (fú) = good fortune, a graceful thing of crisp lines and balance. What he had made looked like a startled potato.

"Aiyo," he moaned, and crumpled the sheet.

---

He tried again.

The second banner came out so lopsided that the bottom characters slid into a pile in the corner, like passengers who'd all stood on the same side of a boat.

The third one he gripped the brush too hard, and a great teardrop of gold went *splat* and slowly oozed downward, turning his elegant wish for **平安** (píng'ān) = peace and safety into something that looked like it was melting in fear.

The fourth, the fifth, the sixth. Crumple, crumple, crumple. A little mountain of scrunched red paper grew beside him, and the more he failed, the harder he steamed, until the whole corner of the kitchen was as foggy as a hot spring at dawn.

By the seventh banner, Cài Tóu Guǒ could no longer even *see* the paper through his own steam. He brushed three confident strokes, blew the fog away, and discovered he had been writing enthusiastically on the cutting board instead. A nearby radish, who had wandered over to watch, now had a glittering gold mustache and seemed quietly delighted about it.

"This is a disaster," Cài Tóu Guǒ whispered. "I've vandalized a vegetable."

"I can't do it," he said at last, very small. "Pick someone with steadier hands. Pick the Mochi twins — they're stretchy and smooth. Pick anyone who isn't *me.*"

And he sat down right in the middle of his crumpled-paper mountain and let his steam sink to a sad, low simmer.

---

That was when Grandmother Lotus Seed sat down beside him — slowly, the way wise old beans do — and picked up one of the crumpled banners. She smoothed it flat across her knee.

"Tell me," she said. "What does this character say?"

"It's *supposed* to say good fortune," he mumbled. "But it looks like a potato that's seen a ghost."

"Mm." She turned it this way and that. "And *why* did you want to write good fortune?"

Cài Tóu Guǒ blinked. No one had asked him that. He'd been so busy worrying about the *shape* of the wish that he'd never once thought about the *wish* itself.

"Well," he said slowly, "because... I want this house to be lucky. I want Grandfather's cough to go away. I want the little Tāng Yuán to grow up round and giggly. I want Uncle to find at least *one* matching pair of socks." He paused, and his steam softened. "I want the kettle to always be warm when somebody comes in cold. I want this to be the house where nobody is ever lonely. I want everyone who lives here to feel safe, even on the dark nights when the wind rattles the shutters."

He stopped, surprised at himself. He hadn't known there were so many wishes folded up inside one small square cake.

Grandmother Lotus Seed smiled the smile of someone who has been waiting all evening for exactly those words.

"Then you already know how to write it," she said. "Listen, little cake. A chūnlián is not a *test.* No examiner stands at the door with a red pen. A couplet is a *wish* — a hope you hold for the people inside, written big enough for the whole street to share. The brush does not care if your lines are crooked. It only cares if your heart is in them."

"But the neighbors will *see,*" he whispered.

"Yes," she agreed warmly. "They'll see what you hope for the ones you love. And do you know what a person thinks, when they walk past a door and read a wish written like *that?* They think: *I hope so too. I hope it for my house. I hope it for theirs.*" She patted his warm little side. "A wish you write for your home is a wish for everyone in it — and a wish hung by the door becomes a wish for everyone who passes by. That is the whole secret. There never was anything to get right except the loving."

Cài Tóu Guǒ steamed — but this time it was a soft, happy steam, the kind that smells like a warm kitchen on a cold night.

"Could I... write whatever I truly mean?" he asked. "Even if it isn't the fancy old phrases?"

"*Especially* if it isn't," said Grandmother Lotus Seed.

---

So Cài Tóu Guǒ picked up the brush one more time.

He did not try to make the lines perfect. He didn't even try to make them straight. He just thought, very hard, about every soul who slept under this roof — and he let the brush say it.

On the right-hand banner he wrote, in big wobbly heartfelt strokes:
*May everyone here wake up warm.*

On the left-hand banner, a little crooked, a little smudged, glowing gold:
*May every cough fade and every laugh stay.*

And across the top, in the place of honor, he painted a single character. It still looked a *tiny* bit like a startled potato. But underneath it he had thought so fiercely about good fortune for the people he loved that the potato seemed, somehow, to be smiling.

"There," he breathed. "It's not pretty. But it's *true.*"

"True," said Grandmother Lotus Seed, "is the prettiest thing there is."

---

They carried the banners to the front door together and pressed them up on either side, still tacky with gold ink, while the cold New Year wind nibbled at their edges and the lanterns of the whole street glowed like a river of little moons.

And then a wonderful thing happened.

A snail family passed by on their evening stroll and stopped. They read the wobbly words. "*May everyone here wake up warm,*" the smallest snail whispered, and his mother squeezed him close. "Oh — I hope that for *our* shell too."

The plum-blossom vendor read it and got something in her eye that was definitely not a tear, certainly not, she had simply been *standing near an onion,* she insisted, even though there were no onions for several streets. A pair of stray dumplings, far from any home of their own, read *may every laugh stay* and laughed, right there on the street, for the first time all winter — a big wobbly soup-filled laugh that splashed a little and made everyone else laugh too.

An old, very serious Tea Egg came stomping by, the kind who collected complaints the way some people collect stamps, fully intending to point out that the top character looked a *great deal* like a startled potato. He opened his mouth. He read the words underneath. He closed his mouth again. "...Mine wakes up cold," he admitted gruffly to no one, and shuffled home looking thoughtful, which for a Tea Egg is practically singing.

Soon a whole little crowd had gathered at the door — not because the calligraphy was fine, but because it wasn't. Because it was honest. Because it sounded like exactly what they each, secretly, hoped for the ones *they* loved.

"Whose door is *this?*" someone asked. "It's the warmest banner on the whole street."

Inside, in the foggy happy kitchen, a small square turnip cake heard them — and steamed so proudly and so gently that, for just a moment, the entire doorway smelled of toasty shallots and good fortune and home.

The new year arrived right then, on a wave of firecrackers and laughter, and it found the whole household awake and warm and gathered close, exactly as the banner had wished.

And not one single sock, the whole year through, was ever lonely again. Well — *almost* none. But that, dear reader, is a story for another night.

## Goodnight Blessing

May your wishes wobble, little one, and may they be true.
May the words you write for your own warm home
wrap themselves around everyone you love,
soft as steam, gold as lantern light.
You do not have to do it perfectly.
You only have to mean it.
Sleep now, snug as a turnip cake on the warmest spot of the counter.
Goodnight.
