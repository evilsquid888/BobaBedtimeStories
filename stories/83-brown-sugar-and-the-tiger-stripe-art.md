# Story 83: Brown Sugar & the Tiger-Stripe Masterpiece

**Main Character:** Hēi Táng (Brown Sugar)
**Guest Characters:** Boba
**Setting:** A bubble-tea shop where syrup is swirled into cup-art
**Theme:** Make your mark sweetly, and let others shine too.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 黑糖 (hēi táng) = brown sugar

---

The Sweet Swirl Tea Shop had a window full of clear glass cups, and on most nights the whole shop smelled like a warm hug wearing a caramel coat. That smell was **黑糖** (hēi táng) = brown sugar, and on this particular evening the brown sugar in question would not stop talking about himself.

"Behold!" cried Hēi Táng, rearing up off the counter in a glossy dark-amber ribbon, dripping in slow tiger-stripe swirls. "Tonight I shall paint the most DRAMATIC cup the island has ever seen!" He flung a curl of syrup into the air and caught it on his own tail like a circus performer. It made a little *plip*. He bowed to the empty chairs as though they had applauded.

A small black-brown sphere bounced up onto the counter beside him with a cheerful *boing*. It was Boba, glossy and round and endlessly curious.

"Hey, Hēi Táng," said Boba. "Want to make some cups together?"

"Together?" Hēi Táng gasped, pressing one syrupy ribbon to where his heart would be if syrup had hearts, which it sort of does. "My dear bouncy friend, art like mine cannot be shared. It must be witnessed."

Boba just grinned and bounced. "Okay. Let's bounce!"

Now, the Sweet Swirl Tea Shop ran a little contest every night. Diàn Diàn, the round mochi clerk in the tiny convenience-store vest, would hold up the prettiest finished cup in the glowing front window for all the night market to admire. Whoever made it got a gold sticker shaped like a star.

Hēi Táng wanted that sticker more than a moth wants a lamp.

"Watch and learn, kiddo," Hēi Táng said, even though Boba was probably the same age as him. He slithered up the inside of a fresh, tall glass and began to paint.

And oh, he painted. He swirled himself in great looping stripes up one side of the glass. He drizzled a flourish across the other. He drew a sunburst, then a spiral, then what might have been a dragon or possibly a very excited noodle. He added stripes on top of stripes on top of stripes.

"More drama!" he announced, adding more.

"Um," said Boba, peering at the glass. "Where does the tea go?"

Hēi Táng froze mid-swirl. He looked at the cup. He had filled every single inch of it with brown sugar. There was no room left for milk, no room for ice, and absolutely no room for one single bouncy pearl. It was just a glass packed solid with syrup, dark as a midnight puddle.

"It is a *bold artistic choice*," Hēi Táng declared.

Diàn Diàn rolled over, beeped politely, took one sip, and turned a delicate shade of green. "Beep — that is... *a lot* of sweet," the little clerk wheezed. "Open all night, but maybe not for this one." He set it gently to the side.

Hēi Táng's swirls drooped.

"Try again!" Boba said kindly, already rolling a fresh glass into place.

So Hēi Táng tried again. This time he made a cup so wild with crisscrossing stripes that it looked like a tiger who had fallen into a tornado. It was undeniably dramatic. It was also undeniably impossible to drink, because the syrup was so thick it had glued itself into a solid amber brick. When Diàn Diàn tipped the glass, nothing moved. Not a drop. It just sat there, looking smug and sticky.

"Beep," said Diàn Diàn, gently turning the cup upside down to prove his point. Still nothing fell out. "Sturdy. Very sturdy."

By the third cup, the counter was a sticky disaster, Hēi Táng was puffing little theatrical sighs, and the gold star sticker was looking further away than the actual moon.

"I don't understand," Hēi Táng moaned, pooling himself into a sad puddle. "I am the sweetest! I am the swirliest! Why won't the contest love me?"

That was when Boba bounced into the very middle of the empty glass, sat down at the bottom with a soft *clink*, and looked up at Hēi Táng with shiny, hopeful eyes.

"Maybe," Boba said, "the cup isn't supposed to be all you."

Hēi Táng blinked his syrupy blink. "Beg pardon?"

"Watch," said Boba. "But this time — make me look good instead of you."

Hēi Táng did not understand this at all. Make *Boba* look good? But the night was getting late, and his other ideas had all turned into sticky bricks, so he sighed his most patient theatrical sigh and gave it a try.

Very, very gently, he dripped a single thin ribbon of himself down the inside of the glass. Just one. It slid down slow and glossy and left behind a streak of warm golden-amber, like a comet's tail painted on glass.

"Ooh," breathed Boba from the bottom. "Do another."

Hēi Táng made another stripe beside it. Then another. Not loops, not sunbursts, not dragon-noodles — just soft, slow tiger stripes running down the sides of the glass, with plenty of *space* between them. Empty space. He had never left empty space in his life. It felt wonderfully strange, like leaving room at a dinner table for a guest who hadn't arrived yet.

Then Diàn Diàn poured in the cold milk tea — and something magical happened.

The pale tea filled the glass and made the brown-sugar stripes *glow*. Where there had been only syrup before, now the amber swirls framed the creamy tea like sunset clouds around a moon. And down at the bottom, the little black pearls of Boba sat gathered together, dark and shiny and perfectly happy.

"Now stir us soft," said Boba.

Hēi Táng gave the gentlest swirl. The brown-sugar stripes spun lazily through the tea, marbling it in slow golden curls — there and then fading, there and then fading, never hogging, just *dancing*. Through it all, the pearls bounced up and twirled, catching the light. The whole cup looked like a tiny galaxy. Tiger stripes for the sky. Boba for the stars.

Diàn Diàn rolled over, took one careful sip, and his little face lit up so bright that the front window seemed dim by comparison.

"Beep," he whispered. "Oh. *Beep.* That's the one."

He lifted the cup high into the glowing window, and the whole night market crowded close to look. Sweet Potato pointed. Lychee said "*darling*, it's divine." Shaved Ice took off his sunglasses, which he NEVER does. Even Taro adjusted his reading glasses and murmured, "A swirl that lifts another is sweeter than a swirl alone."

The gold star sticker was pressed right onto the glass.

Hēi Táng stared. He had made hundreds of dramatic swirls in his life, but he had never, ever made a cup that made the whole market go quiet and then *aww* all at once.

"It worked," he said softly. "But — the stripes are smaller. There's so much *room* now. So much tea. So much... Boba."

"Mm-hm," said Boba, twirling happily in the golden swirls. "And everybody's looking at *all* of it. Including you."

Hēi Táng felt something warm spread through his whole gooey self, and it wasn't just the tea. It was the feeling of being part of something pretty instead of being the whole entire thing.

"I think," he said slowly, "I have been doing my art backwards. The best swirl isn't the one that fills the cup." He gave a happy theatrical sniffle. "It's the one that makes the *cup* look like a whole little world."

"Now you've got it," Boba laughed. "Want to make another?"

"Darling," said Hēi Táng, borrowing Lychee's word and striking a glorious pose, "I want to make a *hundred*."

And so they did. All through the warm, glowing night, the two of them painted cups together — Boba bouncing into place, Hēi Táng draping gentle tiger stripes that framed the tea instead of flooding it. Each one was different. Each one made somebody in the night market gasp. And on every single cup, Hēi Táng left a little room — for the tea, for the pearls, and for whoever came next.

"I make everything sweeter," Hēi Táng said proudly, swirling one last soft stripe as the lights began to dim. Then he added, quieter and warmer, "*Especially* when I'm not the only thing in the glass."

And in the front window, beneath a row of glowing tiger-stripe cups, two friends took a long, slow, well-earned bow — together this time — as the Sweet Swirl Tea Shop hummed its sleepy late-night song.
