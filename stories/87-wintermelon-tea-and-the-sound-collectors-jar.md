# Story 87: Wintermelon Tea & the Sound-Collector's Jar

**Main Character:** Dōngguā Chá (Wintermelon Tea) — a tall, amber, sweet-and-cooling drink in a glass bottle, an absent-minded collector
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A cluttered old apothecary-and-bottle shop in Tainan's lantern-lit back lanes
**Theme:** Listening closely is a kind of giving
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 聽 (tīng) = to listen

---

In the curliest, most forgotten back lane of Tainan — the kind of lane where the lanterns hum like sleepy bees and the cobblestones snore at night — there was a shop so crammed with bottles that even the dust had to wait outside for a turn to come in. And inside that shop lived Dōngguā Chá, who was, without question, the chattiest bottle of Wintermelon Tea in all of Taiwan.

He was tall and slender, the color of warm amber honey held up to a candle, with a faint sweet steam that smelled of caramel and cool melon. When he tipped from side to side, you could hear him swish — *blub, blub, swishhh* — which was lucky, because Dōngguā Chá adored sounds the way some people adore snacks.

"Listen! Oh, you simply must *listen!*" he cried to a passing moth that very evening. "I collect them, you see. The good ones. The crunchy ones. The *delicious* ones!"

The moth, who had places to be, fluttered off mid-sentence. Dōngguā Chá did not notice. He rarely did.

---

You see, all around the shop sat his treasures: hundreds of little empty glass jars, lined up on shelves that climbed clear to the ceiling. Each one, he insisted, held a sound he had caught.

"This one," he said proudly, swishing toward a fat round jar, "holds the *plip* of the very first raindrop of the rainy season. Took me three years to catch it. The drop kept arriving early."

He bobbed to the next. "And *this* one holds the **咯咯** (gē gē) = giggle of a dumpling who heard a joke about garlic. Marvelous joke. I'll tell it to you sometime. Actually, I'll tell it now—"

And off he went, telling the garlic joke, then a joke about a turnip, then a long rambling story about a spoon he once knew. The jars listened, because jars are excellent listeners and have very little choice in the matter.

"This green one," he swished on, "holds the *clink* of two ice cubes meeting for the very first time. Love at first clink. They were never apart again." He sighed happily at the green jar. "And this teeny one holds a hiccup. Just one. A very fine hiccup. I'm saving it for a special occasion."

Now, you might ask — and it would be a sensible question — how exactly does a bottle of tea *catch* a sound and shut it up in a jar? Dōngguā Chá would tell you it takes a steady grip, an open lid, and an enormous amount of confidence. He had all three. What he did *not* have, as we are about to see, was the faintest idea that he might be missing the whole point.

The truth — and we may as well whisper it, since Dōngguā Chá certainly never did — was this: every single jar was completely, perfectly *empty*. There were no bottled sounds. You cannot put a giggle in a jar any more than you can fold up a sunbeam and keep it in your pocket. But Dōngguā Chá believed it with his whole glassy heart, and so the jars stayed on their shelves, gleaming and hopeful and silent.

He simply loved sounds so much that he was sure, *positive*, he had caught them. The funny thing about loving to talk is that it can leave very little room for anything else to be true.

---

One drizzly evening, the shop's little brass bell went *ting*, and in slid a small new arrival.

It was a square of **凤梨酥** (fèng lí sū) = pineapple cake, golden and buttery-edged, with a soft jammy middle. But this pineapple cake did not look golden and warm at all. It looked crumbly and folded-in, sitting in the doorway with its paper wrapper drooping like a coat too big for it.

"AH-HA!" boomed Dōngguā Chá, swishing so hard he nearly tipped his cap. "A new friend! A new SOUND! Welcome, welcome! Tell me everything! Where are you from? What's the funniest noise you've ever made? Do you crunch or do you crumble? Oh, I bet you crumble *beautifully*—"

The pineapple cake opened its mouth.

Nothing came out.

It looked at the floor and pressed its wrapper a little tighter and said... not one single thing.

Dōngguā Chá blinked. An empty jar! A guest who made no sound at all! How tremendously unusual. How wonderfully collectible.

"Don't you worry," he announced, grabbing his quietest, smallest, dustiest jar — the one on the very bottom shelf that had never once been filled. "I'll just hold this *right here*, and the moment you make a lovely sound, I'll catch it. Off you go! Make a sound! Any sound at all!"

The pineapple cake said nothing.

So Dōngguā Chá, who could not bear a silence the way some folks cannot bear an itch, did the only thing he knew how to do.

He filled it.

---

"Did I ever tell you about the rainy day I caught the *plip*?" he said, swishing in a circle. "Three years, that one took. Now, the turnip joke — you'll love the turnip joke. There once was a turnip who thought he was a radish..."

He told the turnip joke. He told the spoon story. He described all seventeen jars on the top shelf and the smell of the lane and his theory about why moths are always in a hurry. (His theory was that moths are late for a very important party and have been late for a thousand years. He was quite committed to this theory.)

"And *here*," he went on, swishing to a tall blue jar, "is the squeak of a mouse who stepped on a grape. And *here* is the burp of a kettle. And *here* — oh, you'll adore this — is the exact sound a noodle makes when it's surprised. It goes *slorp* but *backward*. Hard to describe. Easy to catch, if you're quick and a little bit lucky and don't mind getting splashed."

He talked through the drizzle stopping. He talked through the lanterns turning gold. He offered the pineapple cake a tour of the bottom three shelves, then gave it anyway without waiting for a yes. He talked until his own amber tea sloshed tired against the glass and the steam off his cap had gone thin and quiet.

And the whole time, the little pineapple cake sat in the doorway, smaller and smaller, the way a snack does when it has something heavy inside and nowhere to set it down.

Finally Dōngguā Chá paused — only to think up his next story — and in that tiny crack of quiet, he heard something he had never once heard in all his collecting life.

He heard *nothing from the cake.*

And for the first time, the nothing didn't feel like an empty jar waiting to be filled. It felt like a closed door with someone sad standing behind it.

Dōngguā Chá looked down at the dusty little jar in his grasp. Still empty. Of course it was empty. He had been so busy making his *own* noise that he had left no room at all for the cake's.

"Oh," he said softly. And then, even softer: "Oh, I've been doing it all wrong, haven't I."

---

Now, here is the brave thing Dōngguā Chá did, and it was much harder for him than catching a raindrop.

He stopped talking.

He set the little jar down. He swished himself gently across the floor until he was sitting right beside the pineapple cake, close enough to share the warm of the lantern. And he did the one thing a chatty bottle almost never does.

He **聽** (tīng) = listened.

He listened with his whole glassy self — not waiting for his turn, not hunting for a sound to catch, not lining up the next joke. He just leaned in, quiet and round and patient, and let the silence be the cake's, not his.

The rain dripped softly off the eaves. A lantern crackled. The shop held its breath. Somewhere far off a moth was, presumably, still late for its party.

It was, for Dōngguā Chá, the longest quiet of his entire life. Three whole minutes. He wanted to fill it so badly that his tea practically fizzed. A joke rose up in him — a *brilliant* joke, possibly his best, something about a radish and a raincoat — and he swallowed it back down with a small heroic *gulp*.

And slowly — so slowly — the pineapple cake's wrapper unfolded the littlest bit.

"I came from a bakery in the north," it whispered, in a voice like crumbs settling. "My whole family was on the same tray. Warm oven. Everybody close. And then... they were all bought, one by one, and I got packed in a box and sent here, and I don't *know* anybody, and the lane is so dark, and I keep listening for my family's voices but all I hear is..."

It stopped. It looked up at Dōngguā Chá, who was being magnificently, gloriously quiet.

"...but you're the first one who's actually heard me," the cake finished. "Everyone talks *at* me. You're the first who let me talk."

Dōngguā Chá did not say a word. He simply nodded, and reached over, and held up the smallest jar — the dusty one, the never-filled one — and held it close while the pineapple cake told the whole story. The warm oven. The crowded tray. The names of every cousin. The smell of pineapple jam on a Sunday.

And when the cake was finished, and let out one long, soft, unfolded sigh — *haaaah* — Dōngguā Chá pressed the lid onto that little jar with the gentlest *click* he had ever managed.

"There," he whispered. "Caught it."

He set the jar on the very top shelf, all by itself, in the place of honor.

"It's the truest sound I've ever kept," he said, eyes shining like amber in candlelight. "It's the sound of somebody finally being heard."

---

From that night on, the lonely little pineapple cake was lonely no more, because it had a friend — a tall amber friend who, very slowly and with much swishing effort, was learning that the best gift you can give a sad heart isn't a story or a joke or even a hug.

Sometimes it's just to go quiet, lean in close, and *listen*.

And the jokes still came, of course. The turnip joke especially. But now Dōngguā Chá always told it *after* he had asked, "And how are you, dear friend?" — and then waited, all the way, for the whole answer.

The dusty little jar still sits on the top shelf in that curly Tainan lane. And if you ever pass by, and the lanterns are humming, and you press your ear very close... you won't hear a single thing.

Because the truest sounds aren't kept in the jar at all.

They're kept in the listening.

## Goodnight Blessing

May your busy thoughts grow soft and slow,
may the lanterns of your dreams burn low.
If someone you love has something to say,
lean in, stay close, and listen today.
And tonight, sweet one, the whole world will keep
quiet and kind while you drift off to sleep.
You are heard. You are held. You are loved through and through.

Goodnight.
