# Story 80: Bubble Waffle Sisters & the Kite Festival Sky

**Main Character:** Shuāng Bāo Tāi (the Double-Bun Twins) — two fried twin-dough buns joined at the middle, one daring and one cautious
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** The windy grassy hills of a Hsinchu kite festival on a blustery afternoon
**Theme:** Two who pull in different directions can still fly the same kite
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 風箏 (fēngzheng) = kite

---

On the breeziest hill in all of Hsinchu, where the grass combed itself sideways and the clouds raced like they were late for dinner, there rolled a pastry shaped like the number eight. Or maybe two doughnuts who had hugged so hard they couldn't let go. This was Shuāng Bāo Tāi — the **雙胞胎** (shuāng bāo tāi) = twins — two golden fried buns joined snugly at the middle, crispy-edged and dusted with sugar that sparkled when they tumbled.

The bun on the left was Dou. She was brave, bold, and bouncy, with a freckle of sesame on her chin and absolutely no fear of anything — not heights, not spiders, not even the squeaky noise the front gate made at midnight. Her crust was toasty-dark and a little reckless-looking, the way crust gets when it leaps into the fryer instead of waiting its turn.

The bun on the right was Mi. She was careful, thoughtful, and a tiny bit nervous, with a crinkle in her crust she got from worrying — about the weather, mostly, and about whether they'd remembered to pack snacks (they hadn't), and about whether buns were even *supposed* to fly. Her edges were pale gold and neatly tidy, because tidy edges helped her feel calm.

Together they made one slightly lopsided pastry who could never quite walk in a straight line, because Dou always tugged left and Mi always leaned right, and so they mostly rolled in cheerful little circles, which is a perfectly nice way to travel if you are not in a hurry.

And on this windy afternoon, the twins had one enormous dream.

"We," announced Dou, "are going to fly the GRANDEST kite at the whole festival!"

"We are?" squeaked Mi. "Are you sure? It looks awfully windy up there—"

But it was too late. The dream had already been declared.

---

The hill was a riot of color. Everywhere the twins rolled, **風箏** (fēngzheng) = kites swooped and dove and looped through the sky — dragon kites with rippling tails, butterfly kites flapping silk wings, a kite shaped like an enormous startled octopus that kept tangling with everyone.

The twins rolled their way up the hill in a wobbly zigzag — left, right, left, right — bumping past a picnic blanket, a sleeping grandpa, and a basket of egg tarts who shouted "WATCH IT!" as they tumbled by. ("Sorry!" said the twins. "We can't help it!")

At the very top of the hill, a kite vendor (a kindly old steamed bun with flour on his cheeks and a hat made entirely of receipts) handed the twins the most magnificent kite of all. It was shaped like a phoenix, with feathers of red and gold paper and a tail so long it brushed the grass and tickled the toes of anyone standing nearby.

"One string," said the vendor, "but two of you. Remember — a fēngzheng flies on the wind, not on the puller. Listen, and it will lift you." Then he winked, which kite vendors apparently love to do, and rolled away.

Dou grabbed the left side of the string. Mi grabbed the right. (When you are joined at the middle, you each get a hand, and both hands belong to the same kite.)

"Okay," said Dou, bouncing with excitement. "On three, we RUN and YANK as hard as we can!"

"Or," offered Mi gently, "we could let the string out slow and easy and see what the wind wants—"

"YANK!" said Dou.

"EASY!" said Mi.

And that, dear reader, was the beginning of the trouble.

---

Dou ran left. Mi leaned right. The phoenix kite leapt into the air, wobbled, looked extremely confused, and then nose-dived straight into a patch of mud with a *splort*.

"You pulled too hard!" said Mi.

"You didn't pull enough!" said Dou.

They tried again. This time Mi let the string go loose to keep the peace, but Dou yanked twice as hard to make up for it, and the kite shot up like a startled chicken, flipped over backwards, and crashed into the snack stand, knocking a tray of pineapple cakes into the air. (The pineapple cakes, to their credit, looked thrilled.)

"Sorry!" called the twins together — because some things they still did in unison.

They tried a third time. The phoenix made it nearly two whole seconds into the air before Dou's yank and Mi's hold canceled each other out completely, leaving the kite hovering in baffled silence — and then dropping like a stone onto a passing dumpling, who wore it as a hat for the rest of the afternoon and seemed rather pleased about it.

They tried a fourth time, and a fifth. Dou pulled hard; Mi held back. Mi held back; Dou pulled harder. The poor phoenix kite spent more time face-first in the grass than it did in the sky. It crashed into a flowerpot. It crashed into a fence. It crashed, somehow, *upward* into a low cloud and came back damp. By the seventh crash, its paper feathers had a grass stain, a mud smudge, a flower petal stuck to its beak, and one very crumpled wing.

"At least it's a well-traveled kite now," offered Mi weakly.

All around them, other kites soared. The dragon climbed. The butterfly danced. Even the startled octopus had figured itself out and was now bobbing proudly. And the grandest kite of all — the magnificent phoenix — lay flat on the hill like a tired pancake, while two fried buns argued over a single string.

"This is hopeless," sighed Mi, her crinkle crinkling deeper. "We pull in different directions. We always have. You go left, I go right. Maybe twins joined in the middle just aren't meant to fly kites."

Dou opened her mouth to say something bold and bouncy. But for once, she didn't. She looked at the sad muddy phoenix. She looked at Mi's worried crust. And she felt, deep in her doughy middle, that something was wrong with the whole plan.

---

So Dou did a brave thing — braver than yanking, even. She stopped.

"Mi," she said quietly. "Let's not pull at all for a minute. Let's just... feel."

The twins went still. They closed their eyes. They held the string loose between them and waited. Around them the festival hushed to a soft hum — the flap of distant kites, the laugh of a faraway child, the *shush* of grass leaning all one way.

And then — they felt it. The wind. It came in long, gentle breaths, like the hill itself was breathing. *Whoosh* in. *Hush* out. *Whoosh* in. *Hush* out. The string tugged softly with every breath, rising and falling all on its own.

"Do you feel that?" whispered Mi. "It's not steady. It comes in waves."

"It does," breathed Dou. "When the wind *pushes*... the kite wants to go up. That's when we should ease the string out together. And when the wind *rests*..."

"...that's when we gently tug, to hold what we've climbed," finished Mi.

The twins looked at each other — really looked, the way only someone joined to you at the middle can. Dou the daring. Mi the careful. And for the very first time, they understood: Dou's tugging wasn't *wrong*, and Mi's easing wasn't *wrong*. They'd just been doing them at the wrong moments, against each other, instead of taking turns *with* the wind.

"Okay," said Dou, and her voice had no bounce in it now, only calm. "We don't fight the wind. We dance with it. You count the breaths."

Mi smiled her careful smile. "**準備** (zhǔnbèi) = ready?"

"Ready," said Dou.

---

They lifted the muddy phoenix one more time. Mi watched the grass bend. "Wind's coming... now!"

*Whoosh.* Together, they eased the string out — loose, loose, gentle — and the phoenix caught the breath of air and climbed.

"Resting!" called Mi as the gust softened. And together — at the very same gentle moment — they tugged, just a little, holding the height they'd won. The kite hovered, steady as a star.

"Wind's coming... NOW!" Out went the string. Up went the phoenix, higher.

*Tug.* Hold. *Ease.* Climb. *Tug.* Hold. *Ease.* Climb.

Dou pulled when it was time to pull. Mi eased when it was time to ease. And because they finally did it at the *same* moment — not one winning and one losing, but two halves of one breath — the phoenix kite rose and rose and rose.

It passed the butterfly. It passed the dragon. It sailed clear over the startled octopus, who looked more startled than ever and then, being a good sport, gave a little wave with one of its eight paper arms. Up through the racing clouds the muddy phoenix climbed, its grass-stained feathers glowing gold in the afternoon sun, its flower-petal beak leading the way, until it was the highest **風箏** in all of Hsinchu — a tiny brave spark of red and gold at the very top of the sky, so high it tickled the belly of a passing cloud.

The whole hill gasped. Children pointed. The kite vendor, far below, clapped his floury hands and shouted, "I *told* you! Listen, and it lifts you!"

And down on the grass, two fried buns joined at the middle held one string between them and laughed and laughed, sugar sparkling, crusts crinkling with joy instead of worry.

"We did it!" cried Mi. "We pulled in different directions—"

"—and flew the same kite," finished Dou.

The wind breathed in. The phoenix soared. And the twins, who would never agree on everything, agreed on this one perfect thing: that the best flying isn't about who pulls hardest. It's about feeling the same wind, and choosing — together — the very same gentle moment to let go.

---

As the sun melted gold over the Hsinchu hills, the twins reeled their brave muddy phoenix slowly home, breath by breath, the way they'd learned. They were tired in the very nicest way, the way you get when you've done something wonderful with someone you love. Dou yawned a bold yawn. Mi yawned a careful one. And joined at the middle, they yawned the rest of the way together.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, the wind does not blow all at once — it breathes, in and out, slow and soft, just like you are breathing now. You don't have to pull hard at the world tonight. You don't have to win any tug. Somewhere up in the dark, a tiny golden kite is still drifting, held gently by two who learned to ease and hold at the very same moment. Let your worries go loose on the string. Let the sleepy wind lift you. And know that even when you and someone you love pull in different ways, you can still, always, fly the very same sky. Breathe in. Breathe out. Goodnight.
