# Story 91: The Hundred-Snack Cooking School

**Main Character:** Lǔ Ròu (Braised Pork Rice)
**Guest Characters:** Niú Niú, Red Bean, Cōng Cōng
**Setting:** A bustling pop-up cooking school teaching young snacks the old recipes
**Theme:** Pass it on — that's how flavor lasts forever.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 學習 (xuéxí) = to learn

---

On the morning the school opened, Lǔ Ròu burned the toast.

This was a tremendous problem, because Lǔ Ròu was a bowl of braised pork rice — glossy minced pork piled on warm rice, a single perfect tea egg sitting on top like a hat — and bowls of rice are not supposed to be anywhere *near* toast. He had wandered into the wrong kitchen. He had pressed a button he did not understand. Now a tiny black square was smoking gently inside a machine, and a hundred very small snacks were watching him from a hundred tiny stools.

"Ah," said Lǔ Ròu, steam curling off his rice. "Lesson one. Even old bowls make mistakes."

A hundred tiny snacks laughed. And just like that, the Hundred-Snack Cooking School was officially, smokily, *deliciously* open.

---

The idea had started, as good ideas often do, over a slow steaming bowl on a cold night.

Lǔ Ròu had been sitting at the corner of the night market, soy-sauce sheen catching the lantern light, when he noticed something that made his tea egg tilt with worry. The little snacks of the market — the brand-new ones, fresh out of the oven and the fryer that very week — didn't know the old recipes anymore. They knew how to glow under a neon sign. They knew the newest bubble-tea dances. But ask one how to braise pork low and slow until it melted, or how to fold a bun so soft it sighed, and they'd just blink up at you with shiny, clueless little eyes.

"Simple things, done with love," Lǔ Ròu murmured, which was his way of saying *somebody ought to do something.* And because he was a humble bowl who never expected anyone to mean *him,* he was very surprised when he realized that the somebody was going to be him.

So he gathered the elders.

The first to arrive was **牛肉麵** (niú ròu miàn) = beef noodle soup — that is, Niú Niú, a great warm clay bowl wrapped in a long wheat-noodle scarf, with a steam-cloud halo and tender beef-cube shoulders. He came rumbling in slow and cozy, like a winter night that had learned to walk.

"A cooking school, kiddo?" Niú Niú slurped thoughtfully. "I like it. The little ones should know the old flavors." He tossed his noodle scarf over one shoulder, dramatically, and accidentally flung it into a bucket of broth. "...That'll come right out," he said.

Next came Red Bean, the burgundy bean with the white stripe down her back, warm and nurturing as a grandma, never without her wooden ladle. She took one look at the hundred empty stools being set up and got a soft, misty look in her eye. "Oh, the *babies,*" she whispered. "I'm going to feed every single one of them until they wobble."

And last, cartwheeling through the door on his crispy golden edges, came Cōng Cōng — the flaky scallion pancake with green freckles and flappy pancake hands perfect for high-fives. He landed in a spin, struck a pose, and announced: "Heard there's a school. Heard it needs *style.* Let's flip the script!"

That was the teaching staff. A bowl, a bowl, a bean, and a pancake. Lǔ Ròu thought it was the finest faculty any school ever had.

---

The school was a pop-up tent strung with paper lanterns and dangling wooden spoons, and the hundred tiny students were the youngest, freshest snacks in all of Taiwan. There were baby buns no bigger than thumbprints. There were teaspoon-sized dumplings. There was one extremely small popcorn-chicken nugget who would not stop bouncing and kept introducing himself as "Crunch, the *littlest* Crunch."

Lǔ Ròu cleared his throat, which for a bowl of rice meant a soft warm rumble. "Today," he said, "we **學習** (xuéxí) = learn. To *xuéxí* means to take something old and beautiful and carry it forward, so it never disappears. Are you ready?"

"READY!" shouted a hundred tiny voices, and one of them fell off a stool from excitement.

It did not go smoothly.

Red Bean's station was supposed to teach gentle sweet-bean filling. Within four minutes, the tiny snacks had discovered that bean paste is extremely fun to fling, and Red Bean was standing in a polka-dotted storm of it, laughing so hard her white stripe wiggled. "Use the *ladle,* loves, not your *faces!*" she cried, which only made them aim for her face.

Niú Niú's noodle-pulling class was a glorious tangle. He showed the babies how to stretch wheat dough long and springy, swinging it grandly over his head — and a dozen tiny students immediately got wrapped up like presents in their own noodles. "Slurp first, worry later," Niú Niú chuckled, unwinding a giggling dumpling. "That's a noodle, kiddo, not a scarf. Although... it *does* look good on you."

And Cōng Cōng's flipping class? Cōng Cōng's flipping class became airborne. He'd meant to teach a simple pancake-toss. Instead, eleven students attempted somersaults at once, and the tent briefly filled with spinning, shrieking, delighted little snacks before they all came down in a soft heap of crumbs and giggles. "Okay," Cōng Cōng wheezed, flat on his back. "Maybe we *learn to walk* before we *flip the script.*"

By lunchtime, the kitchen was a disaster. Flour ghosts drifted in the air. Bean paste freckled the walls. A trio of baby buns had glued themselves together into one large, very confused bun. Somebody had taught a teaspoon dumpling to whistle, and now it would not stop. And there was a noodle hanging from the ceiling that nobody could explain, and that Niú Niú kept looking up at with quiet, baffled respect.

And not a single recipe had been learned.

---

Lǔ Ròu sat down on an overturned bucket, his steam drooping a little. He had wanted so badly to pass on the old flavors — and instead he'd made a hundred small messes and a hundred sticky students. Maybe, he thought sadly, some bowls were just meant to *be* dinner. Maybe a humble bowl of rice had no business teaching anybody anything.

That was when the littlest Crunch climbed up onto his rim.

"Mister Lǔ Ròu?" the tiny nugget peeped. "How come your pork is so *shiny?*"

Lǔ Ròu blinked. "Oh. That's the braising. Low and slow, all day, with soy sauce and rock sugar and five-spice and patience. My grandmother taught me. And her grandmother taught her."

"All *day?*" gasped Crunch. "What do you *do* all day?"

"You wait," said Lǔ Ròu softly, "and you stir, and you taste, and you let the flavor become itself. That's the secret nobody can rush." He stirred his own pork gently with one chopstick, and the smell of five-spice and home unrolled across the tent like a warm blanket.

And a funny thing happened.

The hundred tiny snacks stopped flinging. They stopped flipping. They climbed down off the ceiling-noodle and the bean-walls and the somersault-pile, and they gathered close — a hundred small faces turned up toward the glossy, humble bowl — and they *listened.* Because the secret to teaching, Lǔ Ròu suddenly understood, was not to perform. It was to share something you truly loved, slowly, until the loving caught.

"Watch," he said. And he braised.

The tent went hushed and golden. A hundred tiny snacks leaned in so close their little noses nearly dipped into the warm. Lǔ Ròu took his time — that was the whole secret, the taking of time — and as the pork grew glossy and the smell grew deep, he talked the way his grandmother once talked to him: not like a teacher with a lesson, but like family sharing the only treasure that grows when you give it away.

"Taste before you add more," he said gently, and let the littlest Crunch dip one careful claw-tip into the sauce. Crunch's whole face lit up like a lantern.

"*Whoa,*" Crunch breathed. "It tastes like... like somebody loves me."

"That's the five-spice," said Lǔ Ròu, his steam wobbling with feeling. "And also, kiddo — yes. It's that too."

---

He taught them low and slow. He taught them patience tastes better than hurry. Red Bean came over and showed how a gentle hand makes the sweetest filling, and the babies — calmer now — pressed their tiny hands into the paste so carefully you could have cried. Niú Niú pulled one perfect noodle, glistening and endless, and the students watched it stretch and held their breath. Cōng Cōng did one slow, *beautiful* flip — just one — and the whole school sighed *ohhh.*

The elders weren't putting on a show anymore. They were giving away the best of themselves, recipe by recipe, the way it had been given to them. And the more they gave, the more they had — which made no mathematical sense at all and yet was completely, deliciously true.

By the time the lanterns glowed gold against the dark, every tiny snack had braised a thimble of pork, pulled a noodle, folded a bun, flipped a crumb. Crunch the littlest had made a single perfect bite of braised rice, no bigger than a sesame seed, and he carried it to Lǔ Ròu on his crispy palms like the most precious treasure in the world.

"I learned it," Crunch whispered. "I'll teach it too. When I'm big."

Lǔ Ròu's steam rose all the way to the lanterns. "That," he said, his voice thick and warm, "is how flavor lasts forever. You don't keep it. You *pass it on.*"

Niú Niú slung an arm around him, dripping broth and joy. "Best class I ever taught, kiddo."

"Best class we ever *made,*" said Red Bean, dabbing her eyes with her ladle.

And Cōng Cōng, balanced on the very tip of one crispy edge, raised a flappy hand. "So," he grinned. "Same time tomorrow?"

A hundred tiny voices roared *YES* — and one of them, naturally, fell off a stool again.

---

That night, the elders cleaned the tent together while the little ones slept in a warm pile of crumbs and contentment. Lǔ Ròu looked around at the bean-spotted walls and the mystery noodle and the hundred small sleeping faces, and he felt fuller than any bowl had ever felt — not full of pork or rice, but full of *handing something on.*

Some treats are meant to be eaten. But the very best recipes are meant to be *given* — and given, and given again — so that long after every bowl is empty, the flavor of love keeps cooking somewhere down the line. Simple things, done with love. Passed gently from hand to tiny hand.

Goodnight, little market. Goodnight, hundred small snacks. The pot is still warm, and the recipe is safe with you.

## Goodnight Blessing
May your hands remember every kind thing they were taught, little one, and may you pass each one gently along. May the people who love you steep into you slow and sweet, like soy sauce and patience, until you are warm all the way through. Tonight, let the pot simmer without you; let the lanterns dim; let the noodle stay tangled on the ceiling until morning. You have learned enough for one day, and you are loved more than enough for always. Rest now, small and shiny and full of good recipes. Goodnight.
