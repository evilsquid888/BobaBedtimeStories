# Story 85: Cuttlefish Ball & the Tangled Lantern Strings

**Main Character:** Huāzhī Wán (Cuttlefish Ball) — a bouncy, springy seafood ball on a skewer, eager and a little frazzled
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A temple courtyard the morning the festival lanterns must be hung
**Theme:** Asking for help untangles what you can't sort out alone
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 幫忙 (bāngmáng) = to help

---

The morning the festival lanterns had to go up, Huāzhī Wán bounced into the temple courtyard so fast he ricocheted off three pillars, a drum, and a very surprised pigeon — *boing, boing, boing, OOF.* He was a Cuttlefish Ball, you see: a plump, pearly-gray sphere of squiggly seafood, springy as a rubber thumb, skewered neatly through the middle on a slim bamboo stick that wobbled behind him like a tail. When he was excited he bounced. He was almost always excited. So mostly he was a blur with a stick.

"I," announced Cuttlefish Ball, landing in a perfect spin, "shall hang every single lantern myself. All two hundred of them. Before lunch!"

The pigeon gave him a long look that clearly meant *good luck with that, dumpling.*

Cuttlefish Ball did not believe in good luck. He believed in **努力** (nǔlì) = effort. And bouncing.

---

The lanterns were stacked in the courtyard in glowing crimson heaps — fat round ones, long skinny ones, ones shaped like rabbits and fish and one suspicious one shaped like a lantern shaped like a rabbit. Each came with a long silken cord for tying it high between the temple's wooden beams. Two hundred lanterns meant two hundred cords, and that, to a Cuttlefish Ball, was simply two hundred chances to bounce.

The temple courtyard itself was a wide, peaceful square of worn stone, swept clean for the festival, edged with dark wooden pillars that smelled faintly of incense and old rain. Up above, the beams crisscrossed like the bones of a great sleepy dragon, just begging to be strung with light. Aunties from the kitchen had told Cuttlefish Ball, very kindly, that there was no rush, that everyone would pitch in after the soup was ready.

But Cuttlefish Ball did not want to wait for the soup. He did not want everyone to pitch in. He wanted them to walk out, see two hundred lanterns already glowing, and gasp so hard their chopsticks dropped.

"Easy," he said, and got to work.

He sprang to the first beam — *boing!* — and looped a cord. He sprang to the second — *boing!* — and looped another. He bounced from beam to beam, trailing cord behind him, tying as he went, and oh, he was magnificent. He was a gray blur of glory. The first ten lanterns swayed up beautifully. The next ten too.

It was around lantern number twenty-three that things got, shall we say, *interesting.*

Because here is a fact about cords: they do not like to be left dangling. The moment Cuttlefish Ball's back was turned, the cord from lantern five reached out and shook hands with the cord from lantern eleven. The cord from lantern eleven introduced itself to lantern seventeen. Soon the whole courtyard was one big silk party, and nobody had told Cuttlefish Ball.

He bounced left. A cord tugged him right. He bounced up. A cord yanked him down. *Boing — SPROING — bonk.*

"Curious," he said, blinking. He was, at this point, wearing four cords like a scarf. He plucked at one with the very tip of his skewer, the way you might pull a thread you definitely should not pull, and discovered — to his deep alarm — that pulling that thread tightened *three other places he could not even see.* It was as if the knot had eyes in the back of its head and a wicked little sense of humor.

"Right," he said, with the confidence of a snack about to make everything much worse. "I'll simply out-bounce it."

He bounced harder. That, dear reader, is exactly the wrong thing to do when you are a springy ball inside a knot. Each bounce pulled a cord. Each pulled cord tugged another. The knot drew tight around his middle, then his skewer, then looped jauntily over the top of his head like a very bad hat.

*Boing.* Tighter. *Boing.* Tighter still.

Within five gloriously catastrophic minutes, Huāzhī Wán was bundled into the center of an enormous snarl of silk, suspended a foot off the ground, spinning gently, like a meatball in a hammock made entirely of spaghetti.

"...Ah," he said. "**糟糕** (zāogāo) = uh-oh."

---

Now. A wiser snack might have called out right away. But Cuttlefish Ball had announced — loudly, to a pigeon — that he would do this alone. And there is a particular kind of stubborn that lives in the middle of a knot and whispers, *you can fix this yourself, just bounce a little more.*

So he bounced a little more.

The knot did not care. The knot, if anything, seemed delighted. It cinched him so snugly that his cheeks puffed out and his skewer pointed straight up like a tiny flagpole of defeat.

He wriggled. He squirmed. He tried very hard to look like a Cuttlefish Ball who had meant to do this. "Just resting," he told the pigeon, who had returned to watch. "Athletes rest."

The pigeon was not fooled.

He tried twisting clockwise. Three cords tightened. He tried twisting the other way. Four cords tightened, plus one rude one that flicked his nose. He tried going perfectly still and hoping the knot would simply forget about him and wander off. The knot did not. Knots are excellent at remembering you.

He hung there, slowly rotating, while the sun climbed higher and the courtyard filled with the smell of the festival kitchens warming up — sweet osmanthus, frying scallion, the bubbling of soup — and not one single lantern got hung, because the only one hanging was *him.*

A small breeze wandered through the courtyard and set him swinging. He counted the beams as he turned past them. One, two, three... he had tied lanterns to exactly three of them before everything went sideways. Three beams out of forty. He had been so *sure* he'd be the hero of the whole festival, and instead the only thing he'd successfully hung up was his own dignity.

His eyes prickled. Not crying — Cuttlefish Balls are far too bouncy to cry — but the room had gone a bit shimmery. He had wanted to be so impressive. He had wanted everyone to walk in and *gasp.* Instead he was a dumpling in a doily, and the festival started at sundown, and the lanterns were still in their heaps, dark and unlit.

He opened his mouth to call out.

Then he closed it. Because what would he even say? *I made a mess?* *I can't do the one thing I bragged I could do?*

The knot gave a smug little squeeze.

And finally — finally — Cuttlefish Ball understood something that all the bouncing in the world had not taught him: that staying silent inside a tangle does not loosen the tangle. It only makes you dizzier.

So he took the biggest breath a small round seafood ball can take, and he squeaked, in a voice gone wobbly and high:

"**幫忙** (bāngmáng) = help! Someone, please — *bāngmáng!*"

---

The courtyard, it turned out, had not been empty at all. It had simply been *busy.*

From the kitchen doorway popped Tāngyuán, a soft glutinous rice ball dusted in peanut powder. From behind the drum rolled Má Shǔ, a stretchy mochi who could reach absolutely anything. Down from a beam fluttered a paper-thin Cōng Yóu Bǐng, a flaky scallion pancake who had been napping in the rafters. And waddling in from the soup pots came round little Wán Zi the fish ball, Cuttlefish Ball's own cousin, trailing steam.

They took one look at Huāzhī Wán — spinning, bundled, skewer pointed at the heavens — and to his enormous relief, not one of them laughed at him.

(Tāngyuán laughed a *little.* But it was a warm laugh, the kind that wraps around you instead of poking. Cuttlefish Ball found he didn't mind it at all.)

"Oh, **小花** (xiǎo huā) = little flower," said his cousin fondly, using his nickname. "You tried to do all two hundred *by yourself?*"

"I wanted to surprise everyone," Cuttlefish Ball admitted, very small.

"You did," said Má Shǔ, stretching up to examine the snarl. "Just not how you planned."

And then — here is the marvelous part — they did not yank. They did not bounce. Each friend simply found *one* strand and held it.

"I've got this one," said Tāngyuán, peanut powder puffing as she gripped a cord.

"This one's mine," said Cōng Yóu Bǐng, pinching a strand in a flaky corner.

"Mine," said Má Shǔ, and being stretchy, he took two — but gently.

"And I'll hold *you,* cousin," said little Wán Zi, cupping Cuttlefish Ball so he'd stop spinning.

"But that's only four of you," Cuttlefish Ball squeaked, "and there are about a *thousand* cords."

"There are not a thousand," said Má Shǔ patiently. "There only *feel* like a thousand when you're inside one and panicking. Watch."

"Now," said Tāngyuán, "everybody — *slowly.* No yanking. We each only worry about our own string. You take one, you mind one, you trust the others to mind theirs."

Each one stepped back, just a step, just their own single cord. And the knot that one frazzled snack could never undo in an hour came apart like morning fog. One loop slid free. Then another. Then a whole cascade, *slip-slip-slip,* and suddenly Huāzhī Wán dropped, unbundled and astonished, into his cousin's waiting hold.

"That took *four minutes,*" he whispered.

"Many hands," said Má Shǔ, "make a knot into a ribbon."

---

After that, the hanging went like a song.

Tāngyuán passed the cords, calling out which lantern went to which beam so nobody crossed their strings. Cōng Yóu Bǐng floated up to the high beams, light as a falling leaf, tying each loop with a flaky flourish. Má Shǔ stretched to the impossible corners, the ones no ladder could reach, going *boooiiing* in a way that made even the pigeon impressed. Wán Zi steadied the ladder and kept the cords from getting friendly with one another again. And Cuttlefish Ball — who turned out to be a *spectacular* bouncer when he wasn't trying to do everything at once — sprang each lantern up to a waiting friend, one perfect *boing* at a time.

The work that had crushed him in the morning turned into the best kind of game. They sang as they hung. They invented silly names for the lanterns — *Sir Wobbles, Madame Floof, the rabbit-shaped-as-a-lantern they called Mr. Confusing.* When Cuttlefish Ball bounced a lantern just a little too hard and it sailed clean over Cōng Yóu Bǐng's head, everyone laughed, including Cuttlefish Ball, and nobody minded at all. A mistake shared among friends, he was learning, weighs about a feather.

Two hundred lanterns. Up before lunch, with time to spare for soup.

As the sun went down and the first lantern was lit, then the second, then a hundred more, the whole courtyard bloomed into a sea of swaying red light, warm as a hug, glowing on every happy face. The aunties came out from the kitchen, and they *did* gasp — but they gasped at all five friends standing together beneath the light, which Cuttlefish Ball decided was an even better gasp than the one he'd imagined. Cuttlefish Ball bounced softly in place, watching, his skewer wobbling like a contented little tail.

"I couldn't have done it alone," he said.

"Nobody can," said his cousin, leaning against him. "That's not a sad thing, *xiǎo huā.* That's the *best* thing."

And the pigeon, perched on the brightest lantern of all, gave a single approving coo — which, in pigeon, means: *now you've got it, dumpling.*

## Goodnight Blessing

May your tangles be small and your friends be near,
and when the knots come — for they will, my dear —
may you remember the bravest of arts
is the squeak of a snack with the biggest of hearts:
*bāngmáng.* Just ask. The cords will come free,
and the lanterns will rise over you and over me.
So loosen your shoulders, let go of the day.
The hard knots can wait. They'll untie the soft way.
The lanterns are glowing. The soup's warm and deep.
Close your bright eyes now, little one. Sleep.

Goodnight.
