# Story 81: Lychee & the Hand-Me-Down Fan

**Main Character:** Lìzhī (Lychee)
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A grandmother fruit-seller's shaded stall at a sweltering traditional market
**Theme:** The oldest, plainest things often hold the most love
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 扇子 (shànzi) = fan

---

On the hottest morning of the entire summer — the kind of morning where the sun sat in the sky like an enormous fried egg refusing to flip — Lìzhī (lychee) strutted into the **市場** (shìchǎng) = market as though the whole place had been built just to admire her.

And honestly? Lychee believed it had.

She was a small, round little fruit, blushing rosy-pink all over, with bumpy crimson skin like a thousand tiny rubies stitched together. Beneath that bumpy peel she was pearly-white and impossibly sweet, the sort of sweetness that made bees write poems. But this morning Lychee was not thinking about her insides. This morning she was thinking about her brand-new **扇子** (shànzi) = fan.

Oh, that fan! Pure silk, the color of moonlight on a pond, painted with golden cranes and trimmed in shimmering thread. She had bought it yesterday and had practiced her elegant flutter in the mirror forty-one times.

"Out of my way, ordinary fruits," she sang, fanning grandly. "Beauty is passing through."

---

The market sweltered. Dragonfruit drooped. The mangoes were practically melting into smoothies on their own. But Lychee glided between the stalls, fluttering her silk fan with the air of an empress inspecting her kingdom.

"Notice anything?" she asked a tired-looking longan hanging on a vine.

"Notice... what?" said the longan, mopping his brow.

"My *fan*," said Lychee, with a flourish so dramatic she nearly fanned herself off her own feet. "Pure silk. Golden cranes. I am, quite simply, the most refined fruit at this market." She gave the fan a triumphant *snap*.

She wasn't done. Lychee paraded past the egg-tart bakery (where the tarts whistled at her, which she pretended not to enjoy), past the pile of dragonfruit (who were far too hot to clap), and right up to a wobbly tower of mochi who were leaning on each other like sleepy cousins.

"Behold," Lychee announced, "*art*." And she did her practiced flutter — number forty-two, her best one yet — tipping her head just so, the way she'd seen the opera singers do on television.

The mochi blinked. "Is your arm tired?" asked the smallest one. "You've been flapping for ages."

"Beauty is *never* tired," sniffed Lychee, who was, in fact, quite tired.

That was a mistake.

Because silk, you see, is a delicate, fussy, fragile thing — beautiful to look at and absolutely useless in a fight with a Taiwanese summer. With a sad little *rrrip*, a golden crane tore clean in half. Then another. Then the whole left wing of the fan flopped open like a broken umbrella.

"No — no no no!" Lychee gasped. She tried to fan herself anyway. The torn silk just wheezed and wobbled, pushing around a sad little puff of hot air that smelled faintly of overripe durian.

The mochi tried very hard not to giggle. They failed. A small puddle of giggles spread across their corner of the stall.

"It's the heat!" Lychee insisted, flapping the floppy silk like a wounded butterfly. "Silk is *temperamental*. Anyone refined would understand."

"It looks like a noodle now," observed the smallest mochi, helpfully.

The heat pressed down. Lychee began to sweat — and a sweating lychee is a serious matter, because the heat made her bumpy skin go *blotchy*. Her elegant rosy blush turned a panicked, splotchy red.

"Help," she squeaked, flapping the ruined fan. "I'm — I'm *poaching*! I'm becoming syrup! I refuse to become syrup, it is *deeply* unfashionable—"

(Reader, between you and me, a poached lychee in syrup is the most delicious thing in the world. But Lychee did not know that yet. She had a few lessons left to learn.)

---

She stumbled, dizzy and damp, into the shadiest corner of the market, where the air was a whisper cooler. There, in the deep cool shade, sat the oldest stall in the whole **市場**.

Behind a low wooden table heaped with fruit sat **阿嬤** (āmà) = grandmother — a tiny, wrinkly old wax apple, her skin a soft faded pink-brown, crinkled and gentle as tissue paper. She had been selling fruit in that exact spot for, oh, about four hundred summers, and she had the calm, unbothered look of someone who had seen every kind of heat there ever was. A little stool. A pot of tea gone lukewarm. A drowsy cat-shaped shadow napping near her basket.

It was the sort of corner that other fruits hurried past without a glance, because nothing there sparkled. Lychee herself had walked past it a hundred times and never once stopped. But today she had no choice — her legs simply gave out in the cool of the shade, plonk, like a dropped plum.

"You look like a dumpling left in the steamer too long, dear," said Āmà kindly.

"My fan *broke*," Lychee wailed, holding up the limp silk wreck. "It was the most beautiful fan in the world!"

"Hmm," said Āmà. She reached beneath her table and rummaged about — past baskets, past a sleeping cricket, past a tin of pineapple cakes — and pulled out a **扇子**.

But oh, what a sorry-looking thing it was.

It was a paper fan, old as old could be. Its bamboo ribs were worn smooth and honey-colored from years of handling. The paper was faded to the color of weak tea, soft and crinkly at the edges, and one corner had been mended with a tiny patch. It was, in a word, *shabby*.

Lychee looked at it the way you might look at a sock pulled out of a swimming pool.

"That?" she said, wrinkling her bumpy little nose. "Oh, no. No, thank you. I have *standards*, Āmà. I couldn't possibly be seen with something so... so *old*." She fanned at the air with her broken silk fan to demonstrate her refinement, which would have been more convincing if the broken silk fan had moved any air whatsoever. It did not. It made a faint, sad *fwip* and dropped a thread.

"Suit yourself," said Āmà comfortably, and she fanned *herself* with the shabby paper fan, looking perfectly cool and content.

"As you like," said Āmà, unbothered, and she gave the shabby fan a single, lazy flick.

*Whoosh.*

A breeze rolled off that little paper fan like a wave off the sea — cool, deep, and wide, smelling faintly of plum blossoms and rain. It washed right over Lychee and her splotchy skin, and for one glorious second she felt her panic melt clean away.

"...oh," said Lychee.

---

"Try it," said Āmà, holding it out. "Just once. An old fan won't bite."

Lychee hesitated. Beauty had standards. But beauty was also *extremely* hot. At last, with a sniff, she took the shabby thing between two delicate fingers and gave it a tiny, doubtful flap.

*Whoooosh.*

The cool came again — even better up close — riffling over her like the first breeze after a thunderstorm. Her blotches calmed. Her rosy blush came back. She gave the fan a bigger flap, then a *bigger* one, and laughed out loud as the air danced around her.

"It's — it's *wonderful*!" she cried, flapping happily, her splotches fading with every wave. "How? It's just old paper! My silk fan cost a fortune and it couldn't push a feather!"

Across the way, the wobbly tower of mochi had drifted over to watch, drawn by the lovely cool breeze rolling off the little fan. "Ooooh," they sighed together, all leaning into the draft. "Do that again." Even the drowsy cat-shadow stretched out a paw into the cool.

Lychee fanned them all, beaming, and for the first time all morning she wasn't performing for anyone. She was simply *sharing* something good.

"Silk is pretty," said Āmà, with a wink as soft as her wrinkly skin. "But paper, folded just so, *catches* the air. This fan knows how to work. It's had practice."

And that's when Lychee, holding it up to the light, finally looked — *really* looked — at the faded paper.

It wasn't blank.

Beneath the soft brown of all those summers, hidden under the crinkles and the little mended corner, the paper had been *painted* — by hand, long ago. There were lychees on it. Tiny rosy lychees, dozens of them, painted with loving, careful little brushstrokes. There were plum blossoms. There was a small, lopsided, smiling sun in the corner. And along the bottom edge, in faded ink, someone had written four wobbly characters: **慢慢來** (màn man lái) = take it slow.

"My granddaughter painted this," said Āmà softly. "Many summers ago, when she was small. She gave it to me to keep cool at the stall. I've fanned myself with her love every hot day since." She smiled. "That's why it cools so well, you know. A fan painted with love moves more than air."

Lychee went very quiet. She looked down at her ruined silk fan — the expensive one, the one with the golden cranes and the shimmering thread — and realized, with a small hot blush that had nothing to do with the weather, that no one had ever painted *anything* on it at all. It was beautiful. But it was empty.

This old, faded, crinkly, mended paper fan held *more*. It held four hundred summers. It held a little girl's careful brushstrokes. It held a sun that smiled.

"Āmà," Lychee whispered, "I almost said no to the best fan in the whole market just because it looked old. I'm — I'm sorry. I think I had my standards pointing the wrong way round."

Āmà chuckled, a sound like dry leaves in a kind breeze. "The plainest things often carry the most love, little lychee. New shine fades. But a thing that's been *loved* — that keeps its warmth forever." She closed Lychee's bumpy fingers gently around the fan. "Keep it. I think it would like to travel a while."

"Oh, I couldn't—"

"You could," said Āmà firmly. "Just promise me one thing. **慢慢來.** Take it slow. Fan gently. Treasure it. And when you're as old and crinkly as me, give it to someone who needs cooling down — and a lesson."

Lychee held the little fan to her chest as carefully as if it were made of moonbeams. "I promise. I promise, I promise."

---

So Lychee left the silk fan folded respectfully on Āmà's table — *somebody* might still like the cranes — and she walked back out into the sweltering market, no longer strutting, but *strolling*, fanning herself with slow, loving little flicks of the oldest, plainest, most beautiful fan she had ever held.

And here is the lovely part, reader. The cool breeze of that little paper fan made Lychee's sweet white insides sweeter still — so sweet that a kind dessert-maker bought her right up, gently, and slipped her into a chilled bowl of crushed ice and floral syrup. And Lychee, who had been so afraid of becoming syrup, simply *beamed*, because now she understood that to be loved and shared was the most beautiful thing a small round fruit could ever be.

She kept the fan tucked beside her the whole time, smiling sun and all.

---

## Goodnight Blessing

Little one, when the day is hot and your favorite shiny things break, remember the soft old fan with the smiling sun. The grandest treasures don't sparkle — they *hold*. They hold summers, and brushstrokes, and somebody's love folded carefully into every crease.

So tonight, hug close the oldest, softest, most well-loved thing you own. Breathe slow. **慢慢來.** Let it cool you, let it keep you, let it carry you gently down into your dreams.

Sleep sweet, my rosy little lychee. You are loved more than the newest, shiniest thing in all the world.

Goodnight.
