# Story 77: Salty Crisp Chicken & the Spice-Drawer Mystery

**Main Character:** Yán Sū Jī (Salt-and-Pepper Crispy Chicken) — a crunchy, basil-flecked fried snack with a detective's magnifying glass
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A bustling Shilin night-market fry stall after closing, lit by a single bulb
**Theme:** Curiosity solves more than strength ever could
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 為什麼 (wèishéme) = why

---

When the last customer wandered off into the warm Shilin night, the fry stall went quiet — and that was exactly when Yán Sū Jī woke up.

He was a small, golden lump of **鹽酥雞** (yán sū jī) = salt-and-pepper crispy chicken, crunchy all over, dusted with flecks of fried green basil that stuck out of him like tiny detective's eyebrows. Best of all, tucked under one crispy little arm, he carried a magnifying glass made from a polished drop of cooking oil.

"A detective never sleeps," he announced to absolutely no one, "except for naps, which are different and important."

He hopped down onto the counter, struck a noble pose, lost his balance, and rolled three times before stopping nose-first against the soy-sauce bottle.

"That," he said, muffled, "was on purpose."

And then the screaming started.

---

It wasn't scary screaming. It was the squeaky, panicky, hopping-in-circles kind of screaming that only kitchen ingredients can do.

"It's GONE!" wailed Auntie Garlic, a plump clove with a face like a worried dumpling. "The spice drawer! It's GONE!"

Yán Sū Jī skidded over to the little wooden drawer that lived beside the fryer. It was the most beloved object in the whole stall — the cozy home of white pepper, five-spice, chili flakes, and salt. Without it, tomorrow's chicken would taste like... well, like a sad wet napkin.

The drawer was wide open. Completely empty. Not a grain of pepper in sight.

"The morning rush is in just a few hours!" cried Mr. Sweet-Potato-Fry, flapping his long crispy arms. "No spices means no flavor! We must DO something! Let's flip the counter over! Let's shake the whole stall until they fall out!"

"Yes! Strength!" agreed Auntie Garlic. "We shall be VERY strong at the problem!"

Yán Sū Jī raised his magnifying glass like a knight raising a sword.

"No," he said. "We shall be curious at the problem."

Everyone stared.

"Strength flips counters," he explained, polishing his oily glass. "But curiosity finds answers. And the most powerful word in any language is a tiny one." He leaned in close, and his basil eyebrows wiggled. "**為什麼** (wèishéme) = why."

"Why?" repeated Auntie Garlic, blinking.

"Exactly! You're a natural." He beamed. "Now. Why is the drawer empty? Let us investigate."

---

He pressed his nose to the empty drawer and squinted through the glass. The whole stall held its breath. Somewhere outside, a scooter puttered past, and a stray cat yawned at the moon.

"Clue number one," he murmured. "The drawer is open, but not broken. So nobody *forced* it. Somebody *opened* it. Why?" He tapped his crispy chin, and a little flake of basil drifted off his eyebrow. "Why would somebody open a spice drawer in the middle of the night?"

"To eat the spices?" guessed Mr. Sweet-Potato-Fry.

"Nobody eats plain white pepper for fun," said Yán Sū Jī. "Trust me. I tried once. I sneezed for an hour and saw my ancestors. They told me to stop."

"Maybe a customer took them," offered Auntie Garlic. "An especially hungry one. With very big pockets."

"The stall was locked, Auntie. The only key is hanging on the hook, exactly where it always hangs, swinging gently and looking innocent." He narrowed his crispy eyes at the key. "*Suspiciously* innocent. But no. A locked stall means the culprit was already *inside* when we closed. Which means..." He spun dramatically. "...the thief is someone who lives here, or someone very, very small."

A nervous murmur rippled through the ingredients. Everyone looked at everyone else. Mr. Sweet-Potato-Fry edged away from Auntie Garlic, just in case.

"Don't panic," said Yán Sū Jī, panicking slightly himself. "A good detective trusts the clues, not the gossip."

Just then, from the dark corner of the stall, came a sound.

*"AH... AH... AH-CHOO!"*

Everyone froze.

"THE THIEF!" shouted Auntie Garlic. "GET HIM!"

Yán Sū Jī held up one crispy finger. "Wait. Don't pounce. Ask." He hopped toward the corner. "Hello? Why are you sneezing in the dark?"

A wobbly tower of napkins peeked out. It was Old Man Napkin, the softest, sleepiest resident of the stall, who spent his whole life being terribly dusty.

"I always sneeze," he sniffled. "I'm a napkin. It's basically my hobby. That, and falling over."

To prove it, he fell over.

"AHA!" cried Auntie Garlic. "Guilty! He fell over! That's exactly what a guilty napkin would do!"

"Auntie," said Yán Sū Jī patiently, "that is exactly what an *innocent* napkin would also do. Let us ask, not accuse." He turned to the napkin and lifted his magnifying glass. "Old Man Napkin. Why are you sneezing?"

"Because of the *dust*," wheezed the napkin. "I haven't touched your drawer. I can't even reach it. I'm a stack of paper with low self-esteem."

Yán Sū Jī lowered his glass. "A red herring," he sighed. "Or, in our stall, a red *fishball*. Old Man Napkin didn't take the spices. He just has the dustiest nose in Shilin." He patted the napkin kindly and helped him stand back up. "You see? If we'd grabbed the first sneeze we heard, we'd have bullied a poor tired napkin and learned absolutely nothing. Curiosity also means knowing when somebody is innocent. We don't blame. We ask *why* first."

Old Man Napkin sneezed gratefully, then fell over again.

---

Yán Sū Jī crawled along the counter on his belly, magnifying glass leading the way, until — *clink!* — something tiny rolled against his nose.

"AHA. Clue number two." He held it up. It was a single round peppercorn, dark and shy. "A runaway pepper! He must have escaped from the drawer. Quick, witness! What did you see?"

The peppercorn said nothing, because he was a peppercorn.

"He's not talking," whispered Auntie Garlic, awed. "He must be VERY brave. Or VERY guilty. I can't tell. Let's squash him to be sure."

"He's a seasoning, Auntie. They're the strong silent type, and we are *not* squashing the witnesses." Yán Sū Jī set the peppercorn gently on a folded napkin like a tiny king on a tiny throne. "Now. Why was this peppercorn on the floor? A peppercorn cannot fly. He cannot walk. So somebody *carried* him out of the drawer, and dropped him along the way. The spices didn't vanish into thin air — they went *somewhere*, in somebody's arms, and they left a trail behind them like crumbs." He pressed his glass to the counter, crawled forward an inch, and gasped so hard he nearly inhaled a basil flake. "And LOOK."

There, leading off the edge of the counter and across the floor, was a faint sprinkle of green.

"Basil leaves," he breathed. "Tiny ones. Torn from a bunch. Somebody dropped them as they walked. And **為什麼** would somebody carry basil through a fry stall at midnight?"

"Why?" the whole stall asked together, leaning in.

"That," said Yán Sū Jī, "is exactly what *why* is for."

---

He followed the trail. The others tiptoed behind in a nervous crispy parade — Auntie Garlic clutching Mr. Sweet-Potato-Fry, Mr. Sweet-Potato-Fry clutching the soy-sauce bottle, and the soy-sauce bottle clutching absolutely nothing, because it was a bottle. The basil leaves led down the table leg (Yán Sū Jī climbed down so carefully that he only fell off twice), across the cool tile floor, past a puddle of spilled tea, around a dropped chopstick the size of a fallen log, and toward a dark gap behind the loose baseboard where the single bulb's light could not reach.

"This is the part of the mystery," whispered Yán Sū Jī, "where a less curious detective would turn around and go have a snack."

"Can *we* go have a snack?" asked Mr. Sweet-Potato-Fry hopefully.

"No. We are the curious kind." He squared his crispy shoulders. "Onward."

The trail of green vanished into the gap.

"In there," whispered Yán Sū Jī.

"Charge!" said Mr. Sweet-Potato-Fry, rolling up his nonexistent sleeves. "We squash whatever's inside!"

Yán Sū Jī threw his crispy arms wide to block him. "We do NOT squash. We have come this far with *why*. We do not finish with *splat*." He took a breath, lowered his voice, and called softly into the dark: "Hello in there? I'm a detective. You're not in trouble. I only want to know — why?"

For a long moment, nothing.

Then, very slowly, two trembling whiskers poked out. Then a small grey nose. Then a tiny, sleepy mouse, no bigger than a soup spoon, wrapped cozily in a blanket of all the stall's missing spices.

"Please don't be cross," the mouse squeaked. "I didn't eat any of it. I promise. I only... I only wanted to *smell* it."

---

Yán Sū Jī lowered his magnifying glass and sat right down on the floor, eye to eye with the little thief who wasn't really a thief at all.

"Why?" he asked. Not loud. Not angry. Just curious. "Why did you want to smell it?"

The mouse pulled the spice-blanket tighter. "My nest is so cold and grey and plain," she whispered. "But every night your stall smells like five-spice and pepper and warm fried basil, and it's the *coziest* smell in the whole market. I thought... if I borrowed it, just for one night, my little home would smell like somewhere wonderful too. I was going to bring it all back before morning. Truly."

The whole stall went quiet. Auntie Garlic, who had wanted to be very strong at the problem, sniffled into her own papery skin.

"You see?" said Yán Sū Jī gently to his friends. "If we had flipped the counter, we'd have a mess and no answer. If we had charged in shouting, we'd have a frightened mouse and *still* no spices." He smiled. "But we asked **為什麼**. And *why* found us a cold little nest that only wanted to feel cozy. That's something strength could never have found at all."

He turned back to the mouse. "Here's what we'll do. You give the stall its spices back for the morning rush. And every single night after closing, you may come and curl up right here, by the warm fryer, and breathe in all the five-spice you like. Free of charge. Detective's orders."

The mouse's eyes went huge and shiny. "Really? Why would you be so kind?"

Yán Sū Jī winked. "Because somebody asked the right question first. And because a stall that smells this good should be shared."

Auntie Garlic wiped her papery eyes. "I really wanted to flip a counter," she admitted. "But this is much nicer. And there is no mess to clean up."

"That," said Yán Sū Jī, "is the secret nobody tells you about *why*. It almost never makes a mess."

---

So the spices came home, every grain and flake, and the drawer was full and happy again long before the first customer arrived. The morning chicken was perfect — crunchy, salty, peppery, and flecked with fried green basil, just the way it should be.

And true to his word, the little mouse came back each night, curling into a warm spoon-shaped ball beside the fryer, breathing in the coziest smell in all of Shilin. She named her nest "The Cozy Place," and she was never cold or grey again.

As for Yán Sū Jī, he polished his oily magnifying glass, gave a satisfied little crunch, and announced to the sleeping stall:

"Case closed. Solved not by being the biggest, nor the boldest, nor the strongest — but by being the very most curious." He yawned an enormous crispy yawn. "And now, the most important part of detective work."

"What's that?" murmured the sleepy mouse.

"A nap," he said. "They're different. And important."

---

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, when something puzzles you in the dark, you do not need to be the biggest or the bravest. You only need one small, brave word — **為什麼**, *why* — and the patience to ask it gently, again and again. Curiosity is a tiny lantern that lights up even the coziest hidden corners. So tuck the blanket close, breathe in something warm, and let your sleepy mind wonder all it likes. Every question can wait safely until morning. Goodnight.
