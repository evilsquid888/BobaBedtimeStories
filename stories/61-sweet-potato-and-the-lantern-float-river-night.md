# Story 61: Sweet Potato & the Lantern-Float River Night

**Main Character:** Dìguā (Sweet Potato)
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A slow river at night during a floating water-lantern ceremony in eastern Taiwan
**Theme:** Letting go is sometimes braver than holding on
**Mandarin Spotlight:** 放手 (fàngshǒu) = to let go

---

On the night the whole town came down to the river, Dìguā waddled along the bank wearing his skin like a comfy purple-red coat that didn't quite zip. He was a sweet potato, you see — plump as a sleepy cat, his orange insides peeking through one small scuff near his bottom, which he insisted was "a window, not a hole, thank you very much." When he walked, he rolled a little, and when he laughed, he rolled a lot.

"Wah!" he gasped, because the river was wearing a thousand small fires. Paper lanterns drifted on the dark water, each one glowing like a firefly that had eaten too much dinner. The town called it **放水燈** (fàng shuǐ dēng) = floating the water lanterns, and Dìguā thought it was the most beautiful, sniffly, glowy thing he had ever seen.

Then he noticed someone who was not floating anything at all.

A little rice dumpling sat on the bank, hugging a lantern so tightly his green wrapper crinkled.

"Hello," said Dìguā, rolling to a stop beside him. "Going to set that fellow loose?"

"No," said the dumpling.

"Ah," said Dìguā, sitting down with a soft *fwump*. "Excellent plan. Hug it till morning. Very practical."

The dumpling — whose name was Zòngzi, a sticky-rice bundle tied up in bamboo leaves with a string bow on top — did not laugh. That's how Dìguā knew it was serious. Usually his jokes landed at least *halfway*. A halfway laugh, he always said, is still a laugh wearing socks.

"What's the lantern for?" Dìguā asked gently.

Zòngzi's voice came out small. "You write a worry on it. Then you put it on the river, and the river carries it away." He hugged tighter. "Mine has my worry on it. A big one."

"How big? Watermelon big? Mountain big?"

"Bigger." Zòngzi squeezed his eyes shut. "It's that I'm scared. The dumpling-boat race is tomorrow, and everyone's watching, and I don't think I'm fast enough, and what if I fall in, and what if everybody laughs, and—" He stopped, out of breath. "If I let the lantern go, it means I'm not holding the worry anymore. But what if I *need* it? What if letting go means I stop being careful and then something bad happens? So I'm just… keeping it. Right here. Forever."

Dìguā nodded slowly, the way you nod at something you understand all the way down to your roots.

"You know," he said, "I used to live underground."

Zòngzi opened one eye. "Underground?"

"Oh, deep down. In the dark, cozy dirt. That's where sweet potatoes grow, little friend. We hold on with everything we've got — roots gripping, body swelling, all of us clinging to the earth saying *mine, mine, this is my spot.*" He patted the soil beside him fondly. "I was a champion holder-on. Best grip in the whole field. I held that dirt so hard I thought I would hold it my entire life."

"What happened?"

"A farmer came," said Dìguā. "With a fork and a friendly face. And she started to dig." He shivered, but it was a laughing shiver. "Oh, I was *terrified*. The light hurt my eyes. The air was cold. I gripped the dirt and I thought, *if I let go, I'll lose everything I know.* I held on so tight I nearly pulled the whole field up with me."

Zòngzi was listening now, lantern loosening just a little in his arms.

"But here's the funny part," Dìguā went on, leaning in like he was sharing the world's best secret. "Holding on *hurt*. The longer I clung, the more it ached. And the dirt I loved so much? It was getting smaller and darker and lonelier by the day. Down there I was just a lump. I never saw the sky. I never met a single soul." He spread his stubby arms wide at the glittering river. "I never saw *this.*"

"So you let go?"

"I learned a word," Dìguā said, and he said it like it was a magic spell. "**放手** (fàngshǒu) = to let go. Fàng — to release. Shǒu — hand. To open your hand. And do you know the bravest thing about an open hand?"

Zòngzi shook his head.

"It can hold something *new*." Dìguā's eyes twinkled. "A closed fist can only hold what's already in it. An open hand can be filled with morning, and friends, and warm steam, and a farmer who carries you to market singing off-key the entire way." He chuckled. "She really was terribly off-key. I let go anyway. Best decision a potato ever made."

The river slid past, gold and quiet. A grandmother lantern bobbed by, then a wobbly child-sized one, then a fat round one that looked, Dìguā thought, a bit like himself.

"But I'm still scared," Zòngzi whispered. "Even with the word. *Fàngshǒu.* I'm still scared of the race."

"Of course you are," Dìguā said warmly. "Letting go isn't pretending you were never scared. That's the trick people get wrong. Letting go means you *carried* the scared thing all the way to the river — see, you walked it here, you didn't leave it home — and then you decide it doesn't have to ride home with you. You set it down. You wave goodbye. You let the river do the carrying for a while." He bumped Zòngzi gently with his round side. "Holding on is easy, little dumpling. Any lump in the dirt can do that. *Letting go* — that's the brave one."

Zòngzi looked down at his lantern. The worry was written on it in shaky letters. His arms, he noticed, were tired from squeezing. They had been tired for a long time. He just hadn't let himself feel it.

"Will you do it with me?" he asked.

"I would be honored," said Dìguā, and meant it more than he had ever meant anything, including the time he said the night market sold the best **地瓜球** (dìguā qiú) = sweet potato balls, which, between us, were really just his distant cousins deep-fried and he chose not to think about it too hard.

They scooted to the very edge of the bank together. The water was cool and slow and patient, the way good water is. Dìguā placed one stubby hand under the lantern, and Zòngzi kept his on top, and for a moment they both just held it, feeling the little candle's warmth.

"On three," Dìguā said softly. "We open our hands. Both of us. **放手** — together."

"Together," Zòngzi breathed.

"**Yī**," said Dìguā. *One.*

"**Èr**," whispered Zòngzi. *Two.*

"**Sān**." *Three.*

And they let go.

For one heartbeat nothing happened, and Zòngzi nearly snatched it back. But then the river — gentle, golden, in no hurry at all — slid its cool palm beneath the lantern and lifted it away. The little light wobbled, found its balance, and began to drift.

Zòngzi's empty hands hovered in the air. They felt strange. Light. *Open.*

"Oh," he said.

The worry-lantern floated out to join the others, and that was when both of them truly saw it: not one lantern, not a hundred, but a whole river of them, a slow-flowing road of gold winding off between the dark hills. Every light was somebody's let-go. Every glow was a hand that had opened. The whole town's worries were out there sailing peacefully along together, keeping each other company, none of them alone.

"It's not gone," Zòngzi said wonderingly. "It's just… traveling."

"That's the secret," said Dìguā, his orange window glowing faintly in the lantern light. "We don't throw the worry away. We let it go *somewhere kind.*"

They sat on the bank a long, long while, two round friends watching the river carry its quiet golden cargo toward the sea. Zòngzi's shoulders had come down from up around his ears. Somewhere downstream his worry was having a lovely little voyage without him, and he found — to his great surprise — that he could wish it well.

"I think I might be fast enough tomorrow," he said at last. "And if I'm not, I think the river will still be here."

"Now *that*," said Dìguā, rolling happily onto his back to look at the stars, "is the wisest thing a dumpling ever said. Slightly wiser than anything a potato has said, but I'll allow it. Just this once."

Zòngzi laughed — a real one, all the way through, no socks on it at all.

And the lanterns drifted on.

## Goodnight Blessing

Sweet dreamer, if you are holding something tight tonight — a worry, a what-if, a small heavy thing — you do not have to keep gripping it while you sleep. You carried it bravely all the way here. That was enough. Now open your hand, slow and gentle, and let it drift onto the quiet river of dreams, where the gold lights go. It will be cared for. So will you. Breathe out. Let your fingers uncurl. **放手.** Let go.

Goodnight.
