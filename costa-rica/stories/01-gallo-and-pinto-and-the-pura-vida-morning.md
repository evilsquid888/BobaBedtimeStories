# Story 1: Gallo and Pinto and the Pura Vida Morning

**Main Character:** Gallo and Pinto (new)
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A sunny tin-roofed kitchen in a small Costa Rican town at dawn
**Theme:** The best mornings begin with friendship and a warm welcome.
**Spanish Spotlight:** Pura Vida (POO-rah VEE-dah) = pure life (Costa Rica's all-purpose happy greeting)

---

The first thing Gallo ever saw was a sunbeam, and the first thing he ever did was bonk his head on a frying pan.

"BONK," said the frying pan.

"Whoops-a-doodle, *hum*," said Gallo, rubbing the top of his little woven palm hat. He was a plump white grain of rice, glossy as a tiny pearl, with golden toasty bits speckled all over him and one cheerful fleck of red pepper stuck to his cheek like a freckle. He blinked. He wiggled. He discovered, with great delight, that he had feet.

"I have FEET!" he announced to the kitchen. "Two whole entire feet! Rise and pinto, what a morning!"

The kitchen did not answer. The kitchen was a cozy little room with a wobbly wooden table, a window with no glass (just sunshine pouring through), and a tin roof that went *tink-tink-tink* as it warmed up. Somewhere a rooster cleared his throat to crow, then thought better of it and went back to sleep.

And right beside Gallo, in the warm black skillet, somebody small and round was beginning to stir.

---

The somebody was a black bean.

He was smooth and deep and glossy, dark as a midnight pebble, with a tiny pale stripe along his middle that looked exactly like a smile. He yawned a slow bean yawn and reached for the smallest wooden spoon you ever saw, which he kept tucked beside him like a walking stick.

"Good morning," said the bean, in a voice as warm and soft as a blanket fresh off the line. "I'm Pinto."

"I'm Gallo!" said Gallo, hopping in a happy circle. "I just got feet! Do you have feet? You should get feet, they're marvelous, *hum*."

Pinto looked down. "I appear to have feet too," he said calmly. "Better together, *mae*." (*Mae*, by the way, is what good friends in Costa Rica call each other — like saying *buddy*, or *pal*, or *you wonderful goofball you*.)

The two of them stood in the skillet and looked around. The skillet was big and round and still a little warm from yesterday. On one side sat a fluffy pile of white rice grains, all fast asleep and snoring tiny rice snores. On the other side sat a heap of black beans, also snoozing, glistening like a hill of polished buttons.

"It's just us awake," Gallo whispered. "Everybody else is still dreaming."

"Then we should be very welcoming when they wake," said Pinto, "and very quiet until they do."

"I am SO good at quiet," said Gallo. Then he sneezed a tiny grain-of-rice sneeze, which sounded like *poff*, and flipped himself head over feet across the entire skillet.

"*Poff!*" he giggled, upside down. "Sorry. That was loud quiet."

Pinto rolled over to help him up, very gently, the way you'd help a friend who has just discovered both feet and gravity on the same morning. "You are a little bouncy," he observed.

"I'm a LOT bouncy," Gallo agreed proudly. He tried to do a dignified bow and accidentally somersaulted again. "*Poff!* See? Bouncy. It's a gift, *hum*."

Pinto smiled his stripe-smile. Already he could tell: he was the calm one, and Gallo was the giggly one, and that was exactly how a good pair was supposed to fit — like a spoon and a bowl, like a roof and the rain that goes *tink* upon it.

---

Now here is a thing about a Costa Rican kitchen at dawn. It does not stay sleepy for long.

The window-light grew warmer and golder. The tin roof went *TINK-TINK-TINK*. And then, from the doorway, came a smell — oh, a *wonderful* smell — a deep, dark, cozy smell that wrapped around the whole room like a hug.

"What IS that?" gasped Gallo, sniffing the air with his whole tiny body.

A coffee bean shuffled in.

He was a small dark bean with a deep crease down his middle, wearing a straw farmer's hat and leaning on a walking stick even tinier than Pinto's spoon. He smelled like morning itself — like warm earth and sunrise and the very nicest part of waking up.

"Buenas," said the coffee bean kindly. "I am Don Café. I roast on the windowsill so the whole town wakes up smiling." He tipped his little hat. "And you two are new."

"We got feet today!" said Gallo.

"Ah," said Don Café, with a slow, twinkly nod. "Then today you will learn something, young ones. Good things brew slowly." He sniffed the skillet and chuckled, a sound like a kettle humming. "And the best things, sometimes, do not happen alone."

"What do you mean?" asked Pinto, tilting curiously.

But Don Café only smiled his slow smile, sat himself down on the warm windowsill, and began — very gently — to roast. The wonderful smell grew. And grew. And grew.

"Should we wake everybody up?" Gallo whispered, hopping with excitement. "I could shout! I'm good at shouting! Watch — RISE AND —"

Pinto popped his tiny wooden spoon over Gallo's mouth, so the shout came out as "*rmff mff pmmtoo*."

"Not yet, *mae*," Pinto said kindly. "A good morning sneaks up on people gently. First the light. Then the smell. Then the shouting." He thought about it. "Maybe a little less shouting."

"Aww." Gallo's freckle drooped, then perked right back up. "Okay. But when it's shouting time, I get to do the shouting."

"When it is shouting time," Pinto agreed solemnly, "the shouting is all yours."

---

That smell did what smells do. It woke everybody up.

The pile of white rice grains stretched and yawned. The heap of black beans rolled and stirred. And all at once the skillet was full of sleepy little voices saying "buenas" and "good morning" and "ooh, who made that smell?"

The rice grains gathered on their side. The black beans gathered on theirs. And right down the middle of the skillet was a wide, empty stripe, like an invisible fence, with rice on one side and beans on the other, and nobody crossing over.

"Hmm," said Gallo, standing on the rice side. He looked across the empty stripe at Pinto, standing on the bean side. "Pinto, you're all the way over there."

"And you," said Pinto, "are all the way over here." He sounded a little sad about it.

The oldest rice grain (a wrinkly fellow named Tío Arroz) cleared his throat. "Rice belongs with rice," he said primly. "It is how it has always been."

"Beans belong with beans," agreed the oldest bean (a round grandmother named Abuela Frijol). "Tidy. Sensible. Separate." She fluffed herself importantly. "It keeps the pan organized."

"The pan does not look organized," whispered Gallo. "The pan looks lonely."

And it did. The rice grains on their side were all very white and very neat and very... beige. The beans on their side were all very black and very round and very... quiet. Nobody was dancing. Nobody was laughing. It was the tidiest, sleepiest, least-fun skillet in all of Costa Rica.

Gallo's little face fell. His one red-pepper freckle drooped. "But Pinto is my *mae*," he said. "We got feet on the same morning. How can rice belong with rice if my favorite person is a bean?"

A few of the younger rice grains shuffled their feet. A few of the younger beans peeked over at the empty stripe, curious. The truth is, nobody could quite remember *why* the fence was there. It had just always been there, the way fences sometimes are.

---

From the windowsill came a warm, slow chuckle.

"Young Gallo," said Don Café, "what is your name again?"

"Gallo, sir."

"And yours, little bean?"

"Pinto, sir."

Don Café's eyes crinkled all the way up. "Gallo," he said softly. "Pinto. Do you not hear it? When a grain of rice and a black bean wake up in the same warm pan in this little corner of the world... do you know what wonderful thing they are meant to become?"

The whole skillet went quiet. Even the tin roof stopped tinking to listen.

"*Gallo pinto*," said Don Café. "Spotted rooster. The most beloved breakfast in all of Costa Rica. Rice and beans, dancing together, sizzling in one happy pan." He tapped his walking stick. "You are not meant to stay on separate sides, little ones. You are meant to *mix*."

Gallo gasped. Pinto's stripe-smile grew enormous.

"You mean —" said Gallo.

"I think he means," said Pinto, "*come here*."

And Gallo did not walk. Gallo did not hop. Gallo *ran* — across the empty stripe, past the surprised rice grains, right into the middle of the skillet — and Pinto rolled to meet him, and the two of them collided in the happiest little tumble you ever saw.

---

Then the skillet began to warm.

Not in a scary way — oh no, never that. It was the coziest, most welcoming warmth, like sliding into a bath that's *just right*, like a sunbeam giving you a squeeze. The pan sang a soft *ssssss*, and a little oil shimmered, and suddenly everyone wanted to dance.

"Pura Vida!" cried Gallo, spinning Pinto by the hand. (**Pura Vida** (POO-rah VEE-dah) = *pure life* — it's what Costa Ricans say for hello, goodbye, thank you, no worries, isn't-life-wonderful, and about a hundred other happy things, all in two little words.)

"*Pura Vida!*" the rice grains shouted, tumbling off their side.

"*Pura Vida!*" the black beans laughed, rolling off theirs.

And the invisible fence vanished completely. Rice and beans swirled together, round and round, white and black and golden and glossy, sizzling and giggling and hopping in a great big breakfast dance. Gallo lost his hat and a kind little bean caught it and bopped it back on his head. Pinto twirled his tiny spoon like a band leader. The whole pan smelled of toasty garlic and sweet pepper and warm, warm friendship.

"This is the best morning EVER," panted Gallo, sizzling with joy. "We're a — we're a — what are we again?"

"*Gallo pinto*," beamed Pinto, dizzy and delighted. "You and me, *mae*. They named the whole dish after the two of us."

"Better together," said Gallo.

"Better together," agreed Pinto.

---

And then — oh, this is the loveliest part — the kitchen door creaked open.

A sleepy little girl in pajamas padded in, rubbing her eyes, her hair sticking up in every direction. She climbed onto a wobbly wooden chair, set her chin in her hands, and looked at the sizzling, dancing, wonderful-smelling pan.

"Mmm," she said, with the biggest, drowsiest, happiest smile. "*Gallo pinto*. My favorite."

And every grain of rice and every glossy bean felt a glow of pride, warm from their toasty toes to the tops of their little heads — because there is no prouder, happier feeling in all the world than being the warm welcome that makes somebody smile in the morning.

"Ready, *mae*?" whispered Pinto.

"Ready," whispered Gallo, holding tight to his best friend's hand. "Rise and *pinto*."

The little girl lifted her spoon. The whole pan glowed like a sunrise. And together — always, happily, proudly together — Gallo and Pinto became the very best breakfast in the world.

Outside, the rooster finally crowed. The tin roof went *tink*. And Don Café, warm on the windowsill, smiled his slow and twinkly smile.

"Pura Vida," he murmured. "Good morning, little town. Good morning."

## Goodnight Blessing

Goodnight.
