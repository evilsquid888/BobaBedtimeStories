# Story 18: Don Café and the Cloud Forest Climb

**Main Character:** Don Café (a roasted Costa Rican coffee bean from the highlands)
**Guest Characters:** Gallo, Pinto
**Setting:** The misty, cloud-wrapped slopes of the Monteverde cloud forest
**Theme:** Choose kindness over the goal, and the wonder finds its own way to you.
**Spanish Spotlight:** **montaña** (mohn-TAH-nyah) — the mountain

---

It was so early that even the moon was thinking about going to bed.

Two tiny dots came up the dark trail — well, one bounced, and one rolled politely beside it. The bouncer was Gallo, a plump grain of fried rice in a little woven palm hat, hopping so fast his golden specks blurred. "Rise and pinto!" he sang, hum-hum-humming. The roller was Pinto, a glossy black bean with a pale stripe like a smile, carrying his tiny wooden spoon.

At the top of a mossy step waited the oldest, kindest coffee bean in all the highlands. Don Café wore a straw farmer's hat and leaned on a miniature walking stick, and he smelled, as he always did, of warm mornings and good news.

"Buenos días, little ones," he chuckled. "Tonight we climb the **montaña** (mohn-TAH-nyah) — the mountain — because at the very top there is a mossy lookout called the Misty Window. And at first light, and *only* at first light, the resplendent quetzal comes to greet the dawn there."

Gallo gasped so hard his hat spun. "The quetzal! Green like wet leaves, red like sunset, with a tail longer than a kite! Tico says he's the most beautiful bird in the whole forest!"

"He is," said Don Café. "But hear me, mae. The quetzal waits for no one. When the sun clears the ridge, he is gone. So tonight we walk briskly — and we do not dawdle."

Pinto tapped his wooden spoon once, like a tiny drum of agreement. "Then let's climb."

---

Up they went, into the cloud forest, where the trees wore sweaters of moss and the air was so wet you could practically drink the breeze.

And oh, the fog. The **niebla** (NYEH-blah) — the fog — was thicker than soup that night. So thick that Gallo chatted with a friendly stranger for two whole minutes before discovering the stranger was a rock.

"You could have SAID something," Gallo huffed at the rock.

"He's shy," said Pinto solemnly.

Don Café tapped along at a good steady pace, *tik-tik-tik* went his little stick, while the sky above the niebla slid from black to deep blue. Halfway. More than halfway. The Misty Window was maybe a hundred mossy steps above them, and the edge of the sky was just starting to think about turning pink.

"We're going to make it!" Gallo whisper-cheered. "We're early! We're SO early! The quetzal is going to look at us and say, *my, what punctual breakfast foods!*"

And that was the exact moment they heard the sound.

It came drifting through the fog, small and snuffly and wobbly at the edges. It sounded, Pinto thought, like somebody very little trying very hard to be brave and only mostly managing it.

"*Mamáaa?*" said the sound. "Mamá... where did the world go?"

---

They found her three steps off the trail, sitting in a puddle of fog: a baby **coatí** (koh-ah-TEE) — a fuzzy raccoon-cousin with a long ringed tail — and her tail was standing straight up behind her like a stripey question mark. She had a smudgy nose, enormous worried eyes, and leaves stuck in her fur in a way that suggested the leaves had won.

"Hello, little one," said Don Café gently. "Are you lost?"

"I am NOT lost," sniffled the baby coatí. "The path is lost. *I* know exactly where I am. I am... right here. It's everywhere *else* that's missing." Her bottom lip wobbled. "I followed a beetle. He was very interesting. And then the niebla came, and my nose got all confused, and now I can't smell my way back to my mamá."

Then she leaned down, sniffed Pinto with great interest, and opened her mouth.

"I am a *bean*," said Pinto quickly, rolling back a step, "not a berry. Common mistake. Happens weekly."

The baby coatí giggled — a small, damp, snuffly giggle — and then remembered she was lost, and her ears drooped all over again.

Gallo looked at the baby. Then he looked up the trail, where the Misty Window waited, and at the sky, where the pink was spreading like spilled juice. Then back at the baby. Then back at the sky.

"Ohhhh no," he said. "Oh no, oh no. Don Café. The sun is coming. If we stop now, we'll miss the quetzal. He only comes at first light! Once! And the coatí dens are *down* the mountain, the whole wrong way!"

Don Café was quiet for a moment. The fog drifted between them, soft as breath.

"You are right, Gallo," he said at last. "We cannot do both. So I will not choose for you. I will only ask you this: what kind of beans do we want to be?"

Gallo groaned the longest groan in the history of rice. "Why is being good so *itchy*," he muttered. He hopped in a tight little circle. He squeezed his eyes shut. Then he hopped over and stood next to the baby coatí, arms crossed, chin up.

"Fine. FINE. The quetzal has a thousand mornings," he announced. "She only has one mamá."

Pinto rolled up beside him and patted the baby's paw with his tiny spoon. "Better together, mae," he said softly. "And right now, you've got nobody. So now you've got us."

Under his straw hat, Don Café smiled a slow, proud, grandfatherly smile. "Then down we go," he said. "Hold your noses high, little ones. Kindness first. Quetzals second."

---

Going down was its own adventure, because the baby coatí could not smell her way home — but it turned out she could smell *Don Café* perfectly.

"You smell like warm mornings," she said, trotting close behind him with her nose down. "It's the best smell in the whole niebla. I'm going to follow it forever."

"Most people do," said Don Café modestly.

So they went: an old coffee bean leading the way by scent, a baby coatí following the coffee bean, a black bean rolling beside her saying calm and steadying things, and a grain of rice hopping in front shouting helpful warnings like "ROOT!" and "PUDDLE!" and "DIFFERENT ROOT!"

Once, the fog tickled the baby's nose and she sneezed — a sneeze so mighty it blew Gallo clean off the trail and into a fern.

"I'm okay," came his small voice from inside the fern. "Bless you. Please never do that again."

They called for her mamá as they went. The baby called loudest of all, braver every time, because it is much easier to be brave when somebody kind is walking beside you.

And then, from below, an answer — a sharp, joyful chittering — and out of the silver fog burst a whole family of coatís, tails up like a parade of stripey flags, the mamá at the front of them all.

What happened next was mostly squeaking, nuzzling, fur, and happiness. The baby vanished into her family like a raindrop returning to a river.

It was exactly then, of course, that the sun cleared the ridge.

---

The gold came pouring down through the cloud forest all at once, lighting every hanging dewdrop, and far, far above them, the Misty Window lookout glowed like a little green lamp at the top of the world.

A lookout that was very high up. And very beautiful. And very much *not where they were standing*.

Gallo sat down on a pebble. His specks didn't twinkle at all.

"We missed him," he said quietly. "First light came, and we're at the bottom, and the quetzal greeted the dawn without us." He sniffed. "I'm happy for the baby. I'm *really* happy for the baby. But I'm sad for us too. Is that allowed? Both at once?"

Don Café sat down beside him, slow and creaky and warm.

"It is more than allowed, mae," he said. "It is the truth. We chose the better thing, and it still cost us something — that is what made it a real choice. If kindness were free, everyone would buy two." He patted Gallo's hat. "Be sad for the quetzal. Be proud of yourself. A heart is big enough to hold both, with room left over for breakfast."

Gallo sniffed again, and nodded, and felt the tiniest bit better — sad, but the clean kind of sad, the kind that doesn't leave a stain.

That was when the mamá coatí walked over and looked at the three of them for a long, thoughtful moment.

"You gave up your morning for my baby," she said. "Coatís do not forget that. Tell me, travelers — what were you climbing to see?"

"The quetzal," said Pinto. "At the Misty Window. We missed him."

The mamá coatí's whiskers twitched, and her eyes went bright with a secret.

"The Window," she said, "is where the quetzal *greets* the morning. But we coatís know every breakfast tree on this montaña — and we know where he *eats* it. Follow me. We owe you a kindness, and we coatís pay our debts."

---

She led them along a hidden coatí path, a tunnel through the ferns no map had ever met, to a quiet hollow where one tree stood apart from the rest — a tree hung all over with **aguacatillos** (ah-gwah-kah-TEE-yohs) — tiny wild avocados, each one no bigger than a grape.

"His favorite," whispered the mamá. "Every morning, after the dawn, he comes here. Now hush. Hearts quiet."

They hushed. They quieted. Even Gallo, who one hour ago could not have held still if you'd glued him, held perfectly still.

And then — a sound. A clear, soft *keow... keow...* — like a tiny silver bell wrapped in velvet.

Through the golden mist he came: the resplendent quetzal, gliding down to a low branch barely an arm's length above their heads. Closer than any lookout. Closer than any telescope. So close they could see every feather. His chest was the deep red of a ripe coffee cherry. His back shimmered green-gold, every color the forest had ever dreamed of. And his tail streamed down behind him like two long ribbons of green river-water, swaying gently in the morning breeze.

He plucked an aguacatillo and swallowed it whole.

"He didn't even *chew*," Gallo breathed, scandalized and delighted. "Hum!"

The quetzal turned his head and looked down at them — at the tired round bean, the toasty grain, the old farmer with the kind eyes, and the coatí family gathered behind them — and gave one slow, friendly blink, as if to say: *I heard what you did this morning. The forest hears everything.*

Then he lifted away into the high green hush, trailing his ribbons of river behind him.

"We didn't climb to the quetzal," said Don Café softly, tipping back his hat. "We were kind — and the quetzal came down to us. Remember this, little ones. Chase a wonder and it flies. Help somebody, and sometimes the wonder lands on the branch right above your head."

---

The coatí family walked them partway back, and the baby hugged each of them goodbye — gently, and without nibbling anyone, which Pinto appreciated.

Then the three friends found a mossy ledge in the warm new sun, because climbing down could wait, and naps could not.

"Best morning ever," Gallo yawned. "We lost the quetzal and found him anyway. We rescued a baby. I got sneezed into a fern. Ten out of ten, hum." His eyes were already closing. "I'm sleepy now. The good kind. The earned kind."

"The very best kind," agreed Don Café, tipping his straw hat down over his eyes.

Pinto tucked his wooden spoon away and leaned, contented, against the warm flank of the old coffee bean. Gallo curled up against Pinto. And the mist came back soft and slow, like a blanket the montaña pulled up over all of them.

And the old mountain held them gently, the way mountains have held small kind travelers since the very first morning of the world.

## Goodnight Blessing
May you always have the courage to stop for the small lost ones, even when something shiny is waiting at the top. May your sad-and-proud feelings sit peacefully side by side, and may the wonders you give up come winging back to find you, closer and brighter than before. Wherever your montaña is, little one, climb it with a kind heart — that is the only map you need. Snuggle in now, soft as cloud-forest moss. Pura Vida, dear one. Goodnight.
