# Story 36: Pan Bon and the Christmas Eve Mix-Up

**Main Character:** Pan Bon the Caribbean Sweet Bread
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A cozy Caribbean-coast home on Christmas Eve, with the oven warm and gifts waiting
**Theme:** Owning up to a mistake mends things faster than hiding it.
**Spanish Spotlight:** perdon (pehr-DOHN) = sorry / pardon

---

Pan Bon could not hold still, and frankly, neither could his raisins.

He was a round, glossy loaf of Caribbean sweet bread, golden-brown on top and freckled all over with plump dark raisins that jiggled whenever he got excited — which, on this particular Christmas Eve, was approximately always. His crust shone with a syrupy glaze, his middle was warm and spiced with ginger and cinnamon, and tucked into his crumb were little jewels of candied papaya that caught the lamplight like tiny stained-glass windows. He smelled, if you must know, like the best hug you have ever sniffed.

"Tonight is the night!" Pan Bon sang, bouncing across the kitchen table so hard that two raisins very nearly launched into orbit. Outside, the Caribbean rain pattered warm and friendly on the tin roof. Inside, the oven glowed, the lights twinkled, and on the long wooden table sat a tray of beautiful gift-bundles, each wrapped in banana leaf and tied with string — each one labeled with a careful little name tag.

This is where, dear reader, things go marvelously sideways.

---

You see, the whole **familia** (fah-MEE-lyah) = family was coming for **Nochebuena** (noh-cheh-BWEH-nah) = Christmas Eve, and Abuela had spent all afternoon wrapping presents. There was a bundle for Tío Beto. One for the twins, Lola and Lalo. One for little Cousin Mango, who was three and bit things. Each tag was written in Abuela's loopy handwriting, and each bundle was placed *just so* on the tray.

"Don't touch the tray, Pan Bon," Abuela had said, wagging a flour-dusted finger before going to stir the rice. "Those are sorted perfect."

"I would never," said Pan Bon, who absolutely meant it at the time.

But oh, the tray was so *shiny*. And the bows were so *bouncy*. And Pan Bon was so very full of Christmas that his whole crust felt like a shaken soda. He leaned in for just one teeny sniff of Cousin Mango's bundle — surely a sniff was allowed? — and his round little bottom bumped the tray, which slid, which tipped, which —

*FLUMP-a-tumpa-THUMP.*

Every single bundle tumbled to the floor in a heap. Banana leaves slid. String tangled. And worst of all — *oh no, oh raisins* — every name tag popped off and scattered like startled fireflies.

Pan Bon froze. The tags lay everywhere. Whose was whose? He had not the foggiest, freckliest idea.

"It's fine," he whispered, hopping down. "I'll just fix it before anybody — yes! Quick and quiet. Nobody needs to know."

This was Pan Bon's first mistake. Well, second. The tray was the first. But you take my meaning.

---

He grabbed a tag that said *Tío Beto* and slapped it onto a bundle. Was it Tío Beto's? He had no clue. But it looked very Beto-ish — big, square, a little grumpy in the corners — so he nodded firmly and moved on. He stuck *Lola* on a lumpy one, *Lalo* on a flat one, and *Cousin Mango* on the smallest, because Mango was the smallest, and that seemed like math. Pan Bon was rather proud of the math, right up until he realized he had two tags left over and one bundle too few, which is the kind of arithmetic that makes a sweet bread feel distinctly unsweet.

"Okay. Okay okay okay," he muttered, raisins quivering. "I'll just start over. Calm. Professional. Like a postman."

But the more he sorted, the worse it got. The string had knotted itself into a creature that seemed almost alive — it had loops for ears and a tail and everything. A tag fell off and stuck to his own glaze. He peeled it free, which un-stuck a different tag, which floated down and landed perfectly wrong on yet another bundle, like the whole tray was playing a prank on him. He chased a runaway tag, tripped over Reynaldo-the-string-creature (though he had not yet named it that), lost his balance, and rolled *splat* into the whole pile, scattering everything *again*.

"PERDON!" he squeaked to nobody, then clapped a raisin-studded hand over his mouth.

By now Pan Bon was sweating glaze. He had retied bundles so wrong that one was shaped like a duck. He had matched tags so badly that Cousin Mango — who was three — now appeared to be receiving a present labeled in a way that suggested it weighed more than Cousin Mango. The tray was a disaster, the bundles were a mystery, and the **regalos** (reh-GAH-lohs) = gifts were nobody's and everybody's all at once.

Then he heard it. Footsteps. Laughter. The squeak of the screen door.

The familia had arrived.

---

Pan Bon dove behind the sugar jar.

In came everyone, shaking off warm rain, smelling of the night sea and somebody's cologne. Tío Beto boomed a hello. The twins thundered. Cousin Mango immediately tried to eat a Christmas ornament. And Abuela went to the table, clapped her hands, and announced, "¡Los regalos! Come, come, find the one with your name!"

There was a pause.

"...Abuela," said Lola slowly, holding up the duck-shaped bundle, "why does mine look like a *pato*?"

"Why is Mango's bigger than Mango?" said Lalo.

"This tag is stuck to *this* tag," said Tío Beto, untangling string. "And both of them say my name. Am I getting two? I accept."

Abuela's eyebrows climbed up, up, up. She looked at the tray. She looked at the duck. She looked around the room with the particular gaze of a grandmother who senses a culprit the way a shark senses a swimmer.

Behind the sugar jar, Pan Bon's raisins trembled.

*Say nothing,* whispered the fearful voice in his crumb. *Stay hidden. Maybe they'll think it was the rain. Maybe they'll think it was the duck.*

But here is the thing about hiding, dear reader. It is exhausting. Pan Bon's tummy ached. His glaze had gone all cold and tight. And the longer he crouched there, the more the lovely warm room felt far away, like he was watching Christmas through a window instead of standing inside it. He could hear the carols. He could smell the rice and the roast and the sweet of his own cousins in the basket. But he couldn't *be* in it — not really — because a secret was sitting in his crumb like a stone, and stones do not let you float.

Every confused face out there was confused because of *him*, and pretending otherwise didn't make it untrue — it just made him lonely on top of guilty, which is a truly terrible recipe. And worse: if he stayed quiet, the night would keep getting more tangled, and somebody else might get blamed, and *that* thought made his raisins go cold all the way through. A mess you hide, he realized, doesn't shrink in the dark. It grows.

He took a deep, spicy breath. Cinnamon and courage. And he rolled out from behind the sugar jar into the middle of the floor.

"PERDON," said Pan Bon, loud and shaky and brave. "It was me. I bumped the tray. I knocked all the regalos down and mixed up every single tag, and then I tried to fix it secret-like and made it a hundred times worse, and now there's a duck, and I am *so* sorry. It's all my fault."

The whole room went quiet.

Pan Bon squeezed his eyes shut and waited for the worst.

---

What he got instead was a *snort*.

It was Tío Beto. He was trying *very* hard not to laugh and failing *very* spectacularly. "A *duck*," he wheezed. "He made my present a duck."

Then Lola giggled. Then Lalo. Then Cousin Mango laughed too, even though Cousin Mango had no idea why, because that is what three-year-olds do, bless them. And then Abuela — wonderful Abuela — knelt down to Pan Bon's level, her eyes crinkling warm as the oven.

"Mijo," she said, "you told the truth. And you said *perdon* like you meant it." She tapped his glossy crust gently. "*That* is the hardest thing in the whole world to do, and you did it. So." She stood and clapped. "We have a beautiful problem, ¿no? Nobody knows whose gift is whose!"

"I'm sorry," Pan Bon mumbled again.

"No, no. Listen." Abuela grinned, and it was a mischievous grin, the kind that runs in families. "We make it a *game*. Everybody picks a bundle. You open it. If it fits you — it's yours! If it doesn't — you trade, you guess, you laugh!"

And oh, reader, what a game it was.

Tío Beto opened the duck bundle and found tiny pink hair clips meant for the twins, and wore them anyway, all over his beard, and looked *fabulous*. Lola got a fishing lure and pretended to fish for Cousin Mango. Cousin Mango got a giant soft blanket — far too big for someone the size of a kettle — and disappeared completely inside it, giggling like a haunted laundry pile. Every wrong guess was funnier than the last. The string-creature was crowned "Christmas Pet" and given a name (Reynaldo). Even the rain on the roof seemed to be laughing.

And nobody — not once — was anything but delighted.

Pan Bon sat in the middle of it all, warm and glazed and forgiven, his raisins jiggling with joy instead of fear. He had been so sure that telling the truth would spoil Christmas. Instead, telling the truth had *made* it — turned an ordinary Nochebuena into the silliest, sweetest, most-remembered one in the whole family's history.

Later, when the bundles were all sorted and the laughter had gone soft and sleepy, Abuela lifted Pan Bon onto the table beside the warm bread basket and kissed his crust.

"The best gift tonight," she whispered, "wasn't in any banana leaf. It was one little brave word."

And do you know what? She was right.

Because *perdon* — said honest and out loud — turned out to be the warmest present of all.

## Goodnight Blessing

Sleep now, little one, soft as fresh bread cooling on the sill. If today you made a tangle — a spill, a slip, a tiny wrong — know that a brave and gentle *perdon* will always mend it faster than hiding ever could. You are loved exactly as you are, raisins, mistakes, and all. Let the warm rain hush you, let the oven-glow keep you, and let your dreams be sweet and spiced and silly. Goodnight.
