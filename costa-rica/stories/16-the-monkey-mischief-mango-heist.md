# Story 16: The Monkey-Mischief Mango Heist

**Main Character:** Tico the Toucan
**Guest Characters:** Slowy the Sloth
**Setting:** A jungle treetop fruit market
**Theme:** Playful friends can make mischief right with a clever, kind plan.
**Spanish Spotlight:** **mono** (MOH-noh) = monkey

---

High up in the dripping green ceiling of the rainforest, where the leaves were as big as umbrellas and the morning smelled like rain and ripe everything, there was a market. Not a market on the ground — oh no. This market hung in the treetops, swinging on vines, with little stalls tucked into branches and a creaky rope bridge between them all.

And the loudest thing in the whole market was a bird.

"BEAK THE NEWS! BEAK THE NEWS!" boomed Tico the Toucan, flapping from branch to branch with his enormous rainbow-striped beak pointed at the sky like a banana announcing the weather. "Fresh papayas at Stall Three! Slowy the Sloth has reached the rope bridge — he will arrive sometime around NEXT TUESDAY! And the mangoes — oh, the MANGOES — golden, juicy, and the pride of the entire jungle!"

That last part was the most important news of all.

Because the mangoes were disappearing.

---

It had started small. One mango here. Two mangoes there. But this morning, when Tico swooped down to count the great Mango Mountain at the center of the market, he nearly dropped his own beak.

"Half!" he squawked. "HALF the mangoes — GONE! Vanished! Poof! This is the biggest news since the time a coconut sneezed!"

(A coconut had never sneezed. Tico just liked saying things.)

The market animals gathered round, blinking and worried. Mangoes were everyone's favorite. The parrots used them for breakfast, the frogs used them for hats, and Estrella the Sea Turtle — when she visited from the coast — said a jungle mango was "the closest thing to sunshine you can hold in your flippers."

"Who would take them?" whispered a tiny frog.

"Maybe a ghost," gulped a parrot. "A mango ghost. A *mghost.*"

"It is NOT a mghost," said Tico firmly, though privately he made a note to investigate mghosts later, because that would be excellent news indeed. "There is always an explanation. And I shall FIND it, because finding things out is my whole entire job!"

He puffed up his bright yellow chest so proudly that he overbalanced, tipped clean off his branch, and landed beak-first in a soft papaya at Stall Three. He came up dripping, a slice of papaya on his head like a small orange hat, and announced through a mouthful, "Investigation has BEGUN!"

That was the question — well, after the papaya. And Tico, being the town crier, decided he would solve it. He flew to the slowest, wisest creature he knew.

"SLOWY!" he announced, landing on a branch beside a fuzzy, mossy-furred sloth who was hanging upside down, smiling at nothing in particular. "We have a MYSTERY! A heist! A caper! Half the mangoes are GONE!"

Slowy blinked. Very slowly. The way a sunset closes its eyes.

"Puuura... viiida...," he said. "Howww... excitiiing..."

Then Slowy lifted one long, slow arm and pointed — verrry gradually — toward the top of the tallest tree.

Tico looked.

And there, peeking through the leaves, were three pairs of bright, twinkling, very guilty-looking eyes.

---

They were monkeys. Capuchin **monos** (MOH-noh = monkeys) — little fellows with cream-colored faces, big curious eyes, and tails that curled like question marks. And every single one of them was clutching a mango. One of them had a mango balanced on his head. One was hugging two mangoes like beloved pillows. And the third had somehow stuffed a mango into each cheek and was grinning like a furry, lumpy balloon.

"AHA!" Tico shouted, flapping up to them in a swirl of rainbow feathers. "MANGO THIEVES! Caught beak-handed! This is BREAKING NEWS!"

The three monos froze. Then they did the worst possible thing a guilty creature can do.

They giggled.

"Hee hee hee!" went the first.

"Hoo hoo hoo!" went the second.

The third tried to giggle but his cheeks were too full of mango, so he just made a noise like *mmmpfh* and a little squirt of mango juice shot out of his nose, which made all three of them laugh so hard they nearly fell out of the tree.

Tico opened his beak to scold them. He really did. He had a whole scolding ready — a *good* one, with lots of important words in it.

But it's very hard to stay cross at someone who has just sneezed mango out of their nose.

---

Slowy, who had finally arrived (it took him the length of a short nap), hung beside Tico and gazed at the giggling monkeys with his sleepy half-moon eyes.

"They... aren't... mean...," Slowy said slowly. "They're... just... hungryyy... and... playfulll..."

Tico tilted his head. He looked closer.

The monos weren't snarling. They weren't sneaky-sneaky. They were *playing*. They tossed mangoes back and forth, juggling, laughing, sharing bites. The truth was, the little monkeys lived high at the tippy-top of the canopy where the fruit was small and sour. They had smelled the market's beautiful golden mangoes for weeks. And finally, one brave little mono had reached down and *swiped* one — and it was so delicious, and so *fun* to grab, that they simply hadn't stopped.

They didn't know it was wrong. Nobody had ever invited them down. Nobody had ever invited them to *anything*.

"You see," Slowy went on, taking roughly the time of a gentle rain shower to finish his sentence, "up... theeere... it's... lonelyyy... and... they... can... heeear... the laughing... down here... and... they... just... wanted... to... be... part... of... ittt."

It was the longest speech Slowy had given all year, and by the end of it Tico had grown a much softer heart.

He flew up for a closer look. The first little mono had a chip in one ear and a face so hopeful it could have melted caramel. The second wore a crown of leaves she'd made herself, clearly very proud of it. And the third — the cheek-stuffer — was now trying to share his mango with a passing beetle, holding it out politely even though the beetle was far too small and slightly alarmed.

These were not villains. These were just three little ones who had wandered down looking for sweetness, and found a whole market full of it, and hadn't known how to ask.

Tico's big heart squeezed inside his glossy chest.

"Hmm," he said. Which, for Tico, was very quiet indeed.

---

Now, an angry bird might have chased the monkeys off. He might have built a fence of thorns, or posted a guard, or shouted, "STAY AWAY FROM OUR MANGOES!" until his throat went hoarse.

But Tico was not an angry bird. Tico was a *clever* bird. And he had just had an idea so big and so bright that it practically had its own sunrise.

"SLOWY," he whispered (which was still quite loud), "I am going to make this mischief RIGHT. Not with a fence. Not with a fight. With a FIESTA!"

Slowy smiled, slow and warm. "Puuura... viiida...," he said, which is sloth for *I love it, but I will be helping at sloth speed.*

So Tico got to work. And when a toucan gets to work, the whole jungle hears about it.

"BEAK THE NEWS!" he cried, zooming through the market. "TONIGHT — the FIRST EVER — GREAT JUNGLE FRUIT FIESTA! Everyone is invited! Bring fruit to SHARE! Mangoes, papayas, guavas, bananas — ¡todos! And our special guests of honor are — " he paused dramatically " — the MONOS of the High Canopy!"

The market buzzed. The monos, peeking down from above, blinked in surprise. Guests of honor? *Them?*

There was, of course, a great deal to do, and Tico did most of it at the speed of a feathery hurricane. He delegated busily. "Parrots — you string the fireflies! Frogs — you arrange the leaf mats, and NO sitting on the snacks! Slowy — " Tico paused, then said gently, "Slowy, you are in charge of one very important leaf. Take all the time you need."

Slowy beamed. A single leaf was exactly his kind of responsibility.

Then Tico flew straight up, up, up to the High Canopy where the monos lived, and landed — slightly out of breath, because it was a very long way — on their branch.

"Friends," he said, more softly than the jungle had ever heard him speak. "I know you've been taking the mangoes. But I'm not here to scold. I'm here to *invite*. Tonight there's a fiesta. And it isn't a fiesta without you. Will you come?"

The three monos looked at each other. They had never been invited anywhere. They had only ever sneaked. And sneaking, it turned out, was much lonelier than it looked.

The little one with the chipped ear swallowed hard.

"You're... not... mad?" he squeaked.

"My dear mono," said Tico, "the only thing I am, is excited. And I am ALWAYS excited. Ask anyone."

---

That evening, the treetop market glowed.

Fireflies hung in the branches like tiny lanterns. Big banana leaves were laid out as picnic mats. And in the very center, where Mango Mountain used to stand, Tico built something new — a giant **Fruit-Sharing Tree**, where every animal hung up the fruit they'd brought so that *everyone* could pick whatever they liked.

The parrots brought guavas. The frogs brought berries (and wore mango hats, naturally). Slowy brought one single leaf, which had taken him all day to carry, and everyone clapped for him anyway because that's what friends do.

And then, shy and quiet for the very first time, the three little monos climbed down.

The whole market went hush.

The bravest mono — the one who'd swiped the very first mango — held out his arms. And in them were mangoes. The stolen ones. All of them, polished shiny on his fur, brought back to share.

"We... bring back," he said softly, in monkey-squeak. "We share now. Sorry for the heist."

Tico's eyes twinkled like wet stones in sunlight. He flew down and laid one wing gently on the little mono's shoulder.

"My friend," he announced — but warmly, the way you announce good news to someone you love — "there is only ONE rule at the Fruit-Sharing Tree. You must take a mango. And you must take it HONESTLY, with a friend, in the open, in the giggly golden evening. Can you do that?"

The mono's whole face lit up like the fireflies.

"Hee hee — ¡SÍ!" he cried.

And the fiesta began.

---

Oh, what a fiesta it was.

The monos turned out to be the best party guests in the entire rainforest. They juggled fruit. They did flips off the vines. They taught the frogs a hopping dance and taught the parrots a song that was mostly just the word "MANGO" sung in different funny voices. One mono balanced six guavas on his tail and walked across the rope bridge without dropping a single one, and Tico shouted "BEAK THE NEWS! A NEW WORLD RECORD!" so loudly that a faraway owl woke up and complained.

Slowy laughed too — a long, slow, rumbly chuckle that started at dinnertime and was still going gently at bedtime.

And the best part? With everyone sharing instead of hoarding, there was *more* fruit than ever. The monos showed the market a secret grove high in the canopy where rare star-fruit grew, and they brought baskets of it down to add to the tree. Sharing, it turned out, made the whole jungle sweeter.

By the time the moon climbed up over the leaves, full and silver and round as a peeled lychee, every belly in the treetop market was full and every heart was fuller.

The little mono curled up beside Tico, sticky and happy, with a half-eaten mango in his paw.

"Best heist ever," he yawned.

"It wasn't a heist," Tico whispered, tucking a wing around him. "It was the start of being friends. And THAT, little mono, is the sweetest news I've ever told."

Above them, Slowy was already asleep, smiling, swaying gently in the warm night breeze.

And the mangoes — the golden, glowing pride of the jungle — were never stolen again.

Because you don't have to steal what's already shared with love.
