# Story 5: Don Café and the Slow Sunrise

**Main Character:** Don Café
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A misty hillside coffee farm at first light
**Theme:** Good things take time, and slow can be its own kind of magic.
**Spanish Spotlight:** **familia** (fah-MEE-lyah) = family

---

High on a misty hillside, where the clouds liked to nap before breakfast, lived a wise old coffee bean named Don Café. He was dark and glossy, with a deep crease right down his middle that looked exactly like a thoughtful little frown. He wore a tiny straw farmer's hat, carried a teensy wooden walking stick, and smelled — well, he smelled like the best part of waking up.

This particular morning, the hillside was so foggy you could spread it on toast. And from somewhere inside the misty coffee bushes came a tiny, impatient voice.

"Faster! Come ON, sunrise! I haven't got all day!"

Don Café chuckled, a slow warm rumble. "Now who," he said, tapping his walking stick, "is shouting at the sun before the sun has even had its coffee?"

A small green coffee bean came bouncing out of the mist, jittery as a popcorn kernel in a hot pan.

---

"It's me! Beanito!" the little bean announced. He was bright green, barely roasted, round and unripe, and he zipped back and forth so fast he left tiny trails in the fog. "I want to be famous coffee TODAY. Right now. This second. Maybe sooner!"

Don Café smiled under his straw hat. "Ah," he said. "Good things brew slowly, little one."

"Slowly?" Beanito gasped, as though Don Café had said something truly terrible, like *broccoli for dessert*. "Slowly is the worst word in the whole world! I want to be roasted, and ground, and poured into a steaming cup, and I want to wake up the whole town — and I want it FAST."

"And you shall," said Don Café kindly. "But first, walk with me. The farm is waking up, and there is no better thing to watch in all the world."

Beanito groaned the groan of someone who has been asked to watch paint dry. But he followed, because secretly, he liked the warm smell that floated around the old bean wherever he went.

They climbed a little path between the coffee bushes, the fog curling around them like a soft gray blanket. Don Café walked the way he did everything — slowly, with little proverbs tucked between each step.

"Listen," said Don Café, stopping. "Hear that?"

Beanito listened. *Drip. Drip-drip.* "That's just dew," he said. "Boring water."

"That," said Don Café, "is the mountain making breakfast for every root on the hillside. One drop at a time. The mountain is never in a hurry, and yet — somehow — everything still gets fed." He gave a wise little nod. "Slow is not the same as lazy, you know. Slow is just *careful taking its time*."

Beanito wiggled. "But I'm READY now. Look how round I am! Look how bouncy!" He bounced to prove it and accidentally rolled into a puddle with a tiny *plip*.

Don Café fished him out with the curve of his walking stick. "You are wonderfully round," he agreed, setting Beanito gently on a leaf. "But you are still green, mae. A green bean is sour and hard. Roast you now, and you'd make the grumpiest cup of coffee anyone ever tasted. The whole town would wake up scowling!" He laughed his slow, warm laugh. "Patience is not waiting for nothing. Patience is letting something become its very best."

Beanito did not want to admit it, but the idea of being a *grumpy* cup of coffee horrified him almost as much as the word *slowly*.

---

Just then, the very first thread of sunlight slid over the top of the mountain. It was thin and pink and shy, like the sky was peeking around a curtain.

"Watch," whispered Don Café. "This is the part I love."

And here is the funny thing about a sunrise on a coffee farm: it does not arrive all at once, like someone flipping a light switch. It arrives one warm sip at a time.

First, the pink light touched the highest leaves, and the dew there sparkled awake. Then it slid lower, warming a sleepy beetle who yawned and stretched all six of his legs (which takes a beetle a surprisingly long time). Then lower still, until a whole family of round red coffee cherries on the next bush over began to glow like little lanterns.

"Buenos días, Don Café!" they called sleepily.

"Buenos días, familia," he called back. He turned to Beanito and tapped the word gently with his stick. "**Familia** — that means *family*. And on this hillside, every single bean is part of the same one. The roots, the cherries, the dew, the old beans, the green beans. Even the impatient ones." He winked. "*Especially* the impatient ones."

Beanito felt something warm spread through him that was not entirely the sunrise.

"Now," said Don Café, "you wanted to wake up the whole town. Let me show you how it truly happens."

---

They walked to the very edge of the hillside, where the farm looked down over the little town of tin roofs and red doors just beginning to glow in the morning light. And one by one, Don Café showed Beanito the long, slow, wonderful journey of a morning.

"See that house with the blue shutters? In a moment, an *abuela* — a grandmother — will shuffle to her kitchen. She will not rush. She will scoop the ground coffee into a cloth sock called a **chorreador** (cho-rray-ah-DOR) = a coffee sock-dripper, and she will pour the hot water slowly, slowly, so the coffee drips down golden-brown."

Beanito watched. Sure enough, a little curl of steam rose from the blue-shuttered house.

"It takes time," said Don Café softly. "And while it drips, the whole house begins to smell like morning. The children stir. The dog thumps his tail. The grandfather, who is very stubborn and very sleepy, finally — *finally* — opens one eye." He chuckled. "All because of one slow, careful cup. *That* is how you wake up a town, little one. Not fast. Warmly."

Beanito stared down at the town. He had imagined himself bursting onto the scene like a firecracker — *BANG, good morning, everyone!* But this was different. This was a smell drifting under doors. This was warmth seeping into cold kitchens. This was the whole **familia** of the valley waking up not with a shock, but with a hug.

"Oh," said Beanito quietly. "That's... actually kind of magic."

"The slow kind," agreed Don Café. "The very best kind."

---

But it was here that the gentle problem of the morning popped up — as problems sometimes do, right when everyone is feeling cozy.

For Beanito, in all his bouncing earlier, had bounced a little too hard. He had knocked loose a pebble, and the pebble had rolled, and now it was wedged in the wooden gutter that carried the mountain's dew down to the thirsty roots. The drips had stopped. The whole careful breakfast of the hillside had hit a snag.

"Oh no," said Beanito, his green going a shade paler. "I did that. I was rushing and I — oh no, the **familia** won't get their water!"

"Then," said Don Café calmly, not hurrying even a little, "we shall fix it. Together. *Slowly.*"

"But we have to fix it FA—" Beanito caught himself. He took a breath. "...We have to fix it *carefully*," he said instead.

Don Café's eyes crinkled with pride.

They worked as a team. Don Café could not bounce, but he was steady and strong, and he braced his walking stick against the pebble. Beanito could not push hard, but he was small and round and *exactly* pebble-sized. He rolled himself into the gutter, wedged behind the stone, and — taking his time, breathing slow — he nudged. And nudged. And nudged some more.

"*Despacio*," said Don Café. "Slowly. Slowly does it."

With one last patient push, the pebble popped free and tumbled harmlessly into the grass. And the dew began to drip again — *drip, drip-drip* — feeding every root on the hillside, right on schedule, which on a mountain is no schedule at all.

Beanito flopped back, breathing hard and grinning. "We did it! And we did it... slow."

"You did it," said Don Café warmly. "And the whole valley will have its morning, all because a small green bean learned to take his time."

---

The sun was higher now, golden and full, warming the two beans where they sat side by side on the hillside. Down in the town, a dozen curls of steam rose from a dozen kitchens, and you could almost hear the whole valley sighing its first happy sip of the day.

Beanito leaned against the old bean's side. He did not feel quite so impatient anymore. He felt, instead, like part of something big and warm and slow and wonderful — like part of a **familia**.

"Don Café?" he said. "I think I can wait now. To be the best coffee I can be. Even if it takes a while."

Don Café tucked his walking stick under his arm and pulled his straw hat down against the sun. "That, little one," he said, "is the wisest thing a bean can say. Good things brew slowly." He smiled. "And the morning? The morning always comes — one warm sip at a time."

And up on the misty hillside, with the whole valley glowing gold below them, the two friends watched the slowest, most magical sunrise in all of Costa Rica — together, and in no hurry at all.
