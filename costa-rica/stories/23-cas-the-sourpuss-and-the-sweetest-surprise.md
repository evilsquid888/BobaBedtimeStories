# Story 23: Cas the Sourpuss and the Sweetest Surprise

**Main Character:** Cas the Sour Guava
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A sunny fruit-stand corner of Pura Vida Town where fresco drinks are blended
**Theme:** Being a little different (even a little sour) is exactly what makes you special.
**Spanish Spotlight:** fresco (FRES-koh) = a fresh blended fruit drink

---

At the sunniest corner of Pura Vida Town, where the morning light bounced off mango skins like spilled gold, there lived a small green guava named Cas who was absolutely, positively certain that no one would ever, ever choose him.

You see, Cas was sour. Not a little tart. Not gently zingy. *Sour.* So puckery that one nibble of him could fold a person's whole face inward like a closing umbrella. He was bumpy and pebble-green, no bigger than a child's fist, with a thin blush of yellow at his belly and three pale freckles near his stem that he was secretly rather fond of, though he'd never admit it.

"Sweet fruits get sipped," Cas grumbled, wedging himself behind the bananas. "Sour fruits get *forgotten.*"

And with that, he tugged a leaf over his head like a tiny grumpy blanket and waited to be ignored.

The fruit stand belonged to a fizzy, cheerful corner of town where every afternoon the big glass blender pitcher whirred and gurgled, turning fruit into the most beloved drink for miles around — a **fresco** (FRES-koh) = a fresh blended fruit drink. Children pressed their noses to the counter. Coins clinked. Straws appeared from nowhere. And one by one, the sweet fruits were lifted, cheered, and tumbled happily into the pitcher.

"Mango! Mango first!" sang Mango, who was so ripe she practically glowed, doing a little wiggle as she was scooped up. "Try not to miss me too much, Cas!"

"I'll manage," muttered Cas, sinking lower.

Banana went next, then plump sweet pineapple, then a fat handful of strawberries who all giggled at once like a class on a field trip. Every single one of them was sweet. Every single one of them got chosen. And Cas, small and sour and squashed at the very back of the basket, watched the spot of sunlight crawl across the counter and told himself it didn't sting.

(It stung a little. But guavas are proud, and Cas would have rather been blended than admit it.)

"Why even hope," he sighed to a passing ant. "A drink is supposed to be sweet. I'm the opposite of the whole point."

The ant, who had places to be, did not answer.

This had been Cas's whole life, you understand. Every morning, the fruit stand woke up in a bustle of cheerful noise. The pineapples practiced their songs. The strawberries argued about who was reddest. Mango did her stretches and announced, to absolutely no one, that she was "feeling especially ripe today, thank you for asking." And Cas? Cas rolled himself to the darkest corner of the basket and tried to look like a small green rock, because rocks, at least, are never expected to be sweet.

He had tried, once, to be sweet. Years ago — well, weeks ago, but it felt like years — he had practiced smiling extra wide and humming a sugary little tune, hoping that if he *acted* sweet enough, somebody might forget he wasn't. A passing toucan had taken one curious peck.

The toucan's face had folded inward so dramatically that it flew off sideways into a hibiscus bush and refused to come out for an hour.

"See?" Cas had whispered to himself, mortified down to his stem. "Even a *bird* can't stand me."

After that, he'd stopped trying. Hiding was safer. Hiding didn't make anybody's face fold up. And so Cas the Sourpuss earned his grumpy nickname the way most grumpy nicknames are earned — not from a mean heart, but from a heart that had been disappointed one too many times and decided to stop hoping out loud.

Now, that particular afternoon was a sticky one. Not warm — *sticky.* The kind of hot where the air feels like warm honey and your own shadow seems too tired to follow you. The sun sat fat and yellow over Pura Vida Town and refused to budge, and everything, everywhere, grew sweeter and sweeter and *sweeter.*

The mangoes turned syrupy. The bananas went gooey. The pineapple got so sugary it nearly fell asleep mid-sentence. And when the blender pitcher whirred them all together that afternoon — *vrrrrrrrm!* — out came a **batido** (bah-TEE-doh) = a thick blended drink so sweet and so heavy that the first child who tried it scrunched up her nose.

"Bleh," she said politely, and set it down.

"Bleh?" gasped Mango from inside the pitcher. "Impossible! I am *delicious!*"

But it was true. The fresco was too much. Cloying, the grown-ups called it — a fancy word that means *so sweet your tongue wants to lie down and take a nap.* It coated your mouth like glue. One sip and you wanted a glass of water and a quiet sit.

More children tried. More children scrunched. A small boy took a sip, smacked his lips slowly, and announced with great seriousness, "It tastes like a candy that fell in love with another candy and then married a *third* candy." Which, when you think about it, is far too much candy for one drink.

The line of children shrank. The coins stopped clinking. The straws stayed in their cup, dry and disappointed. And the big glass pitcher, who took enormous pride in being the most beloved drink for miles, began to wobble with worry.

"Maybe more mango?" suggested Mango hopefully, because Mango believed the answer to most problems was simply more Mango.

"We *tried* more mango," groaned the pitcher. "We tried more everything. The sweeter I make it, the worse it gets. It's like adding blankets to someone who's already too hot." It sloshed miserably. "There must be a fruit somewhere — *somewhere* — that isn't like the rest of you."

"Something's wrong," fretted the pitcher, sloshing. "We're all sweet, but the drink is *flat.* It needs — oh, I don't know what it needs! Something bright! Something that wakes the tongue up! Something —"

"Something *different,*" said a small voice.

Everyone turned. There, at the very back of the basket, a leaf slid sideways. Out peeked a bumpy green guava with three pale freckles and a face braced for laughter.

"I'm sour," Cas said, lifting his chin even though his stem was trembling. "I'm the opposite of sweet. I'm probably the worst thing you could put in a drink." He swallowed. "But you said you needed bright. And I'm... well. I'm *very* bright."

There was a pause. The mangoes inside the pitcher exchanged a syrupy, doubtful look.

"He'll *ruin* it," whispered sleepy Pineapple. "He'll pucker the whole town!"

But the pitcher, who was wise the way old glass things often are, tilted thoughtfully toward Cas. "Bright," it murmured. "Yes. A splash of bright is *exactly* what's missing. Come here, little sourpuss. Let's find out."

Cas's heart did something strange. For the first time in his whole green life, somebody was reaching for him *first.*

He rolled himself to the edge of the basket, which was farther than he'd traveled in weeks, and looked down into the swirling golden pool. It was a long way for a small guava. His three freckles tingled. His belly-blush went pale.

"And if I ruin it?" he asked quietly. "What if I just make it sour and horrible and everyone's faces fold up like that poor toucan's?"

"Then we'll have learned something," said the pitcher gently. "But I don't think you will, little one. I've blended ten thousand drinks. I know the difference between a thing that's *wrong* and a thing that's just *different.* And you, Cas — you're the most splendidly different fruit I've ever met."

No one had ever called his difference *splendid* before. Cas turned the word over like a smooth stone in his palm.

He took a breath. He thought, *Here goes nothing,* which is a thing brave fruits think right before they do something enormous. And he hopped.

*Plip.*

In he went, one small tart guava among all that drowsy sweetness, and the blender gave a hopeful little cough and roared to life. *Vrrrrrrrm!* The fruit swirled — gold and pink and pineapple-yellow — and right in the middle of it all spun a thread of zingy, zippy, wide-awake green.

Out poured the new fresco. It was pale and pretty and dotted with tiny flecks of guava. The girl who had said "bleh" eyed it suspiciously. She took the smallest, most cautious sip.

Her eyes flew open.

"*Whoa,*" she breathed.

And then she drank the entire thing in one long, glorious, can't-stop-sipping gulp, the way you do with something so refreshing it practically high-fives your taste buds. "It's not too sweet anymore!" she cried. "It's — it's like a little burst of sunshine! It wakes your whole *mouth* up! ¡**Qué rico**! (keh REE-koh) = how delicious!"

"OTRO!" shouted the boy behind her, which means *another,* and suddenly the whole street was shouting it. *¡Otro! ¡Otro!* The line came roaring back. Coins clinked like rain. Straws appeared by the fistful. The pitcher whirred so happily it nearly danced off the counter.

And inside, swirling proudly among the dazzled mangoes, was Cas.

Even the toucan came back. It landed on the counter, eyed the pale guava-flecked drink with deep suspicion, took one careful sip — and let out a squawk of pure joy so loud it knocked a banana off the shelf. "MORE!" it screeched, which is the only word toucans say when something is truly excellent. It drank four whole frescos and then sat in the hibiscus bush singing, this time on purpose.

"It was *you,*" Mango admitted, gobsmacked. "All that sweetness, and the one thing it was missing was a little sour. The little *bright.* The little —"

"Different," finished Cas. And this time, when he said the word, it didn't taste like loneliness. It tasted like the best secret in the world.

Because here is the thing Cas finally understood, the thing he'd had backward his entire life: his sourness was never the flaw in the recipe. It was the *spark.* All that sweetness needed somebody who wasn't the same — somebody zingy and zippy and one-of-a-kind to wake the whole thing up. Without a little sour, sweet just sits there being sweet. With it? *Magic.*

Word spread the way good news always does in a small town — quickly, and with a great deal of exaggeration. By sundown people were saying the new fresco could cure a bad mood, untangle a knot, and make Tuesdays feel like Saturdays. None of that was quite true, but the part about the bad mood was *almost* true, because it is very hard to feel grumpy with a burst of bright sunshine on your tongue.

By evening, the fruit stand had a brand-new sign, hand-painted and lopsided and wonderful: **FRESCO DE CAS — THE BRIGHT ONE.** And every single afternoon after that, when the pitcher began to whir, it called for the sweet fruits, yes — but it always, *always* called for Cas first.

He never hid behind the bananas again.

(Well. Once. But only to surprise Mango. Guavas, it turns out, also have an excellent sense of humor — bright, just like everything else about them.)

## Goodnight Blessing

May you never be afraid of the way you are different, little one — for the world is full of sweet, and what it needs is *you.* May your zing be a gift, your spark a surprise, and your one-of-a-kind self exactly the splash of bright that someone, somewhere, has been hoping for all along. Snuggle in. You are not too much, and never too little. You are perfectly, deliciously you. Goodnight.
