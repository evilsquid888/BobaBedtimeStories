# Story 47: The Coast-to-Coast Relay

**Main Character:** Tato the Patacon
**Guest Characters:** Gallo, Pinto, Coco the Granizado, Don Café, Estrella the Sea Turtle
**Setting:** A grand relay race from the Caribbean coast to the Pacific
**Theme:** We go farther when we pass the joy along.
**Spanish Spotlight:** **camino** (kah-MEE-noh) — road or path

---

On the morning of the Great Coast-to-Coast Relay, Tato the Patacon was so excited that he forgot he was flat.

"I'm going to RUN!" he announced, and tried to leap into the air. Patacones, as you may know, are twice-fried smashed plantains, round and golden and a little flat-footed, and they are not built for leaping. Tato wobbled, tipped, and landed with a soft *flump* in the warm Caribbean sand, salt dusting off his crinkled edges.

"Smash now, worry never!" he said proudly, even though he had not smashed anything except his own dignity.

A great crowd of treats had gathered where the Caribbean Sea met the shore, all the way to where, three days away, the Pacific waited on the other side of the whole country. A long ribbon of road — a **camino** (kah-MEE-noh), a road or path — stretched from one sea to the other, winding through rainforest and past a sleepy volcano. And today, the friends would carry one small thing all the way across it.

A single, glowing seashell. The Joy Shell.

---

"Here are the rules, and they are very nice rules," called Tico the Toucan from a branch, flapping a tiny flag. "BEAK THE NEWS! Each friend runs ONE leg of the camino! At the end of your leg, you pass the Joy Shell to the next runner! Whoever is holding it when we reach the Pacific gets to ring the Sunset Bell! Pura Vida and GO!"

Tato was first. He held the Joy Shell against his crispy chest, and it hummed warm against him, glowing like a little caught sunrise.

"Don't drop it," whispered Flan the Coconut Flan from the crowd, wobbling. "Steady, steady... ooh, wobbly!"

"I will guard it with my whole flat life," Tato vowed. And off he went — *pat-pat-pat* — his flat feet slapping the sandy camino, salt flying off him like sparks.

The first leg ran along the steamy Caribbean coast, past coconut palms and red crabs who waved with both claws. Tato was doing wonderfully. He was doing so wonderfully that he started to think about how wonderfully he was doing, which is exactly when most accidents happen.

The path dipped. Tato's flat feet skidded. He went *fwoomp* down a muddy bank, rolled three times like a runaway coin, and landed at the bottom in a tangle of vines — but the Joy Shell was still held high above his head, safe.

"Ha!" Tato panted, mud on his crispy edges. "Smashed... but not dropped!"

And here is the secret Tato learned at the bottom of that muddy bank: he did not have to get up alone. A long, slow, fuzzy arm reached down through the vines.

"Puuura... viiida..." said Slowy the Sloth, who happened to be hanging from that very tree, as sloths so often happen to be. He pulled Tato up at the speed of dripping honey — which was very slow indeed, but very sure — and pointed one toe down the path.

"You're not... in this race?" Tato asked.

"I am... in EVERY... race," Slowy said dreamily. "I just... finish... laaater."

Tato laughed so hard a flake of golden crust fell off him. He picked it up, brushed it clean, and stuck it right back on. "Nothing keeps a patacon down," he reminded himself, and that was true, but he was learning something even better as he climbed back onto the camino: a flat little patacon could go a very long way, as long as he didn't have to go all of it by himself. Behind him, Slowy lifted one slow toe in farewell, a goodbye that would take most of the afternoon to finish.

---

Tato pat-pat-patted to the end of his leg, where Estrella the Sea Turtle waited at the edge of a wide river, her mossy-green star-shell shining.

"You carried it well, little patacon," she said, in a voice like slow kind waves. "Now follow the moon home — or in this case, follow the river. Climb on my shell."

Tato passed her the Joy Shell, and the very second he let go, something funny happened. He didn't feel emptier. He felt *lighter*, like he'd handed off not just a shell but a whole armful of worry. He laughed out loud.

"It's better when you pass it!" he said, surprised.

"Better together, mae," called Pinto the black bean from up ahead, lifting his tiny wooden spoon in salute. "That's the whole secret of the camino, ¿verdad?"

Estrella swam the river leg, the Joy Shell balanced on her star-shell, glowing across the water. Fish leapt alongside her just to see it. At the far bank, she lifted it gently onto the shore — and into the cool, frosty hands of Coco the Granizado.

"WHOA, now we're talking!" Coco shouted, rainbow shaved-ice mound sparkling, tiny sunglasses on. "Watch THIS, everybody! Stay frosty, stay sweet!"

---

Coco took off like a frosty cannonball, shaved-ice mound bouncing, syrup-stripes streaking rainbow behind him. For the first stretch he was magnificent. He zoomed past sleepy iguanas. He waved at a waterfall. He shouted "TUANIS!" so many times that a parrot learned the word and began shouting it too.

But Coco's leg ran uphill toward the volcano, and this was a problem, because Coco was made of ice, and the air was getting warm, and warm air is an ice friend's worst enemy.

"I'm — *pant* — melting a little — *pant* — but it's WORTH IT!" Coco cried, leaving a glittering trail of melty syrup-drips behind. Drip, drip, sparkle. Every drop steamed faintly on the warm stones.

By the time Coco reached the misty middle of the country, he had melted into a much smaller, much soggier mound. He held up the Joy Shell with one slushy hand.

"Okay," he wheezed, "I cannot — phew — feel my cone. Who's next? Tell me somebody cool is next."

"Good things brew slowly," came a warm, wonderful smell, and there stood Don Café, the roasted coffee bean, leaning on his tiny walking stick, straw hat tipped back. He was old, and he was slow, and he smelled like the best morning anyone had ever had.

"Don Café, no offense," Coco panted, "but this is a RACE. You walk like Slowy's grandpa."

Don Café chuckled, a deep brown rumble. "Watch an old bean, joven. The camino does not reward the fastest. It rewards the one who keeps going." He took the Joy Shell, tucked it under his arm, and began to walk.

---

And oh, how he walked. Up the volcano path, through the cool cloud-forest where the mist hung like soft gray blankets, Don Café did not run and did not rush. He hummed a slow song. He greeted every flower. He told a proverb to a startled frog. And he never, ever stopped.

The other friends, watching from below, were amazed to see that his steady little pace ate up the mountain bite by bite. Faster runners had tired and flopped. Coco had melted. But Don Café, slow as warm honey and twice as sure, crested the top of the volcano with the Joy Shell glowing under his arm, the whole green country spread out behind him like a rumpled blanket and both seas shining far away.

"You see," he called down to them all, with a wink, "the camino is long. So we share it. One bean cannot drink the whole pot." Steam from a faraway kitchen seemed to nod in agreement.

And just then, who should come puffing up the volcano path behind him but Coco — refrozen, refreshed, and grinning, having sat himself in a cold mountain stream until he was a proper tall sparkling mound again. "The mist froze me back together!" he crowed. "Good as new! Don't you DARE finish this parade without me!"

At the bottom of the volcano's far side, two small friends waited together, as they always were. Gallo the fried-rice grain in his little woven palm hat, and Pinto the black bean with his tiny spoon. The last leg. The leg to the Pacific.

"We'll take it from here, viejo," said Gallo warmly, taking the shell. "Rise and pinto!"

---

"BOTH of you?" asked Flan, who had hurried up to watch the finish. "But — but only one can hold it!"

Gallo and Pinto looked at each other and grinned the same grin.

"Who said only one?" said Pinto. "We never go anywhere alone."

So they ran the final leg side by side, the little rice grain and the little black bean, each holding one edge of the Joy Shell between them. Down through the dry golden forest of the Pacific side they ran, past howler monkeys and bright **flores** (FLOH-rehs) — flowers — with Cajeta the coconut-fudge ball bouncing alongside cheering "Sweet and round, watch me bound!" and Tato pat-pat-patting up behind, mud and all, having caught up at last.

And here came the funniest, warmest part. Because as they ran the last stretch of camino, every single friend who had carried the shell that day fell in behind them — Tato, Estrella waddling on her flippers, soggy little Coco, slow Don Café, even Slowy arriving precisely now (he'd been on his way the whole time). They became one happy, ridiculous, golden parade, thundering and waddling and dripping and bouncing toward the Pacific Sea.

"It's not a race anymore!" laughed Tato. "It's a PARADE!"

"It was always a parade," said Estrella gently. "We just hadn't noticed."

Cajeta bounced so high with excitement that she landed neatly on top of Coco's frosty mound and rode the rest of the way like a tiny fuzzy passenger. Tico the Toucan flew loops overhead announcing every single thing that happened — "THEY ROUND THE BEND! THE BEAN IS SMILING! THE SLOTH HAS NEARLY ARRIVED! WHAT A FINISH!" — and behind them all, the whole green country seemed to lean forward to watch, rainforest and volcano and golden dry-forest alike, the way you lean forward at the very best part of a story.

Gallo and Pinto reached the edge of the Pacific together. The sea was on fire with sunset, pink and gold and orange, and there stood the Sunset Bell, waiting.

"You ring it," said Gallo.

"No, YOU," said Pinto.

So of course they rang it together — *DONG!* — one paw each, and the sound rolled out across both halves of the country at once, all the way back to the faraway Caribbean where they'd started that morning. The Joy Shell flared bright as a tiny sun, then settled into a soft, sleepy glow, its work done.

They had carried joy clear across the whole wide world — or at least the whole wide country, which to a tired and happy treat is just about the same thing.

---

That night, all the friends flopped together in a sandy pile on the Pacific shore, watching the stars wink on one by one. The Joy Shell glowed gently in the middle of them, humming.

"We went so far," yawned Tato, his crispy edges finally relaxed.

"We went far," agreed Don Café softly, "because nobody carried it the whole way. We passed the joy along. That is how the longest camino gets walked, niños. Together, and never all at once. Pura Vida."

And one by one, with the two seas whispering on either side of them, the friends fell fast asleep.

---

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, you do not have to carry everything by yourself, not ever. When the road feels long, pass a little joy to someone beside you, and let them pass some back to you, and watch how far you both can go. Tonight you can set everything down, the way Tato set down the shell, and feel how light and free that makes you. The sea is whispering on both your shores, the stars are all switched on, and you are exactly where you belong. Pura Vida, sweet dreamer. Goodnight.
