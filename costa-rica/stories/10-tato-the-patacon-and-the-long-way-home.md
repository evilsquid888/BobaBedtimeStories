# Story 10: Tato the Patacón and the Long Way Home

**Main Character:** Tato the Patacón — a twice-fried smashed plantain disc, golden-crispy, proud of his crunch, stubby-footed and stout-hearted
**Guest Characters:** Doña Pausa the mail-snail, Mopa the sleepy street dog, Garza the night heron
**Setting:** The quiet evening streets of Pura Vida Town, from the harbor all the way home to the corner soda
**Spanish Spotlight:** **despacio** (dess-PAH-see-oh) — slowly, little by little
**Theme:** Step by small step, even the littlest traveler can find the way home.

---

It was closing time at the corner **soda** (SOH-dah) — the little family eatery — and Tato the Patacón was doing his evening rounds.

Every night, while Doña Mari wiped the counters and hummed, Tato marched up and down the windowsill on his two stubby feet, inspecting things. He inspected the salt shaker. He inspected the napkins. He inspected his own reflection in the spoon, gave his crispy golden edges an approving pat, and said, as he said every night, "Magnificent. Crunchiest patacón in Pura Vida Town. Possibly the world. Inspection complete."

Then he yawned an enormous yawn, climbed onto a warm folded napkin near the counter's edge, and dozed off mid-sentence, which was very Tato of him.

And that — right there — is where the trouble began.

Because parked on the counter, two inches from Tato's napkin, sat the big tin lunch pail of Don Rigo the fisherman, lid open, waiting to be packed for tomorrow's early boat.

Doña Mari, sweeping crumbs in the half-dark, gave the counter one brisk wipe — *whisk* — and the napkin, with one small sleeping patacón aboard, slid gently off the edge and settled into the pail like a letter into an envelope.

The lid clicked shut. The pail swung onto the back of a bicycle. And off into the night went Tato, snoring his tiny crispy snore, with absolutely no idea that he was leaving home.

---

He woke to a *clunk.*

The lid had popped loose, and Tato sat up, blinked, and looked out at a world that was one-hundred-percent *not the soda.*

There were boats. There were masts swaying like sleepy palm trees. There was a long wooden dock, and dark water going *glip, glip*, and far across the bay a tall white tower swept a slow beam of light around and around — the **faro** (FAH-roh) — the lighthouse — blinking at him like the world's most patient firefly.

"Ah," said Tato, very calmly. "I appear to be at the harbor. The harbor is on the *other side of town.*" He paused. "I would like to un-appear now, please."

Nobody un-appeared him. Don Rigo had gone whistling down the dock to check his boat ropes, and the streets behind the harbor stood quiet and violet, the lamps flickering on one by one, like the town tucking itself in.

Tato climbed out of the pail, stood up as tall as a flat thing can stand, and put his crispy hands on his crispy hips.

"Right," he announced to the empty dock. "Smash now, worry never. That's the patacón way." Then he looked at the three dark streets winding away from the water, all completely identical, and added in a much smaller voice, "Although nobody ever taught me the patacón way of knowing *which street is which.*"

---

That was when he noticed the snail.

She was inching along the top of a red postbox, a round gray snail with a shell like a cinnamon roll and a pair of tiny reading glasses perched on her eyestalks. A miniature mailbag was slung across her shell.

"Buenas," said the snail, in a voice as slow and smooth as honey off a spoon. "Doña Pausa. Night mail. You look lost, joven."

"Lost!" said Tato. "Me? A patacón is never lost. A patacón is merely... between locations." His stubby feet shuffled. "I'm between the harbor and the corner soda, specifically. Do you happen to know the way? You're a mail-snail. Mail-snails know every address in town."

"Every address," Doña Pausa agreed proudly. "Every doorstep. Every crack in every doorstep. I once delivered a birthday card only four months late, which for a snail is practically rude with earliness." She turned one eyestalk toward the streets. "The corner soda. Easy. Down Calle Sal, across the rain gutter, through the plaza where the pigeons sleep, then up Mango Hill. You'll see the soda's little orange lamp at the top."

"Wonderful!" said Tato. "Let's go!"

"Oh, *I'm* not going," said Doña Pausa. "At my speed, dear, I'd get you home by Thursday. You'll have to walk it yourself." She patted his arm kindly with an eyestalk. "Just remember the only directions that matter: **despacio** (dess-PAH-see-oh) — slowly, little by little. One street at a time. That's how the mail always, *always* arrives."

Tato saluted her, squared his ruffly shoulders, and marched off down Calle Sal — and Doña Pausa called after him, "Mind the gutter! It rained this afternoon!" which took her so long to say that by the time she finished, Tato had already found out for himself.

---

The rain gutter lay across the bottom of Calle Sal like a small silver river, gurgling cheerfully, exactly as wide as a patacón's worst nightmare.

Tato stopped at the edge and eyed it with deep professional suspicion. Water and crunch, as everyone knows, are not friends.

"Okay," he said. "Think, Tato. You are crispy, but you are also *clever.*" He looked around. A mango leaf lay nearby — too floppy. A bottle cap — too round. And then, glinting in the lamplight, a long flat wooden stick from somebody's afternoon ice pop, smelling faintly of strawberry.

"A bridge!" Tato cried. "A delicious-smelling bridge!"

He heaved the stick across the gutter, end to end. It reached — *just* — and wobbled like Flan the Coconut Flan on a windy day. Tato set one stubby foot on it. The stick teetered left. He set the other foot on it. The stick teetered right.

"New plan," Tato whispered.

And then he did the one thing in all the world that a patacón does better than anybody: he lay down flat. Perfectly, splendidly, gloriously flat, spreading his weight from edge to ruffly edge, and inched across the wobbling stick on his belly like a very small, very golden crocodile.

"Ha!" he said, hopping up safe and dry on the far side. "And Gallo says I'm flat-footed. Gallo, my friend, *all* of me is flat. It is my finest professional feature."

Far behind him, atop the postbox, Doña Pausa finally finished watching the whole thing and murmured, "Nicely done," to a street that Tato had already left.

---

The plaza, when he reached it, was hushed and silvery. Pigeons dozed in a row along the fountain's rim like plump gray teapots. The only way through to Mango Hill was a shortcut alley behind an iron gate — and the only way through the gate was a flat gap underneath it.

There was just one small problem with the gap, and the problem was enormous, shaggy, and snoring.

A street dog lay sprawled against the bottom of the gate — a big rumpled fellow the color of a dusty floor mop, paws twitching, fast asleep, blocking the gap from end to end. Tato knew him by sight: Mopa, the sleepiest dog in Pura Vida Town, who had once fallen asleep in the middle of chasing his own tail and had to be woken up by the tail itself getting bored.

"Excuse me," Tato said politely. "Señor Mopa? Pardon me. Coming through."

Mopa snored on, magnificently.

Tato pushed at one giant paw. It was like pushing a sofa. He cleared his throat as loudly as a small fried disc can clear anything. Nothing.

"Hmm," said Tato. And then he had an idea so good his edges crinkled.

Because Tato knew one true thing about the dogs of Pura Vida Town: asleep or awake, every last one of them dreamed about the smell of the corner soda's fryer. And Tato — fresh, golden, kissed with salt — *was* that smell, walking around on two stubby feet.

He stood as close to Mopa's great nose as he dared, and he fanned himself gently with both hands, wafting his warm salty toasty patacón smell straight into the dog's dreams.

Mopa's nose twitched. His paws paddled. "Mmf," he mumbled, deep in his sleep. "Patacones..." And — still ninety percent asleep — the big dog rolled dreamily over toward the wonderful smell, clearing the gap under the gate completely, and settled back down with a sigh of pure happiness, now using the fountain step as a pillow.

"Thank you, señor," Tato whispered. "You have excellent taste, even unconscious."

Then he lay down flat once more and slid under the iron gate as smoothly as a letter through a mail slot — which, he thought proudly, was probably the nicest compliment Doña Pausa could ever have paid him.

---

Mango Hill was the last street, and it was a steep one — smooth old cobblestones rising up and up toward a sky full of early stars.

Tato started walking. Three steps up, he slid two steps back. Five steps up — *whoosh* — he slid all the way down and landed gently in a flowerpot, coming up wearing a red hibiscus blossom like a very small, very fancy hat.

"Lovely," he said from inside the flower. "But not the direction I was hoping for."

He tried rolling up on his edge like a coin. He wobbled, wavered, sang a brave little marching song, and rolled right back down into the same flowerpot, which by now felt rather like an old friend.

Tato sat in the flowerpot and caught his breath, and the night was so quiet he could hear the faro's beam humming far away over the water. And in that quiet, he remembered the painted oxcarts climbing the big mountain road outside town — how they never went straight up. They zigzagged. Back and forth, side to side, easy as you please, all the way to the top.

"Despacio," Tato murmured. "Little by little. Like the mail."

So he climbed the hill the oxcart way — left across the street, then right across the street, then left again, zig and zag, slow and small and steady — and the hill, which had been impossible, turned into nothing but one easy sideways stroll after another.

Halfway up, a tall gray night heron standing one-legged on a lamp post looked down at him with round golden eyes.

"You're out late, little crispy one," said the heron, whose name was Garza and who saw everything that happened in the town after dark.

"Going home," puffed Tato, zigging past her lamp post. "Corner soda. Top of the hill. Two more zigzags, I estimate."

"Three," said Garza gently. "But who's counting. Your lamp is lit, little one. I can see it from here — the small orange one over the doorway. They left it burning for you."

And somehow, of everything that had happened that whole long evening, it was *that* — the little lamp, left on — that made Tato's warm soft middle go warmer and softer than it had ever been.

---

The soda, when he finally reached it, was dark and hushed and smelled like home — like salt and lime and the sleepy *blip... blip... blip* of the resting fryer.

The door was locked. Tato did not mind one bit. He lay down flat one last time and slid under it, smooth as anything, into the warm familiar dark.

He climbed the leg of the counter, slow as a snail and proud of it. He padded past the salt shaker — "Inspection tomorrow," he whispered to it — and past the spoon, too tired even to admire his reflection, which had never once happened before in recorded history.

In the plantain basket by the window, everything was warm and still. As Tato snuggled down into the folded napkins, he heard Pinto murmur from a nearby bowl, deep in his dreams: "Better together, mae..."

"Couldn't agree more," Tato whispered.

Through the window, far away across the rooftops, the faro swept its slow beam around the bay — once for the boats, once for the snails, once, Tato felt quite sure, for small brave travelers safely home. His eyes drifted shut.

"**Buenas noches** (BWEH-nahs NOH-chess) — good night — town," he mumbled. "Good night, gutter. Good night, gate. Good night, Mopa, you magnificent mop."

And the crunchiest patacón in Pura Vida Town — possibly the world — fell fast, fast asleep.

## Goodnight Blessing
May you always find your way home, sweet one, no matter how far the day has carried you — one street, one step, one small zigzag at a time. May there always be kind voices to point the way, a warm light left burning just for you, and a cozy basket waiting at the end of every long adventure. You don't have to hurry. The way home will wait. **Despacio**, little traveler — slowly, little by little, let your eyes grow heavy and your breathing grow soft, like a town turning off its lamps one by one. You are safe. You are home. Pura vida, little dreamer. Goodnight.
