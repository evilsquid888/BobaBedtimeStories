# Story 22: Olla de Carne, the Big Warm Stew

**Main Character:** Pinto
**Guest Characters:** Gallo, Tato the Patacon, Yuca, Chayote, Plátano
**Setting:** A bubbling family stew pot on a cool highland evening
**Theme:** You find out what you can do by doing it — courage shows up when you act together.
**Spanish Spotlight:** the **olla** (OH-yah) — the big pot — the **cuchara** (koo-CHAH-rah) — the wooden spoon — and **upe** (OO-peh) — the friendly hello Ticos call at a neighbor's door

---

High in the cool green mountains, in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and rain, Doña Rosa's biggest pot was simmering up an olla de carne — and everything was going perfectly. Which, as anyone who has ever cooked anything knows, is exactly when trouble likes to knock.

The trouble knocked politely, at least. "¡**Upe**!" called a voice from the front gate — "**upe**" (OO-peh), the friendly hello Ticos call at a neighbor's door. It was Doña Marta from down the road, holding something that needed borrowing or returning, the way neighbors always are.

"Behave yourself, stew," said Doña Rosa, wiping her hands. "I'll be back before you bubble twice." And out she went.

The stew bubbled once.

Then a log in the fire below slipped, rolled against its neighbors, and woke the whole woodpile up. The flames stretched, yawned, and got entirely too excited.

The stew bubbled twice. Then twelve times. Then it stopped counting.

Inside the **olla** (OH-yah) — the big pot — Pinto, the small glossy black bean with a pale stripe like a tiny smile, lowered his little wooden spoon and said, very calmly, "Mae. I don't think she'll be back before we bubble twice."

---

Now, you should know who was where, because in about one minute it was going to matter very much.

*Inside* the pot: Pinto. Gallo, the cheerful fried-rice grain in his tiny palm hat. Tato the Patacon, the brave smashed-plantain disc, who had cannonballed in an hour ago. Plus the beef, who was tender and sleepy; the corn, who only ever hummed; and a bus-line of carrots who did not handle surprises well.

*Outside* the pot, up on the rim, stood three brand-new ingredients who had been too nervous to jump in. Yuca, a long pale root, stiff and sturdy as a fence post. Chayote, a pale green squash shaped like a small smiling pillow, with a neat little groove down her middle like a pocket. And Plátano, a half-ripe plantain, golden-green and so bendy he could practically tie a bow in himself when he fidgeted. Which he was doing right now.

"Is the pot supposed to sound like that?" asked Yuca.

The pot was beginning to sound like a crowd at a futbol match.

"No," said Pinto from below, paddling against the current. "No, it is not."

The broth, warmed by the over-eager fire, rose from a murmur to a rumble to a full rolling boil. The carrots began bobbing in panicked circles. Gallo was swept past on a wave of broth, hat first.

"Settle and pinto! Settle and PINTO!" Gallo hollered. "Why is the soup going so FAST? Soup is supposed to be the slowest food there is! This is practically a water slide!"

"Hold the spoon!" called Pinto, and Gallo grabbed on. From down in the churn, nobody inside could climb the slippery walls, the beef was too tender to budge, and the corn — bless him — just hummed a slightly worried note.

Which meant the only ones who could do anything at all... were the three nervous newcomers standing on the rim.

---

The first problem announced itself with a clatter. The big lid, resting half-on, began to rattle and hop on its cushion of steam — *clank-clank-clank* — bouncing closer to slamming shut. And a pot this excited, sealed up tight, would boil over in no time at all.

"The lid!" Pinto called up. "Yuca! Can you hold the lid open?"

Yuca went pale, which for a yuca is really saying something. "Me? I can't! I've never held anything! I'm just a root! Roots mostly just... lie there!"

"You're a *sturdy* root," called Pinto, steady as ever. "Sturdy is exactly what a rattling lid needs. You don't have to be brave for the whole night, mae. Just be sturdy for ten seconds."

Yuca looked at the hopping lid. The lid hopped at her. And then, because there was no time left to be scared in, she stopped being scared and started being a yuca. She lay herself down across the rim, right in the gap, stiff and proud as a fence post — and the lid came down, met her solid back, and could not close. It rested there, propped wide open, steam sighing out past her in a long relieved whoosh.

"I'm holding it," Yuca whispered. Then, louder, amazed at herself: "I'm HOLDING it! It's heavy and I don't care! Is this what fence posts feel like all the time? No wonder they look so smug!"

"Ten more seconds!" called Pinto. "And then probably a few more after that!"

---

The second problem was already climbing the walls. With the fire still roaring below, the broth foamed up, up, up — a fizzy golden tide rising toward the rim, the kind that slips over the edge and hisses all over the stove.

"It's going to boil over!" wailed Gallo, surfing past again the other way. "Goodbye, my hat! Tell my hat I loved it!"

"Your hat is on your head!" Tato shouted.

"Then tell my HEAD I loved it!"

Pinto turned his eyes to the counter, where Doña Rosa's long wooden spoon lay waiting. "Chayote! The **cuchara** (koo-CHAH-rah) — the wooden spoon! If you lay it flat across the top of the pot, the foam can't climb past it. It's an old kitchen trick. The foam touches the dry wood and — *pop* — it loses its nerve."

"But I can't carry a spoon!" Chayote squeaked. "I don't have hands! I'm a squash! I have never once carried anything in my whole life, and I would remember, because it would have been the most exciting thing that ever happened to me!"

"You don't need hands," said Pinto. "You have that groove down your middle, mae. It's the perfect size for a spoon handle. Tuck it in and roll."

Chayote looked down at the little groove she had spent her whole life being embarrassed about. Then she took the deepest breath a squash can take, hopped to the counter, and nudged herself against the spoon until the handle slipped snugly into her groove — *click* — like it had been carved just for her.

"It FITS," she gasped. "I'm a spoon-holder! I'm a SQUASH WITH A JOB!"

And she rolled. Across the counter, up the warm side of a folded towel, onto the rim, wobbling once — "whoa — WHOA — okay" — and laid the cuchara flat across the top of the pot, right where the foam was tallest.

The foam climbed, touched the dry wooden spoon, and — just as Pinto promised — completely lost its nerve. *Pop. Pop-pop.* The golden tide sighed and sank back down into the pot, grumbling like a wave that had been told to behave.

"HA!" shouted Gallo from below, shaking a tiny fist of triumph. "Take THAT, soup!" Then, quickly: "No offense. We love you. You're delicious."

---

But the fire — the cause of all this commotion — was still roaring away underneath, and as long as it roared, the trouble would just keep coming back. Down at the mouth of the wood stove, the slipped log lay against the others, feeding the flames like a friend whispering "go on, go on."

"Plátano," said Pinto. "Last one. That log has to roll back where it was. Then the fire calms down and all of this is over."

Plátano peered down the warm side of the stove at the glowing doorway below. "That's so far down," he said in a very small voice. "And I'm not even all the way ripe."

"That's exactly why it has to be you," said Tato, bobbing up. "A ripe plantain is soft — he'd droop. A green one is stiff as a board — he'd snap. But *half*-ripe? Half-ripe BENDS, mae. You're the bendiest thing in this whole kitchen. Bend down there, give that log one good nudge, and bend right back up."

"Bend down. Nudge. Bend back," Plátano repeated. "Bend down. Nudge. Bend back." He said it four more times, lost track of which part came first, and decided to just go.

He hooked his top end on the rim of the stove ledge, took a breath, and bent — a long, slow, golden-green arc, curling down the warm side of the stove like a question mark leaning over to read something. The heat tousled him. His ears (plantains have ears for exactly this kind of moment) heard the fire crackle, entirely too pleased with itself.

"Excuse me," Plátano told the log, politely, because his mother had raised him right. And he gave it one good, flexible, springy *boop*.

The log rolled back into its proper place. The flames, with nothing extra to gobble, shrank down, stretched, and settled into the soft steady glow of a fire that has remembered its manners. Plátano boinged back upright so fast he wobbled like a ruler twanged off a desk edge.

"I did it," he announced, to nobody and everybody. "I bent! I nudged! I unbent! In the CORRECT ORDER!"

And the pot — the great big beloved olla — slowed its wild boil to a rumble, the rumble to a murmur, the murmur to the cozy *blub... blub... blub* of a stew doing exactly what a stew should do on a cool mountain night.

---

That was when the gate creaked, and Doña Rosa came back inside.

She stopped. She looked at her pot — lid propped neatly open, wooden spoon laid flat across the top, fire purring, broth simmering as politely as a stew in a cookbook.

"Well," she said slowly, hands on her hips. "Who taught my olla such good manners while I was gone?" She tapped the cuchara and chuckled. "The spoon across the pot. My own abuela's trick. I haven't done that in years." She gave the kitchen one long, fond, suspicious look. "Somebody in here is cleverer than they're letting on."

Six ingredients looked extremely innocent. Two of them whistled. Chayote, who didn't know how to whistle, hummed the corn's note instead, which the corn found deeply flattering.

And then — with the fire gentle and the broth calm and absolutely nothing left to be afraid of — Yuca, Chayote, and Plátano looked at one another and started to laugh.

"You know," said Yuca, "an hour ago I was too scared to get into this pot."

"And then we held it, rolled it, and booped it," said Chayote.

"After all that," said Plátano, "a warm bath sounds wonderful, honestly."

And the three of them jumped in together — *sploosh, sploosh, sploosh* — to gasps of welcome from the carrots, who were ready for some calmer company.

"You three were brave tonight," said Pinto warmly, as the broth gathered them in.

"We were terrified tonight," said Yuca.

"Same thing," said Pinto, "once you start moving. You never find out what you can do by standing on the rim thinking about it. You find out by doing it — preferably with friends on either side. Better together, mae."

Supper that night was the best olla de carne the highlands had tasted all year, and Doña Rosa said so twice. Bowls were filled and refilled, the windows fogged up soft and gold, and somewhere in the warm middle of it all, one sturdy root, one squash with a very useful groove, and one perfectly half-ripe plantain bobbed close together, telling the story to the corn, who hummed along at all the exciting parts.

The fire burned low. The lid was set gently back. And the big warm pot murmured itself, and everyone in it, slowly off to sleep.

## Goodnight Blessing
May you be brave the way the kitchen taught it tonight, little one — not the loud kind, just the kind that takes one small step when a friend calls your name. May you remember that the scared feeling and the brave feeling can sit in the same heart at the same time, and that you find out what you can do by doing it. The fire is gentle now, the pot is calm, the supper was shared and wonderful. So let your busy thoughts simmer down to a murmur, then to a blub, then to nothing at all. Sturdy dreams, soft landings, and a whole warm night of sleep. Goodnight.
