# Story 42: Pejibaye and the Wobbly First Kick

**Main Character:** Pejibaye the Peach-Palm Fruit
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A schoolyard at recreo where the kids are choosing teams for a quick futbol game
**Theme:** Courage means being the first to try, even when your knees shake.
**Spanish Spotlight:** valiente (bah-lee-EN-teh) = brave

---

Pejibaye was a round little peach-palm fruit about the size of a plum, with skin the orange-red of a sunrise that couldn't make up its mind. Up top he wore a stubby tuft of stem like a tiny green hat that flopped whenever he ran, which — and let's be honest here, dear reader — was not very often. Inside he was creamy and golden and warm, the kind of warm that smelled faintly of roasted chestnuts and butter. He was, by every measure, a delicious sort of fellow.

And every single day at **recreo** (reh-CREH-oh) = recess, Pejibaye sat on the low stone wall at the edge of the schoolyard and watched the other kids play futbol. The ball thwacked. The dust puffed. Somebody always yelled "GOOOOL!" so loudly that three pigeons fell off the gutter in surprise. Pejibaye's little stem-hat trembled with longing.

He wanted to play. Oh, how he wanted to play.

But he never, ever did.

Because Pejibaye had a worry, and the worry had a worry, and that smaller worry had an even tinier worry curled up inside it like the seed inside a fruit. The big worry went like this: *What if I kick the ball, and I miss, and everyone sees me miss, and the pigeons laugh, and the gutter laughs, and the sky itself rolls its eyes?*

So Pejibaye watched. Day after day. From the wall.

He had grown quite good at watching, if you must know. He could tell you exactly how Maduro the plantain liked to fake left and zip right. He knew that the tamales were sneaky-fast on the corners and that the dumplings were squishy but absolutely unbeatable as goalkeepers, because how do you get a ball past something that simply jiggles to fill the whole goal? He practiced kicks in his head a hundred times a day. In his head, every kick was perfect. In his head, the crowd roared his name. In his head, Pejibaye was the greatest futbolista who had ever rolled.

But the wall was warm and safe, and the field was wide and watching, and the distance between the two felt as far as the moon.

Now, you should know that the other kids weren't mean. They were dumplings and tamales and a very fast little plantain named Maduro who could dribble between his own feet without anybody being entirely sure how. They waved at Pejibaye sometimes. "¡Venga, Peji!" they'd call. "Come on!" And Pejibaye would smile his golden smile and shake his head and stay exactly where he was, glued to the wall like a barnacle who had given up on the sea.

This particular afternoon, the sun was high and lazy, the kind of warm Costa Rican afternoon that makes even the lizards yawn. The teams were lining up. Maduro counted heads. He counted again. He frowned, which is hard to do convincingly when you are a plantain.

"We're one short," he announced. "We need one more player or we can't start. Somebody has to take the kick-off."

A hush fell over the yard.

Here, dear reader, I must explain something important about the kick-off, because Pejibaye understood it all too well. The kick-off is the very first kick. The whole game stands still and watches it. Everybody is staring. There is no goal yet, no score, nothing — just you, the ball, and a thousand eyeballs (well, several dozen, but to a worried fruit it feels like a thousand). The kick-off is the bravest job in all of futbol, because it is the job of *going first.*

"Who'll take it?" asked Maduro.

Nobody moved.

A tamale studied his own corn-husk wrapper with sudden fascination. A dumpling discovered something deeply interesting in the clouds. Even Maduro, fast as he was, found that his feet had become extremely shy.

"Anybody?" he said, smaller now.

Still nothing. A breeze rolled a lonely leaf across the empty middle of the field, where the ball sat waiting like a question nobody wanted to answer. It is a peculiar thing about going first, dear reader: everyone is delighted to go *second.* Going second is easy. Going second, somebody has already shown you the water isn't full of crocodiles. But going first? Going first you find out about the crocodiles yourself.

"Please?" tried Maduro. "Anyone at all?"

The pigeons leaned forward.

And on the wall, Pejibaye felt his creamy golden insides do a slow, terrible flip. *This is it,* whispered the big worry. *Stay put. Stay safe. Somebody else will go.*

But nobody else went. The yard stayed frozen, all those treats too scared to be the first.

And something in Pejibaye — some small, stubborn, sunrise-colored something — wobbled to its feet.

His stem-hat shook. His round body shook. His shadow on the dust shook, which is genuinely impressive, because shadows usually keep it together. His heart went *thumpa-thumpa-thumpa* like a tiny drummer who had had far too much café.

He remembered something his **abuela** (ah-BWEH-lah) = grandmother had told him, back home in the pot, steam curling around them both. She'd said, "Mi amor, you are not brave because you are not afraid. You are **valiente** (bah-lee-EN-teh) = brave because you are afraid and you roll forward anyway."

Pejibaye rolled forward anyway.

He didn't run — fruits his shape don't run, they tumble — so he tumbled off the wall with a soft *plump* and rolled across the dusty yard toward the waiting ball. Every eyeball turned. The whole world went quiet. Even the lazy sun seemed to sit up a little.

"Valiente," Pejibaye whispered to himself, so soft only the dust could hear it. "Valiente, valiente."

He reached the ball. It was huge up close — black and white and round and enormous, like a moon that had rolled down for a visit. His knees (do fruits have knees? Pejibaye's certainly felt like it) knocked together like maracas. His tuft-hat flopped down over one eye.

The big worry made one last try. *Everyone is watching,* it hissed. *You'll miss. You'll look silly. Roll back to the wall while you still can.*

But for the very first time, Pejibaye had an answer for it. *Yes,* he thought. *Everyone is watching. And maybe I will miss. But the wall has never once cheered for me, and it never will, because the wall is a wall. I would rather miss out here than win back there.*

*Just kick it,* he told himself. *Just go first. It doesn't have to be perfect. It just has to be FIRST.*

He squeezed his eyes shut.

He swung his little round foot.

And — oh, dear reader, I wish I could tell you it was a magnificent kick. I wish I could tell you the ball sailed in a glorious arc while trumpets played. But the truth is far more wonderful than that.

Pejibaye kicked the ball *sideways.*

Completely sideways. A wobbly, lopsided, what-on-earth-was-that flub of a kick that sent the ball spinning off at a ridiculous angle, totally the wrong direction, straight toward a guava tree at the edge of the yard.

The pigeons gasped. Maduro's mouth fell open.

The ball *BONKED* off the tree trunk — *DOINK!* — bounced off a low branch — *BOING!* — knocked a sleepy guava loose that fell and landed *plop* on a dumpling's head — and came rocketing back across the yard at a brand-new angle that nobody, not the players, not the pigeons, not Pejibaye himself, could have predicted in a hundred years.

It rolled.

It rolled.

It rolled right between the two backpacks that everybody knew were the goalposts.

"...GOOOOOOOL!" screamed Maduro.

The yard EXPLODED. The tamales jumped. The dumplings cheered. The plantain did a flip and landed on his face and didn't even care. The pigeons launched into the air in a confetti of feathers. Somewhere a teacher rang a bell for no reason at all, just because it felt like the right thing to do.

Pejibaye stood in the middle of it all, blinking, his hat over one eye, absolutely certain he had done everything wrong.

"I — I missed," he stammered as the crowd rushed over. "I kicked it sideways! It only went in because of the tree and the guava and the — it was an accident! I wasn't good at all!"

Maduro skidded to a stop in front of him, grinning so wide you could have parked a bus in it.

"Peji," he said, panting, "we're not cheering because of the goal."

Pejibaye's stem-hat tilted. "You're not?"

"We're cheering," said Maduro, "because *you went first.* Everybody was too scared. The whole yard was frozen. And you — you tumbled off that wall with your knees knocking like maracas and you took the kick nobody else would take." He bonked Pejibaye gently on the top of his round head. "*That's* the goal, Peji. The brave part isn't the part where you don't miss. The brave part is the part where you try."

The tamale draped an arm around him. "Sideways kicks are the best kind anyway," he said. "Way more exciting than the boring straight ones."

"Teach me to bonk it off a tree!" begged a dumpling. "That was incredible!"

"It was an *accident,*" Pejibaye protested, though he was beginning to giggle now, a warm bubbly giggle that started somewhere down in his creamy middle.

"The best ones always are," said Maduro solemnly, and the whole yard burst out laughing, and Pejibaye laughed loudest of all.

And Pejibaye — round, golden, creamy, ridiculous Pejibaye — looked around at all those happy faces, and felt his insides go warm in a brand-new way. Not the worried-warm of the wall. A different warm. The warm of having finally, *finally* rolled forward.

He hadn't done it perfectly.

He'd done it *valiente.*

And as it turns out, that was the part that counted all along.

After that, Pejibaye never sat on the wall again. He played every single day at recreo, and he missed the ball plenty — sideways, backwards, once even somehow *upwards* — and every time he laughed and tried again. Because he'd learned the secret that the bravest treats know:

You don't have to go first *well.*

You just have to go first.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, somewhere your own first kick is waiting — a hand you want to raise, a song you want to sing, a try you've been too scared to try. And your knees might knock like maracas, and your hat might flop over one eye, and that is perfectly all right. Roll forward anyway. You don't have to be perfect. You only have to be valiente. So snuggle down warm and golden and brave, dear one, and let your sleepy heart whisper it soft: valiente, valiente, valiente. Tomorrow is full of first kicks, and you are ready for all of them. Goodnight.
