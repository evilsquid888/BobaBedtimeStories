# Story 40: Churchill and the Half-Time Repair

**Main Character:** Churchill the Puntarenas Shaved-Ice Sundae
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A packed seaside Puntarenas living room during the big national futbol final
**Theme:** When friends squabble, a peacemaker can mend things before the game even matters.
**Spanish Spotlight:** calma (KAHL-mah) = calm / take it easy

---

The living room in Puntarenas was so full of snacks that the couch cushions were sweating, and the ceiling fan above the television could barely turn for all the excitement bumping against it. Out the window, the sea hushed against the warm dark sand, but nobody inside was watching the sea. Everybody was watching the screen, where the national futbol team was tied — tied! — in the big final, with the whole country holding its breath like one enormous, slightly salty balloon.

On the coffee table, a crispy plantain chip named Tito leaned so far forward he nearly snapped himself in half. Beside him, a bowl of warm bean dip named Frijolita bubbled with nervous little blip-blop-blips. These two had been best friends since the market, back-shelf neighbors, the kind of friends who finished each other's snacks. And right now, both of them wanted the very same thing.

Both of them, it turned out, also wanted to blame somebody when things went wrong.

"AY, NO!" howled Tito, as the other team thumped a shot off the goalpost. The whole room gasped. "Frijolita! You jinxed it! You said the word — you said *easy game* — you NEVER say *easy game!*"

Frijolita's bubbles went from happy blip-blops to angry GLOOP-GLOOPS. "*I* jinxed it? You crunched your snack-crumbs the wrong way! Everyone knows you crunch left to right when we are winning! You crunched RIGHT TO LEFT, Tito, I SAW you!"

"That is not a real rule!"

"It is the OLDEST rule!"

And just like that, on the most important night of the whole futbol year, two best friends turned their backs to each other right there on the coffee table — Tito facing the lamp, Frijolita facing the wall — both of them so cross they forgot to watch the game at all.

The other snacks shifted uncomfortably. A bag of cassava crisps pretended to study the curtains. A wedge of fresh cheese hummed nervously to itself. A little stack of corn tortillas tried to start a friendly cheer — "Go team, go-o-o?" — but it fizzled out like a sparkler in the rain, because everybody could feel it: the whole cozy watch-party had gone, all at once, distinctly frosty.

"This is the worst," whispered the tortillas. "Worse than losing."

"Somebody should DO something," whispered the cheese.

But nobody did, because that is the tricky thing about a squabble between two friends — everyone else stands very still and hopes it fixes itself, the way you hope a wobbly tooth will simply stop wobbling. (It will not. But you hope.)

Which, as luck would have it, was exactly the right temperature for the guest who arrived next.

The screen flashed **MEDIO TIEMPO** — half-time — and the front door swung open with a cool, sugary breeze, and in glided Churchill.

Now, you have never seen anything in your life quite like a Churchill. He was a famous Puntarenas shaved-ice sundae, and he stood taller than the television itself: a glittering mountain of snow-white shaved ice piled high in a frosty glass, crowned with rivers of bright pink syrup and creamy condensed milk that dripped slow and lazy down his sides, and right on top, a single wobbling scoop of ice cream wearing a powdered-milk hat like a wise little cloud. He shimmered. He sparkled. He left a faint trail of deliciously cold air wherever he went, so that every snack he passed gave a happy little shiver. The cassava crisps stopped studying the curtains. The cheese stopped humming. Even the ceiling fan seemed to slow down to get a better look.

For Churchill was not just any sundae. Down at the seaside walk, where the salty air made everyone thirsty and hot tempers melted in the noon sun, folks said a Churchill could cool down anything — a sunburned afternoon, a long wait, even, some swore, a grumpy abuelo. He was Puntarenas's most famous treat, and he had spent his whole sweet life learning the difference between the kind of cold that pushes people apart and the kind of cool that brings them gently back together.

"**¡Buenas!**" he called warmly. "I felt the temperature drop all the way from the corner. And it is not the air conditioning." He looked at Tito facing the lamp. He looked at Frijolita facing the wall. He let out a long, frosty sigh. "Amigos. **Calma** (KAHL-mah) = take it easy."

"He started it," said Frijolita to the wall.

"She started it," said Tito to the lamp.

"And the lamp," said Churchill gravely, "has no idea what either of you is talking about. Neither does the wall. You are arguing at the furniture." A snicker rippled through the room, and even Tito's crispy shoulders twitched, just a little, in something that was almost a laugh and was definitely not still a sulk.

Churchill did not pick a side. This was, the other snacks would later agree, the most remarkable thing about him. A lesser sundae would have melted with the drama, pointed a syrupy finger, declared a winner, and made everything twice as hot. The cassava crisps had rather hoped he would — secretly, everyone loves to know who is *right*. But Churchill only slid himself gently right between the two friends, settling onto the coffee table with a soft, frosty *creak*, so cool and so calm that the angry heat coming off both of them began, ever so slightly, to settle, like steam fading off a cup left out overnight.

"I am not here to decide who jinxed the team," Churchill said. "I am only a sundae. I cannot even reach the remote." A few snacks giggled. "But I have learned a thing, friends, on all my cold nights at the market. When two people shout, nobody hears anything — not the game, not each other. So." He tipped his powdered-milk hat. "Let us try **despacio** (dehs-PAH-see-oh) = slowly. Tito, you first. And Frijolita — you only listen. You do not bubble back."

Tito turned around, just a little. "I am scared," he admitted, and his crispy voice cracked the tiniest bit. "I am scared we will lose, and it will be my fault for crunching wrong, and you will not be my friend anymore."

The room went quiet. You could hear the sea, and the slow tick of the ceiling fan, and somewhere far off a neighbor's radio crackling with the same half-time chatter. Frijolita's angry GLOOPS softened all the way down to the gentlest, sorriest blip. She had been so busy being loud that she had not, until just now, heard the small scared thing underneath all of Tito's noise.

"Now Frijolita," said Churchill, cool as the sea breeze. "Your turn. Tito only listens."

"I am scared too," Frijolita whispered, her bubbles wobbling. "I shouted at you because shouting felt better than being scared. But I do not really think you jinxed anything, Tito. You cannot jinx futbol by crunching. I made that rule up in 2019."

"You MADE IT UP?" gasped Tito — and then, despite everything, he laughed. A bright crispy laugh that scattered crumbs everywhere. "I have been crunching left to right for FOUR YEARS for nothing!"

"I am sorry," said Frijolita.

"I am sorry too," said Tito. "I was scared and I pointed it at you. That was not fair."

Tito reached out one crispy corner. Frijolita stretched out one warm, beany edge. They met, halfway, right in the middle — a little crunchy, a little soft, exactly the way the very best friendships are.

Churchill beamed, and a happy drip of pink syrup slid down his side and landed — *plip* — right on the coffee table between the two friends, like a little sweet handshake. "There," he said softly. "You see? The shouting was never about the crunching, or the jinxing. It was about being scared together and forgetting you were *together*. The game has not even told us who wins yet. But you two — you have already won the harder match."

"How did you get so wise?" asked the bag of cassava crisps, amazed.

"I am ten thousand tiny pieces of ice," said Churchill, "all squished into one glass, all leaning on each other so none of us melts too fast. You learn a few things, leaning." He winked one syrupy wink at the reader, if there happened to be one watching. "Also, it is very hard to lose your temper when you are this cold. You should all try it."

The screen flashed back to life. **SEGUNDO TIEMPO** — the second half — and the whole packed living room held its breath again. But this time it was a warm breath, a together breath. Tito scooted right up against Frijolita, shoulder to shoulder, the way best friends are supposed to watch a final. He crunched left to right. Then, grinning, he crunched right to left, just to prove he could, and Frijolita bubbled the happiest GLOOP of the whole night.

And the game? Oh, the game was wonderful and terrible and wonderful again. There were near-misses that made the bean dip jump clean out of her bowl. There was a save so beautiful that Churchill nearly tipped over from cheering, and the whole room had to lean in to catch him, sundae and snacks all wobbling together in one great happy huddle. And in the very last minute, with the sea hushing outside and the ceiling fan spinning like a tiny dizzy moon, the national team scored — a goal so lovely the whole street erupted, and somewhere a real-life grandmother banged a real-life pot with a real-life spoon.

The snacks leapt and cheered and toppled into one happy pile.

But here is the part Tito and Frijolita remembered most, years and years later, long after they had forgotten the final score: not the goal at all. They remembered the moment a tall, sparkling sundae slid between two frightened friends and said *calma*, and stayed cool, and took nobody's side but the side of the friendship itself.

Because a game lasts ninety minutes. A friendship, if you are gentle with it, lasts a great deal longer.

Churchill, by then, had melted just a little from all the cheering — a soft, contented puddle of pink and white spreading lazily across the table. He did not mind one bit. "Best way to go," he sighed happily, "warm room, good friends, a winning goal. **Pura vida** (POO-rah VEE-dah) = pure life." And the snacks scooped him up by the spoonful, sweet and cold and just exactly right, and that famous Puntarenas sundae became the most delicious goodnight any watch-party ever shared — proud and glad and grinning to the very last melty drop.

Outside, the sea breathed in. The sea breathed out. And one by one, full and happy and friends again, the snacks began, at last, to grow sleepy.

## Goodnight Blessing

May your worries melt gentle and slow, little one, like pink syrup down a cold glass mountain. May you always have a friend to sit shoulder to shoulder with, and the calma to listen before you shout. And if ever your heart goes frosty, may someone kind slide in beside you and help it thaw. The game is over. The sea is humming. Snuggle down where it is warm. Goodnight.
