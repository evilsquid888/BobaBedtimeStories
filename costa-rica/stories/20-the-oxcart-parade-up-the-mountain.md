# Story 20: The Oxcart Parade Up the Mountain

**Main Character:** Don Café (a roasted Costa Rican coffee bean from the highlands)
**Guest Characters:** Gallo, Pinto, Tamalita
**Setting:** A colorful painted oxcart winding up a highland road
**Theme:** Carrying each other's load makes the steepest hill feel light.
**Spanish Spotlight:** **carreta** (kah-RREH-tah) — oxcart (a colorful Costa Rican tradition)

---

On the morning of the Highland Market, a very small coffee bean stood on a very large wheel and announced, in the most important voice he could manage, that everyone was packing the cart wrong.

"No, no, no," said Don Café, tapping his tiny wooden walking stick. "The mangos go beside the milk, not on top of it. And whoever stacked the pumpkins like that — bless your heart — has clearly never met a bumpy road."

The cart he stood upon was a **carreta** (kah-RREH-tah) — an oxcart — and it was not just any cart. It was painted in dazzling swirls of orange and turquoise and sunflower yellow, with little starbursts around every spoke, the way the people of the highlands had painted them for a hundred years. It was so beautiful that butterflies kept landing on it by mistake, thinking it was a flower. One butterfly had been sitting on the same yellow swirl since breakfast, refusing to believe it was not a daisy. (Butterflies are not famous for their thinking.)

"Good things brew slowly," Don Café sighed, smelling of warm morning. "And good carts pack slowly too. A well-packed carreta is a poem, niños. A badly packed one is a disaster waiting to happen on a corner."

Then a pumpkin rolled off and bonked him gently on the hat.

"...as I was saying," he added, straightening his hat with great dignity.

---

"Rise and pinto!" chirped Gallo, a plump grain of fried rice in a little woven palm hat, hopping up onto the cart's edge. Beside him rolled Pinto, a glossy black bean with a tiny pale stripe like a smile, carrying his little wooden spoon.

"Buenos días, Don Café," said Pinto warmly. "Better together, mae."

"Indeed, indeed," said the old coffee bean. "Today we ride this fine carreta all the way up to the Highland Market, where the air is cool and the buyers are hungry. We have coffee to sell, and friends to bring." He gestured grandly toward the back of the cart, where a cozy little bundle wrapped in a glossy green banana leaf was bouncing with excitement.

"Wrapped with love!" giggled Tamalita, puffing a happy curl of steam. "I have never been up the big mountain before! Will there be a parade? I LOVE a parade. I packed three bows just in case."

"There is always a parade when the carreta rolls," said Don Café, smiling. "But first — there is the hill."

Everyone leaned over the side of the cart and looked up.

And up.

And uuuup.

The road climbed into the clouds like a ribbon someone had flung at the sky and forgotten to bring back down.

"Ooh," said Tamalita, in a smaller voice. Her steam came out in a nervous little squeak.

---

The ox was named Buey, and Buey was a kind and enormous fellow with patient eyes and horns like two polished moons. He gave a slow, friendly snort, leaned into his wooden yoke, and began to pull.

*Creeeak. Creeeak. Creeeak* went the painted wheels.

For a little while, it was wonderful. The cart wobbled cheerfully up the lower slope. Tamalita waved her bows at passing flowers and tried to wave back at a cow, who was extremely confused. Gallo hummed a tune and narrated everything he saw — "Ooh, a fern! Ooh, another fern! Ooh, the SAME fern, we're going so slow!" Pinto stirred the morning air with his spoon as if he were stirring a pot of something delicious. Don Café stood at the front like a tiny captain, smelling the cool pine and the faraway rain, and pretending he had not just been bonked by a pumpkin.

But the road grew steeper.

And steeper.

*Creeeeeeak...* went the wheels, slower now.

Poor Buey's breath came in big huffs. His hooves slipped on the loose pebbles. The cart, heavy with mangos and milk and pumpkins and coffee, began to feel like a mountain trying to climb a mountain.

"Steady, steady," murmured Don Café, who was beginning to worry. "Buey, my friend, you must not pull so hard. Your knees are not as young as your spirit."

Buey huffed and tried again. The wheels turned half a turn — then stopped. The whole carreta groaned and sat right down in the middle of the road, stubborn as a sleepy cat.

They were stuck. Halfway up the mountain. With the market waiting at the top and the morning slipping away.

---

"Don't worry!" cried Gallo, leaping down. "I'll push! Rise and pinto!" He set his little grain shoulders against the back wheel and shoved with all his might. He grunted. He strained. He made the face of a rice grain attempting to move a mountain.

The wheel did not move. But Gallo's hat fell over his eyes, which was at least something. "I think it's working!" he announced, into the inside of his own hat.

"Allow me," said Pinto kindly. He rolled over and pushed beside Gallo. Two of them now. The wheel creaked... and held still.

"Wrapped with love — and PUSHING with love!" announced Tamalita, waddling over to help, her bows bouncing. Three of them now, huffing and puffing.

Don Café watched them strain and slip, and he tapped his walking stick thoughtfully. "Friends," he said at last, "stop a moment. You are all pushing, but you are pushing the same little corner. And meanwhile, this poor cart is carrying everything by itself — the mangos, the milk, the pumpkins, the coffee, all piled in one great heavy heap."

"Isn't that what carts are for?" panted Gallo.

"A cart carries the load," said Don Café slowly, the way he said all wise things. "But a cart is not alone. A carreta is a family. Every spoke holds a little. Every wheel shares a little. That is the secret the old painters knew — why they painted them so bright. A carreta is not a cart that carries you. It is a cart you carry *together*."

Tamalita's steam went all swirly with thinking. "So... if everybody carries a little..."

"Then nobody carries a lot," said Pinto softly, his stripe curving into a smile. "Better together, mae."

---

So they did it the new way.

Tamalita, who was warm and snuggly and never spilled a drop, tucked the jugs of milk close against her cozy banana-leaf sides so they wouldn't slosh and slow things down. Gallo, light and bouncy, scampered up the high pile and balanced the wobbly pumpkins so the weight sat even on both wheels. Pinto, calm and steady, rolled to the very back and braced himself like a little doorstop so the cart could never, ever slide backward again. And Don Café — small as he was — climbed onto Buey's broad head, right between the polished horns, and became the lookout.

"To the LEFT, my friend — there is a smooth patch!" he called. "Now slow — a stone — gooood. Now PULL!"

Buey gave a great heave. And here is the strange and lovely thing. The cart did not feel lighter because there was less inside it. There was exactly the same amount of mango and milk and pumpkin and coffee as before — Don Café checked. The cart felt lighter because everyone was holding a piece of it. The same load, shared four ways and one ox, suddenly felt like nothing much at all.

"This is the most science thing I have ever seen," whispered Gallo, wide-eyed, from atop the pumpkins.

"It is not science, mae," said Pinto gently from the back. "It is love. Love does math very differently."

*Creak. Creak. Creak-creak-creak!* The wheels began to turn again, faster and gladder, the bright painted starbursts spinning like little suns.

"It's WORKING!" squealed Tamalita, puffing happy steam clouds. "Look at us go!"

---

And then Don Café did the most wonderful thing of all.

He began to sing.

It was an old highland song, slow and warm, the kind that smells of coffee and rain. "Sube, sube, carretita," he sang — *up, up, little cart* — and his voice rolled down the mountain like honey.

Gallo joined in with a sunny hum. Pinto added a low, steady note that felt like the ground itself singing. Tamalita giggled the chorus in puffs of steam — *pfft, pfft, PFFT!* — which was not quite in tune but was easily the happiest sound on the whole mountain. Even Buey lowed along in a deep, rumbly *moooo* that shook the dew off the ferns.

Up they climbed, singing all the way. And the song did what songs do on hard hills: it made their feet light and their hearts big and the steep road feel like a gentle slope.

A passing toucan heard them and shouted, "Beak the news — the carreta is COMING! And it's SINGING! Stop the presses, fluff the petals, the singing cart is nearly here!" Word flew ahead, beak to beak, branch to branch, up the whole mountain to the Highland Market — though by the time it arrived, the toucans had somehow added a brass band and a dancing pumpkin to the story, because that is how gossip works in the jungle.

So when the painted oxcart finally crested the top of the mountain — wheels spinning, bows bouncing, everyone singing — the whole market was waiting, clapping and cheering and tossing flower petals into the cool blue air.

It was, just as Tamalita had hoped, a parade. The best kind. The kind you make yourself, by getting somewhere hard together. There was no brass band and no dancing pumpkin — the toucans had exaggerated, as toucans do — but Tamalita twirled all three of her bows so merrily that nobody minded one bit, and a very small child in the crowd insisted forever after that she HAD seen a dancing pumpkin, and who is to say she didn't?

---

They sold every mango and every drop of milk and every single fragrant coffee bean (except Don Café, of course, who was not for sale, thank you very much). And as the sun dipped low and gold behind the volcanoes, the four friends sat on the empty carreta, full of warm tamales and warmer feelings, while Buey munched sweet grass nearby.

"Don Café," yawned Tamalita sleepily, leaning against his side, "the hill was so steep. But it didn't feel steep at all."

The old coffee bean smiled, smelling of evening and contentment. "That, mi amor, is the whole secret of a carreta," he said. "And the whole secret of a familia. The load never changes. But shared between loving hands, the heaviest hill in the world feels like the gentlest little stroll. Pura vida."

And rolling slowly home down the mountain, under the first sleepy stars, they sang their song one more time — soft now, just for themselves — all the way to the bottom.

## Goodnight Blessing
May your loads always be shared by hands that love you, little one, and may every steep hill in your life have friends pushing close beside you. When tomorrow feels too heavy to carry alone, remember the bright painted carreta and the song that made the mountain small. You were never meant to climb anything by yourself. Sleep now, wrapped warm and safe, knowing you are part of a familia that carries you. Pura vida, sweet dreamer. Goodnight.
