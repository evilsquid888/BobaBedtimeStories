# Story 21: Poás and the Crater of Stars

**Main Character:** Coco the Granizado
**Guest Characters:** Don Café
**Setting:** The cool, misty rim of Poás volcano crater under a starry sky
**Theme:** Cool heads and warm hearts make the perfect pair.
**Spanish Spotlight:** estrella (es-TREH-yah) = star

---

High up where the clouds go to take their naps, on the chilly rim of Poás volcano, a rainbow snowcone went skateboarding into the mist — and screeched to a happy stop.

"Whoa-ho-HO!" hollered Coco the Granizado, his tiny round sunglasses fogging right up. He was a tall fluffy mound of shaved ice in a paper cone, drizzled with red and blue and yellow syrup, dusted with powdered milk like fresh snow. And for the very first time in his cool little life, he was *not melting.*

"It's COLD up here!" he gasped, doing a delighted little spin that flung sparkly flecks of frost into the breeze. "Cold like a freezer that loves me back! Down in the valley I'm always dripping, drip-drip-drip, half-melted by lunchtime. By dinnertime I'm basically a puddle wearing sunglasses!" He patted his frosty top, firm and crunchy and proud. "But up here? Up here I'm crispy as a fresh shave. Stay frosty, stay sweet, baby! This is *tuanis!*"

Tuanis, you should know, is Costa Rican for *awesome.*

He kicked his little skateboard up into his hand and breathed in the thin, cold mountain air. It tasted like clouds and minty mist. Somewhere below, the valley glittered with warm yellow lights, but Coco had skated *up*, up the long winding road, up past the coffee farms, up where the trees grew shorter and the air grew sharper — chasing a rumor he'd heard from a passing hummingbird: that there was a place so high and so cold that ice never melted, and the sky came down to say goodnight.

And then Coco saw the crater pool — and even a granizado can freeze in wonder.

---

The pool sat in the bottom of the crater, smooth and still as a mirror. Above it spread the night sky, and the sky was *stuffed* with stars. Thousands of them. And every single one was reflected in that quiet water, so it looked like there were two skies — one up high, and one down low — and Poás was a cup holding both.

"Pura vida," Coco breathed. That's the Costa Rican way of saying *pure life, all is well, what a beautiful thing.*

Just then a warm, wonderful smell drifted up the trail. The smell of morning, even at night.

"Good evening, young icicle," chuckled a slow, kind voice. Up the path came Don Café, a glossy dark roasted coffee bean with a deep crease down his middle, a tiny straw farmer's hat, and a little wooden walking stick. He smelled so cozy that Coco's frostiness practically sighed.

"Don Café! What are you doing way up here?"

"The best coffee grows on the slopes of the volcanoes," said the old bean, tapping the dark soil with his stick. "I come up to visit the mountain that feeds my whole family. And to watch the **estrella** (es-TREH-yah) = stars. There is no better stargazing in all of Costa Rica than right here." He smiled his deep, slow smile. "Good things brew slowly. And good skies are worth the climb."

"It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen," Coco whispered. "I never felt at home anywhere before. Too cold for the beach, too melty for the valley. But up here, in the chill, with all these estrellas..." He sniffled, which for a granizado is mostly just a tiny refreezing sound. "I'm *home.*"

"The mountain has been waiting for you," said Don Café warmly. "Every mound of ice in this whole country wishes it could be where you are standing. You did not melt away into someone's cup. You climbed. You found your cold corner of the world, where the frost is a gift instead of a goodbye." He chuckled, a slow rumbly sound like a pot just starting to bubble. "Not many treats ever find the place that fits them just right. Pura vida, my frosty friend. Pura vida."

Coco wiped a happy frost-tear off his cheek before it could refreeze and grinned so wide his sunglasses slid down his nose. He felt, for the first time, like the place and the snowcone were exactly the right shape for each other — like a scoop of ice settling perfectly into its cone.

And that was when the crying started.

---

It was a small sound. A wobbly, sniffly, scared little sound, coming from the bushes near the crater rim.

Coco skated over, ice crunching softly. In the bushes, huddled together in a shivery pile, were the mountain critters of Poás. A fuzzy little volcano mouse. Three tiny tree frogs the color of green jelly. A roly-poly mountain quail with a feather curl bobbing over her eyes. And one very small, very round armadillo who had rolled himself into a ball so tight he looked like a polished river stone.

"Hey, hey, hey," said Coco gently. "What's all the sniffling about, mountain friends?"

"It's the *dark!*" squeaked the volcano mouse. "Every night the sun goes down and the whole sky turns black and we don't *like* it!"

"We hide in the bushes," wobbled the quail.

"We squeeze our eyes shut," peeped the frogs.

"And *I*," said the armadillo ball, his voice muffled, "do not come out until breakfast. The dark is too big. It could have *anything* in it."

"And worst of all," whispered the smallest tree frog, "the night has *sounds.* Crick-crick. Whoo-whoo. Rustle-rustle. We don't know what makes them, so we're sure it must be something terrible with very large teeth."

"It's probably just the wind," said a second frog, not sounding sure at all.

"The wind does not go *whoo,*" argued the third frog.

"Owls go whoo," offered the quail, then clapped a wing over her own beak. "Oh no. I shouldn't have said owls."

The whole pile shuddered, and the armadillo rolled himself one notch tighter.

Coco's frosty heart gave a melty little squeeze. He knew exactly what to do — well, almost. He pushed his sunglasses up. He cracked his frosty knuckles. He did *not* actually know what to do.

So he did the thing he always did: he got loud and tried to make it fun.

"OKAY!" he boomed. "Everybody UP! We're gonna run laps and shout at the dark until it's not scary, woo-HOO!" He grabbed the quail's wing and tugged. "C'mon! Big energy! Stay frosty, stay—"

"AAAH!" The whole pile of critters shrieked and dove deeper into the bushes. The armadillo rolled clear under a fern.

Coco blinked. "...That went worse than I planned, huh."

"Mmm," hummed Don Café, settling onto a rock. "Cold ice and a hot fright do not mix, young one. You cannot *shout* fear away. Fear is not loud. Fear is small and cold and lonely." He tapped his stick. "What does a frightened thing need?"

Coco thought about it. He thought about all the times *he'd* felt out of place, melting alone in the valley, nobody's snowcone, belonging nowhere.

"...A friend," he said softly. "A friend, and something beautiful to look at, so the scary part has to share the room."

Don Café's eyes twinkled like two little coffee-colored estrellas. "Now *there* is a cool head with a warm heart. Go on. Brew it slowly."

---

So Coco did it slow, for once in his fizzy life.

He skated quietly to the bushes — no shouting — and he sat right down on the cold ground beside them. He didn't pull anybody out. He just sat.

"Y'know," he said, real soft, "I used to be scared too. Not of the dark. Of being the only melty thing in a place full of people who weren't melty." He looked up. "Felt real lonely. Like the dark, kinda. Big and empty."

The armadillo ball unrolled the tiniest crack. One eye peeked out.

"But then I came up here," Coco went on, "and I found out the dark isn't empty at all. It's the *opposite* of empty. Wanna see? You don't gotta come out. Just... look up where I'm pointing. Just one peek."

Slowly, slowly — slow as Slowy the Sloth on a sleepy Sunday — the little volcano mouse turned her face to the sky.

"There," whispered Coco. "See that one bright estrella? The shiny one? That's not the dark. That's a *star.* And there's another. And another. The dark isn't full of scary stuff, friends." He swept his frosty arm across the whole spangled sky. "It's full of *light.* You just gotta hold still long enough to let your eyes find it."

The frogs peeked. The quail peeked. And the armadillo unrolled all the way, sat up on his little tail, and stared up with his mouth open.

"There are... *so many,*" he breathed.

"Hundreds," said Coco. "Thousands. And watch *this.*"

He skated to the edge of the crater pool and gestured down at the still water. The whole star-stuffed sky shone back up from below.

"TWO skies!" gasped the mouse. "One up and one down!"

"Estrellas everywhere," said Coco, grinning. "Up, down, all around. The dark's just the comfy blanket they sleep under. And here's the best part, mountain friends." He plopped down between them, his cold shoulders against their warm fuzzy ones, and the powdered-milk snow on his head sparkled in the starlight. "You don't gotta be scared of it alone. 'Cause now you've got each other. And you've got me."

---

They stayed up half the night, the lot of them, lying on the cool crater rim counting estrellas. Don Café told slow gentle stories about each star — that bright one was a coffee farmer's lantern, that little cluster was a family of fireflies who flew too high and decided to stay, and that wide milky smudge across the middle of the sky was, he said, "spilled granizado syrup from the very first snowcone, who got so excited about the view that he tipped his whole cup over the heavens." Coco gasped. "That's the most *me* thing I've ever heard."

And one by one, the night sounds stopped being scary. *Crick-crick* turned out to be a cricket no bigger than a sesame seed, who came over to introduce himself and apologize for the racket. *Rustle-rustle* was just the ferns having a sleepy conversation with the wind. And when a soft *whoo-whoo* floated down, the critters held their breath — until a chubby, fluffy little mountain owl with enormous surprised eyes drifted onto a branch, looked at the whole crowd, and said, very politely, "Oh! Are we stargazing? I *love* stargazing. May I join? I'll be quiet, I promise. Whoo." And the frogs laughed so hard at how un-scary the scary whoo had turned out to be that two of them got the hiccups.

Coco caught a falling star reflection in the pool and pretended to slurp it up like syrup, which made the frogs laugh so hard *again* that they fell off a rock (gently, into moss, completely fine, giggling the whole way down).

The quail nestled into Coco's frosty side. "I'm not scared anymore," she said.

"Nope," said Coco. "And next dark night?"

"We'll look *up,*" peeped the frogs.

"Better together," said Don Café, which is exactly what his old friend Pinto the bean always says, in his own way, far down in the warm valley.

The armadillo had rolled himself into a ball again — but this time it was just because he was sleepy and cozy and full of stars, and Coco tucked him gently into the crook of his cold little arm like the world's roundest, happiest pillow.

Up above, the estrellas glittered. Down below, they glittered right back. And in between, on the misty rim of Poás, a snowcone who finally felt at home held his warm new friends close and decided this — *this* — was the most tuanis night of his entire frosty life.

"Stay frosty," Coco whispered to the sleeping critters, "stay sweet. And never, ever be scared of the dark again. There's too much light hiding in it."

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, the dark is not empty — it is a soft blanket sprinkled with a thousand estrellas, just waiting for your eyes to find them. When the room goes quiet and the night grows wide, hold still and breathe, and let the light come twinkling in. You are not alone in the dark; you are tucked beneath the same starry sky that holds the whole sleepy world. May your heart be warm and your head be cool, and may you dream of two skies — one above you, one below — both full of gentle light. Pura vida, dear one. Goodnight.
