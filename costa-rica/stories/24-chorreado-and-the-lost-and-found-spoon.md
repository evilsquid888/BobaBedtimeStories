# Story 24: Chorreado and the Lost-and-Found Spoon

**Main Character:** Chorreado the Corn Pancake
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A misty Monteverde cloud-forest kitchen at first light, where the morning natilla and breakfast things live on a wooden shelf
**Theme:** Asking for help is a brave thing, not a weak one.
**Spanish Spotlight:** ayuda (ah-YOO-dah) = help

---

High in the Monteverde cloud forest, where the fog comes to bed down between the trees like a sleepy white cat, there lived a corn pancake named Chorreado who believed — with his whole golden, slightly lumpy heart — that he could do absolutely everything by himself.

And he was, you should know, a very fine-looking pancake. Round as the moon and twice as cheerful, he was the warm yellow of freshly ground **maíz** (mah-EES) = corn, freckled all over with little toasty-brown spots where the griddle had kissed him. His edges were lacy and crisp. His middle was soft as a pillow. When he sat steaming on the wooden shelf at first light, he smelled like a sunrise that had decided to become breakfast.

"Good morning, shelf!" he announced, puffing himself up. "No need to fuss over me. I am the most independent breakfast in this entire kitchen."

And then, because mornings love a good joke, his only wooden spoon vanished.

---

Now, you have to understand something about Chorreado. A corn pancake cannot cook himself all the way through by simply sitting there feeling proud. At some point — and that point is very important — he must be *flipped*. One side golden, then *whoop*, over he goes, and the other side golden too. Without a flip, a pancake is only half a pancake: toasty on the bottom, pale and doughy on top, and absolutely nobody's idea of a finished breakfast.

The flipping had always been done by his wooden spoon. A trusty, well-loved spoon, worn smooth as a river stone.

But this morning the spoon was simply *gone*.

Chorreado looked left. He looked right. He looked under his own lacy edge, which is a difficult thing for a pancake to do and made him feel slightly dizzy.

No spoon.

"Right," he said, very briskly, the way you talk when you are pretending not to be worried. "No matter. I'll flip myself. How hard could it possibly be?"

This, dear reader, is the kind of sentence that the morning fog finds *hilarious*.

His first idea was to *roll*. He tucked in his crispy edges, took a deep breath of misty cloud-forest air, and rolled himself sideways across the shelf like a runaway tortilla.

He rolled. He gathered speed. The whole shelf blurred past — the salt dish, a sleepy lime, a jar of honey that wobbled in alarm. He felt magnificent. He felt like a champion. He felt, for one shining second, like the most acrobatic breakfast in all of Monteverde.

Then he ran out of shelf.

He landed *splat*, flat on his face, exactly the same side up as before.

"Ah," said Chorreado, into the wood, in a slightly squashed voice. "A practice attempt. A warm-up roll. The real flip — the *masterful* flip — comes next."

From her warm clay home beside him, the pot of **natilla** (nah-TEE-yah) = sour cream wobbled gently. "Chorreado, dear, you have a little shelf-dust on your nose."

"I meant to do that," said Chorreado, who had not meant to do that.

---

His second idea was to *bounce*.

He scrunched himself down small, squishing his pillowy middle until he was tight as a coiled spring, and then — *boing!* — sprang straight up into the foggy air. For one glorious moment he hung there, spinning, the whole misty kitchen turning around him like a carousel: the window, the shelf, the ferns, the window again.

He had time to think one proud thought: *See? Nobody helps Chorreado. Chorreado helps Chorreado.*

He did not have time to think the second thought, which was going to be about *how to land*.

Then gravity, which helps everyone whether they ask or not, reached up and brought him firmly back down.

*Splat.* Same side up. If anything, slightly *more* the same side up than before, as though the morning were teasing him on purpose.

A small grey creature with enormous eyes and ears like two soft satellite dishes poked its head out from behind a coffee tin. It was a cloud-forest mouse, the curious sort that watches everything and judges nothing.

"That," said the mouse, blinking slowly, "looked like it hurt."

"It was a *technique*," said Chorreado, with as much dignity as a face-down pancake can manage.

His third idea was the silliest of all, and I will tell you about it only because you seem trustworthy.

He decided to ask the *fog* for a flip.

"Oh, mighty cloud-forest fog," he called out, "you who drift so freely through the trees — would you kindly gust *underneath* me and turn me over?"

The fog, being fog, did not gust. Fog never gusts. Fog simply drifts about looking mysterious and being absolutely no help with breakfast whatsoever. It curled around his crispy edges, ruffled them a little, tickled his freckles, and then — having considered his request very seriously for about half a second — drifted off to go be mysterious among the ferns instead, the way fog always does the moment you actually need something from it.

*Splat* number three. Chorreado hadn't even gotten off the ground for that one. He'd just sort of flung himself hopefully into the mist and flopped.

---

By now the first light had grown into proper morning. Through the kitchen window, the cloud forest glittered with a thousand beads of dew, and somewhere a tiny green hummingbird was buzzing about its very busy day. The whole world seemed to be getting *on with things*.

And Chorreado was still half-cooked.

His bottom was a beautiful toasty gold. His top was pale, doughy, and — if he was honest, which he was trying very hard not to be — a little bit sad.

The natilla pot peered over at him kindly. "Chorreado," she said, "you have tried rolling, bouncing, and *negotiating with weather*. Perhaps there is one thing you have not tried."

"Impossible," said Chorreado. "I have tried everything a clever, independent pancake can try."

"Have you tried," said the natilla pot gently, "asking?"

Chorreado went very quiet.

You see, this was the hard part. The truly hard part — harder than rolling, harder than bouncing, harder than the splats. Because Chorreado had spent his whole short crispy life being the breakfast who needed *no one*. The independent one. The do-it-myself one. And somewhere along the way he had gotten the idea — a wrong idea, a sticky little idea — that asking for help would mean he wasn't special anymore. That it would make him *less*.

His doughy top wobbled. His crispy edges drooped. He thought about all the mornings he had announced, so proudly, that he needed no one. He thought about how nice it had felt to be the independent one. And he thought, with a little squeeze in his soft middle, how very lonely it suddenly seemed.

"But," he whispered, so softly the fog leaned back in to hear, "what if asking means I'm not... enough? All by myself? What if the most independent breakfast on the shelf isn't allowed to need a hand?"

The cloud-forest mouse crept closer on its tiny pink feet. "I ask for help all the time," it said. "That's how I know where the good crumbs are."

From the back of the shelf came a deep, warm, rumbly voice — the voice of Don Café, the old enamel coffee pot, who had been quietly perking and listening this whole while. Steam curled from his spout like a wise eyebrow.

"Pequeño," rumbled Don Café — that means *little one* — "in all my years on this shelf, I have learned one thing about the bravest cooks. They are not the ones who never need a hand." He gave a warm, gurgling chuckle. "They are the ones who are brave enough to *ask* for one."

Chorreado looked at the natilla pot. He looked at the wide-eyed mouse. He looked at old steaming Don Café.

He took the biggest breath a small corn pancake has ever taken.

And in a tiny, wobbling, golden little voice, he said the bravest word he had ever said:

"...*ayuda*?"

*Ayuda* (ah-YOO-dah) = help.

Well.

You have never seen a shelf move so fast.

---

The natilla pot rocked herself sideways until she could see down behind the shelf. "I can't spot the spoon from here — mouse, you're the explorer. Where do small lost things hide?"

The mouse's enormous ears swiveled like radar. "Small lost things," it said importantly, "always slide *down* and tuck *under*." And quick as a thought it scampered along the shelf, down a table leg, and across the floor to where the big rough coffee sacks slumped against the wall.

It sniffed. It dug. It vanished entirely under a sack, with only its skinny tail sticking out and wiggling.

"¡Aquí!" came its muffled squeak. *Here!*

And out it dragged — by both paws, walking backward, very pleased — the lost wooden spoon, worn smooth as a river stone, dusty but absolutely whole.

"Now then," boomed Don Café. He gave a great gurgle and a *whoosh* of steam, and the steam nudged the spoon up, up, up onto the shelf. The natilla pot caught it with a wobble. The mouse hopped underneath to give it a lift. And together — *together*, all of them, the pot and the mouse and the steam-breathing coffee pot — they slid the trusty spoon beneath Chorreado's golden edge.

"Ready?" said the natilla pot.

"Ready," whispered Chorreado, who had never felt so ready in his life.

*Whoop.*

Up he went, turning gently through the misty morning air — but this time he wasn't alone up there. This time a whole kitchen was lifting him. And he came down softly, doughy-side-to-the-griddle at last, and lay there listening to himself sizzle.

The pale top turned gold. The gold turned toasty. The toasty bloomed into a hundred happy little freckles.

And Chorreado the Corn Pancake was, at last, completely and perfectly cooked, all the way through, on both his beautiful sides.

---

He felt warm clear to his middle. And it wasn't only the griddle. It was something else — the cozy, glowing feeling of having let people in.

"Gracias," he said, looking round at them all — the natilla pot, the mouse with its dusty whiskers, kind old Don Café. *Thank you.* "I thought asking would make me smaller. But I feel..." He searched for it. "I feel *bigger*. Like I belong to the whole shelf now, not just to myself."

"That," said Don Café, with a satisfied gurgle, "is exactly how it works, pequeño. Needing each other isn't the opposite of being special." He sent up one last curl of steam. "It's how a shelf becomes a *family*."

The cloud forest brightened. The fog drifted off to nap among the ferns. And on the wooden shelf, a golden little pancake — flipped, finished, and never again too proud to squeak *ayuda* — glowed like a small warm sun, ready at last for the most joyful ending a breakfast can hope for: being gobbled up, every freckled crumb, by someone very, very hungry and very, very glad.

---

## Goodnight Blessing

May you be brave enough to say *ayuda* when you need it, and warm enough to come running when someone says it to you. May your hard parts be flipped gently, your soft parts be cooked just right, and may you always, always belong to a shelf full of people who love you. The fog is tucking in the trees. The spoon is resting. You are not alone, little one — you never were.

Goodnight.
