# Story 34: Rondon and the Borrowed Pot

**Main Character:** Rondon the Caribbean Coconut Stew
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A breezy Caribbean-coast beach kitchen at dusk where the big cooking pot is shared
**Theme:** Letting go of a grudge makes room for friendship again.
**Spanish Spotlight:** **amistad** (ah-mees-TAHD) — friendship

---

On the warm Caribbean coast, where the palm trees clack their leaves like castanets and the sand stays toasty long after sunset, there lived a stew so cheerful he practically sang when he bubbled. His name was Rondon, and he was a thick, golden coconut stew the color of melted sunshine — flecked with orange dumplings that bobbed like tiny life rafts, ribbons of green plantain, and little stars of red pepper that winked when the steam rolled by. When Rondon simmered happily, he smelled like coconut milk, lime leaves, and the kind of evening where nobody has to go to bed early.

But this particular dusk, Rondon was NOT singing.

He was sulking. Bubble. Sulk. Bubble. Sulk.

And the reason for all this glum bubbling was sitting right beside him, gleaming in the last orange light: the big shared cooking pot, with one large, embarrassing **abolladura** (ah-boh-yah-DOO-rah) — a dent — right in its round silver belly.

"Hmph," went Rondon, and a single grumpy bubble popped at the top of him. *Plip.*

You see, the big pot belonged to everyone on the beach. That was the whole point of it. It was so wide that four pelicans could have taken a bath in it, and so deep that when you stirred the bottom you needed a paddle, not a spoon. Every creature on the coast cooked in that pot at one time or another — the crabs made their pepperpot, the iguanas made their callaloo, and Rondon, of course, made himself.

But three weeks ago, the Pelican had borrowed it.

"He said he needed it for *one afternoon*," Rondon muttered to a passing sand flea, who did not especially want to hear about it but was too polite to fly off. "ONE afternoon. To carry fish, he said. And what did he do? He swooped, he dropped it on the rocks, and *clang* — there's a dent the size of my dumpling. My favorite dumpling, I might add."

The sand flea made a sympathetic noise and escaped at the first opportunity.

Rondon had been cross ever since. Not loud-cross. Not stomping-and-shouting cross. Just the quiet, simmery kind of cross that sits at the bottom of your pot and refuses to float away. Every time the Pelican waddled by to say hello, Rondon turned his bubbles the other direction and pretended to be very busy thickening.

"Buenas, Rondon!" the Pelican would call, ruffling his feathers hopefully. "Lovely steam tonight!"

"Hmph," Rondon would reply, which is a very hard thing for a stew to say, and it takes practice.

Now, on this particular dusk, the whole beach had gathered for the seaside supper. The crabs scuttled up with their forks. The iguanas brought lime wedges. The little hermit hatchlings rolled in carrying their own bowls on their backs, which was both adorable and slightly impractical. Everyone was hungry, and everyone looked at Rondon, all golden and bubbling, and licked their lips.

"Will you share tonight, Rondon?" asked the oldest crab kindly. "We've all brought something for the table."

And here is where I must tell you, dear reader, that even the bubbliest stew can have a stubborn streak. Rondon looked at the big dented pot. He looked at the Pelican, who had landed quietly at the edge of the crowd, shuffling his big webbed feet.

"No," said Rondon. "I'm not in a sharing mood. Some folks dent things and don't even fix them. Some folks borrow and break. And I, for one, would rather simmer alone."

A little gasp went around the beach. The hermit hatchlings drooped their tiny antennae. The Pelican looked at the sand.

"Suit yourself," said the crab gently, and the gathering grew quiet and a bit sad.

And THEN — because the sky has a sense of timing — the wind changed.

The palms stopped clacking and started thrashing. The toasty sand went cold. Fat silver raindrops came down all at once, *pat-pat-PAT-PAT*, the way Caribbean rain does, like the whole ocean had decided to take a turn falling from above. Within a breath, everyone on the beach was soaked, shivering, and *very* hungry, which is the worst combination of feelings there is.

The crabs huddled. The iguanas' teeth chattered. The hermit hatchlings tucked all the way into their shells and squeaked.

"We have to cook something warm, fast!" cried the crab. "Quick — into the little pots!"

But the little pots were too small. One held three dumplings. Another held a single lime. There was no pot wide enough, no pot deep enough, to feed a whole shivering, soggy beach all at once.

No pot, that is, except one.

Everybody turned and looked at the big shared pot — the one with the dent. The one Rondon had been glaring at all afternoon.

"It's the only one big enough," whispered the crab.

Rondon felt something tighten down in his bubbly bottom. The dent. The borrowed pot. The grudge he'd been simmering for three whole weeks. He looked around at all the cold, hungry, hopeful faces — at the hatchlings shivering in their shells, at the iguanas dripping, at the old crab trying to be brave.

And then a wet, droopy, very sorry Pelican stepped forward into the rain.

In his beak — held carefully, like a treasure — he carried three fat, fresh, silvery fish.

"Rondon," said the Pelican, and his voice wobbled. "I'm sorry about the dent. I've been sorry for three weeks. I flew too fast and I dropped the pot and I never said it right, so I'm saying it now, in the rain, in front of everybody. *Lo siento.* I'm sorry. I brought fish. I thought — maybe — we could cook together. If you'd let me."

And he set the fish down at Rondon's edge, all hopeful and dripping, with rain rolling off his big silly beak.

Now, Rondon had a choice. He could hold onto that dent forever. He could keep it down at the bottom of himself where the grudges live, hard and cold like a lump of unmelted coconut. OR.

He looked at the dent again. And do you know what? In the rain, with the lamplight wobbling on the wet silver, that dent didn't look so terrible after all. It just looked like a pot that had been *used*. A pot that had carried fish for a friend. A pot with a little story in its side.

"Oh," said Rondon softly, and a warm bubble rose up through him — not a grumpy one this time, but a glad one. *Ploop.* "Oh, what was I thinking. It's just a dent. And I have been sitting here being cold to a friend over a little dent, when the whole point of a pot is to share what's in it."

He felt the grudge loosen. He felt it lift right up off the bottom of him, light as steam, and float out the open kitchen toward the dark waves, where the sea swallowed it with a quiet *shloop* and that was the very last anyone ever saw of it.

"Pelican," said Rondon, "pour me in. Dent and all."

The whole beach cheered.

And oh, what a supper they made! The Pelican tipped Rondon gently into the big dented pot, and the fresh fish went in, and the crabs added their peppers, and the iguanas squeezed their limes, and the hermit hatchlings sprinkled in a brave pinch of salt. Rondon bubbled and rolled and welcomed every single ingredient like an old friend, growing thicker and richer and more golden than he'd ever been simmering alone. The dent, it turned out, made the pot sit at the perfect tilt for stirring. Funny how that works.

The rain drummed on the roof. The lamplight glowed. And in the wide warm middle of that borrowed, dented, shared pot, Rondon understood something he'd been too cross to see all afternoon: that letting go of an old grudge doesn't make you smaller — it makes *room*. Room for fish, room for friends, room for **amistad** (ah-mees-TAHD) — friendship — to bubble back up warm.

"To the dent!" cried the old crab, raising a lime.

"To the dent!" laughed everyone, and even the Pelican, and especially Rondon.

When at last the supper was ready, the whole beach gathered close around the steaming pot, fur and feathers and shells all dripping but cozy now, and they dipped their bowls into Rondon, who had never in all his bubbling days tasted so happy. Each spoonful was coconut and lime and fresh fish and forgiveness, which is, if you ask any cook on the Caribbean coast, the very best flavor of all.

The Pelican sat closest of anyone, his big beak resting near the pot's warm rim. "Best supper I ever helped make," he said.

"Couldn't have made it without your dent," said Rondon, and they both laughed so hard a little stew sloshed out, which only made them laugh harder.

And that, my dear sleepy one, is the story of how a coconut stew let a grudge float out to sea — and got a whole beach full of friends in return.

## Goodnight Blessing

May you let the little grumps float off your shoulders like steam off a warm pot. May your dents make good stories instead of sore spots. And may you always have room — in your bowl and in your heart — for one more friend to bubble close. Snuggle down now, soft as coconut milk, warm as the lamplight on the shore. The grudge has gone out to sea, the supper is shared, and everyone is cozy.

Goodnight.
