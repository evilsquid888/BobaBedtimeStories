# Story 15: The Cold Thing in the Snow

**Main Character:** Bǎi (Passion fruit)
**Guest Characters:** Teddy & Tilda Powderpuff, Herschel the Walrus, Sergeant Solveig Snowmane, Auntie Hù
**Setting:** The back ridge of Frostpeak Valley on a grey, still afternoon — the old back-ridge lift, the deep untracked snow beneath it, and the long run down to Herschel's Cocoa Hut
**Theme:** Some things aren't a dare. Some things are a Stop.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **別碰** (bié pèng) = don't touch
**Safety Skill:** A found gun — STOP. Don't touch. Leave the area. Tell an adult.

---

"Bet you won't lick the ski pole," said Bǎi.

Bǎi was a passion fruit — round and wrinkly and purple-brown on the outside, with a grin that suggested her insides were bright orange and full of crunchy seeds and *ideas*. She said "Bet you won't" the way other people said "Good morning." It was how she started conversations. It was how she ended them. Once, she'd said it to a vending machine, and the vending machine had lost.

Teddy and Tilda Powderpuff, the twin polar bear cubs, looked at the ski pole. It was metal. It was very, very cold. It had been sitting in the snow all night, and now it had a faint blue shimmer along its whole length, like it was quietly daring them back.

"That's a bad idea," said Tilda.

"It's a *terrible* idea," said Teddy.

They looked at each other.

"We didn't mean to—" said Tilda, already leaning in.

"—but it's gonna be worth it," said Teddy, already leaning in.

*Thwip.*

*Thwip.*

There was a pause. It was the kind of pause that has a lot of thinking in it.

"Mmmf," said Teddy.

"MMMMF," said Tilda, which was a completely different word and also the same word.

Bǎi lay down in the snow and laughed until her seeds rattled. Two polar bear cubs. One ski pole. Four wide eyes and two tongues, stuck fast, so that when Tilda tried to shake her head the whole pole went with it, and Teddy went with the pole, and the three of them together looked like the world's worst wind chime.

"Pucker up, buttercup!" Bǎi gasped. "Oh, this is my best one. This is my *masterpiece.*"

"MMF MMF MMMMF," said Tilda, which meant *get it off get it off get it off*, and Bǎi understood her perfectly, and did not get it off for another whole minute, because that was the funniest minute available.

---

They got unstuck, eventually. It involved Bǎi breathing warm air on the pole, which she did while giggling, which took twice as long. Both cubs came away with tongues that felt fuzzy and a firm new opinion about metal.

"Never again," said Teddy.

"Never *ever,*" said Tilda.

"Bet you won't jump off the back-ridge lift," said Bǎi.

"OKAY," said both cubs at once.

That was the trouble with Bǎi, and the trouble with the twins, and the trouble with putting them together on a grey afternoon with nothing to do. Bǎi had never met a dare she wouldn't say. The twins had never met a dare they wouldn't take. It was like handing a match to a match.

So they rode the old back-ridge lift — a creaky two-seater that swung over the deepest, quietest, least-skied snow in the whole valley. When the chair dipped low over a soft white drift, Bǎi shouted "NOW!" and all three of them tumbled off into the powder, *pomf pomf pomf*. They lay there laughing in three cub-and-fruit-shaped holes while the empty chair swung away above them, creaking, like it was disappointed.

It was very quiet down there. That was the first thing Bǎi noticed, once the laughing stopped. The back ridge was so far from the lodge that you couldn't hear the market, or the cocoa hut, or Finnegan. Just wind, and the *creak… creak…* of the lift, and snow that nobody had touched.

"Bet you won't—" Bǎi began.

"Hey," said Tilda. "What's that?"

---

She had rolled over onto her stomach to climb out of her hole, and her paw had pushed through the fresh snow into the old crust underneath, and there was something there. Something that wasn't snow, and wasn't a rock, and wasn't a ski pole.

Something dark and heavy and cold.

She brushed a little more snow away.

It was a gun.

Not a toy. Not a water pistol, not a snowball launcher, not a bright orange thing from the gift shop. A real one — grey-black and dull and half-buried, lying there in the snow as if it had been waiting.

For a second, nobody said anything at all.

Then Teddy said, "Whoa," in a voice that was trying to sound excited and didn't quite make it.

And Bǎi — because she was Bǎi, because it was what her mouth did before her brain caught up — heard herself say:

"Bet you won't pick it up."

---

Here is what happened in the next three seconds, and it's the most important three seconds in this whole story.

Tilda's paw moved toward the cold thing.

Teddy's eyes went from the gun to Bǎi, waiting for the grin, the *pucker up*, the laugh that meant it was all a game.

And Bǎi looked at their faces.

Really looked. The way Aiyu had been teaching everyone at the stand — *head up, eyes open.* Two small polar bear faces. Fuzzy tongues. Eyes that trusted her completely, the way you trust the person who's always, always been the one who knows how the fun works.

And Bǎi heard the thing she'd just said, all the way through, as if someone else had said it.

*Bet you won't pick it up.*

Pick up a *gun.*

Something went cold inside Bǎi that had nothing to do with the snow. And for the first time in her entire dare-shaped life, she said the opposite word.

"STOP," she said. "Tilda. Stop. **別碰** (bié pèng) — *don't touch it.*"

Tilda's paw froze in the air.

"But you said—"

"I know what I said. I take it back. I'm un-daring it." Bǎi's voice was shaking a little, and she hated that, and she kept going anyway. "That's not a dare. That's a *Stop.* That's a — that's a real one. We don't touch that. Nobody touches that."

"It's just *lying* there," said Teddy. "Somebody could step on it. We could just move it somewhere safe—"

"Moving it is touching it," said Bǎi, and she didn't know how she knew that, except that Auntie Hù had said it once by the tea-egg pot about something else, and it had stuck. "Get back. Both of you. Get back with me."

They did. All three of them, backing up, out of their holes, until there were five long steps of clean snow between them and the cold thing, and then ten.

"Now what?" whispered Tilda.

Bǎi swallowed. "Now we go get a grown-up. Fast. And we don't come back here."

---

They went down the back ridge the way you go when you're small and scared and trying not to be — half sliding, half running, tumbling, getting up, not laughing. The twins had their skis on and Bǎi didn't, so Tilda grabbed one of Bǎi's stubby little arms and Teddy grabbed the other and they towed her down the mountain between them like a purple sled with opinions.

"We didn't mean to find it—" panted Teddy.

"—and it was NOT worth it," panted Tilda.

The Cocoa Hut appeared below them, glowing yellow in the grey. Herschel the Walrus was at the counter, polishing a mug the size of a bucket, his red knitted hat pulled down over his ears. He looked up and saw three small shapes coming down the hill in a way that small shapes do not usually come down hills. He put the mug down before they'd even reached him.

"Herschel," gasped Bǎi. "There's a — under the back lift — in the snow — it's a *gun.* A real one. We didn't touch it. We didn't touch it. I *almost* — I said — but we didn't touch it."

Herschel's enormous whiskers went absolutely still.

"Where exactly?"

Tilda told him. Under the low dip, past the third tower, in the deep drift. She was very precise. She had, it turned out, been paying attention.

Herschel picked up the lodge phone and said, "Solveig. Back ridge. Now." And then he came around the counter — which he almost never did, being roughly the size of a sofa — and he laid one great flipper across both cubs' heads and the other on top of Bǎi, and said:

"You did that exactly right."

"I said *pick it up,*" said Bǎi, very quietly. "I said it. Before I said stop."

Herschel looked at her for a long moment.

"And which one did you *do?*" he said.

---

Sergeant Solveig Snowmane went up the back ridge alone. She was gone forty minutes. When she came back down, whatever she'd found was somewhere it couldn't hurt anybody, and her face was calm the way it always was, which somehow made everyone else calm too.

"Hunter's sled," she said, sitting down heavily at the counter and accepting a cocoa. "Came through three weeks ago, before the last big snow. Lost it off the back and never knew. Been sitting under the drift ever since."

"So it wasn't — nobody *put* it there?" said Teddy. "It was an accident?"

"It was an accident." Solveig took a sip. "Doesn't matter."

"It doesn't?"

"Not one bit." She set the mug down and looked at the three of them, one at a time — two cubs and a passion fruit, all suddenly very small on their stools. "A gun doesn't know why it's lying in the snow. It doesn't know if you're a hunter or a cub. It doesn't know if you're joking. It can't tell a dare from a person. That's why the rules are the same *every single time,* whether somebody dropped it or lost it or hid it. Four things. Say them with me."

She held up one big white claw at a time.

"**Stop.**"

"Stop," said the twins.

"**Don't touch it.**"

"Don't touch it."

"**Leave the area.**"

"Leave the area."

"**Tell a grown-up.**"

"Tell a grown-up," said Bǎi, and found that her voice had stopped shaking somewhere around *leave.*

"And if a friend says *let's just move it?*" said Solveig.

"Moving it is touching it," said Bǎi. Auntie Hù — who had arrived at some point with a tray of tea eggs and was sitting on the end of the counter with her arms folded — said "Mm," in the way that meant *good.*

"Can you tell if it's loaded?" asked Tilda. "Just by looking?"

"No," said Solveig. "Nobody can. Not me, not a hunter, not anyone. You don't look. You *leave.*"

"It didn't look like a TV one," said Teddy, frowning. "TV ones are shiny."

"Real ones aren't for looking at," said Solveig. "So they don't try."

---

The grey afternoon turned into a grey evening, and then a soft dark one, with snow starting up again outside the Cocoa Hut windows. The twins had gone home with their mother, who had hugged them for a very long time and then, Bǎi suspected, had a very serious talk about back-ridge lifts.

Bǎi stayed. She sat on her stool and looked at her cocoa and didn't drink it.

"You're quiet," said Auntie Hù. "You're never quiet."

"I say 'bet you won't' about everything," said Bǎi. "*Everything.* I said it about the vending machine. I said it about the ski pole. I said it about—" She stopped. "I didn't even *think.* It just came out. What if I hadn't looked at their faces? What if Tilda had been faster?"

Auntie Hù considered this seriously, the way she considered everything.

"Let me tell you what I saw," she said. "I saw a kid who's been playing one game her whole life. And today, for the first time, she played a different one. She saw the thing. She *stopped.* She said the word. She un-dared." Auntie Hù shrugged her small marbled shoulders. "Most people never learn there's a second game. You learned it in three seconds, under a lift, with nobody watching. Mm. That's not bad."

"But the first thing I said was still the wrong thing."

"The first thing is a habit. The *second* thing is a choice." Auntie Hù tapped the counter. "You'll always have the habit, probably. But now you've got the choice too. And the choice is faster than you think."

Bǎi thought about that.

Then, slowly, the corner of her wrinkly purple mouth began to turn up.

"Auntie Hù," she said. "Bet you won't finish your cocoa before I finish mine."

"Mm," said Auntie Hù, and picked up her mug. "You're on."

---

Late that night, curled up on the Cocoa Hut's window bench under Herschel's spare blanket — too tired to walk the forty steps back to the stand — Bǎi lay awake for a while, running the three seconds over and over, until she'd worn them smooth.

And somewhere in there she figured out something new — something she was going to try out on the twins first thing tomorrow, and on Finnegan, and on Zhīma, and on anyone else who'd listen.

*Bet you won't be the one who goes and gets a grown-up.* That was a dare too.

It was, she decided, drifting off, a *much* better one.

## Goodnight Blessing

Some things aren't a dare, little one. Some things are a Stop.
You know the difference now — you knew it all along.
Keep your small hands in warm pockets tonight, and let the grown-ups carry the heavy things.
That's what we're for. Sleep.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **If you find a gun: STOP. Don't touch it. Leave the area. Tell a grown-up.** Every time, in that order.
- **Guns aren't toys.** A gun can't tell a dare from a person. It doesn't know if you're joking.
- **You can't tell if it's loaded by looking.** Nobody can — not even a grown-up. So you don't look. You leave.
- **Real guns don't look like TV ones.** They're often dull, dark, and ordinary. If you're not sure — treat it as real.
- **"Just moving it somewhere safe" is still touching it.** Leave it exactly where it is and go.
- **It doesn't matter *why* it's there** — dropped, lost, or hidden. The rules are the same.
- **Un-daring is allowed.** Saying "Stop, that's not a dare" to your friends — or to yourself — is the bravest thing you can do.
