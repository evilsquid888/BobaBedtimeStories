# Story 12: Knife = Distance

**Main Character:** Coco the Granizado (Costa Rican shaved ice)
**Guest Characters:** Piper Paddlefoot, Tico the Toucan, Coach Rosa the Flamingo, Auntie Hù, Boba, Tato the Patacón, Herschel the Walrus
**Setting:** The Pink Belt Academy — the lodge's boot-drying room, hung with pink banners and drying mittens — and then the wide open ice of the frozen lake outside
**Theme:** Against a knife there is exactly one good idea, and it's a big one: be somewhere else.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **距離** (jùlí) = distance
**Safety Skill:** Knife defense — run first; put things between; throw and go; if trapped, control the arm (never the blade); a cut is not the end

---

Tico the Toucan had been asked to introduce the training knife, and Tico did not introduce things. Tico *announced* them.

"LADIES AND GENTLE-PENGUINS! BEARS OF ALL SIZES! ONE SLOTH!" His rainbow beak swung across the boot-drying room like a searchlight. "In THIS corner — weighing NOTHING, made of RUBBER, undefeated in ZERO fights — the one, the only — SEÑOR CUCHIIIIIILLO!"

Coach Rosa the Flamingo held up a floppy grey rubber knife with a smiley face drawn on the handle.

Nobody clapped. Señor Cuchillo flopped over sideways.

"Beak the news," said Tico, a little hurt. "That was a *good* intro."

"It was a wonderful intro," said Rosa. She was seven feet of pink, standing on one leg in a pink headband, and she had the cheerful, dangerous energy of a gym teacher who has had exactly the right amount of coffee. "It was also the last fun thing that is going to happen for about ten minutes. **¡Dale!** — that means *go!*, or *come on!*, or *let's get to it.* Everybody sit. Coco. Up front."

Coco the Granizado slid forward. He was a tall fluffy mound of rainbow-syruped shaved ice in a paper cone, dusted with powdered milk like fresh snow, wearing tiny round sunglasses in a room with no sun. He was the coolest thing in Frostpeak Valley, and he knew it, and it had never once been a problem.

"Stay frosty, stay sweet," he said, and gave the room a lazy thumbs-up.

"No," said Rosa.

Coco's thumb paused.

"With most things, you stay frosty. With a knife—" she flopped Señor Cuchillo at him "—you stay *far.*"

---

Here's a thing about Coach Rosa. She was silly on purpose. She named her heavy bag Señor Saco and put a sombrero on him. She fell asleep on one leg in the middle of sentences. Every single lesson she had ever taught ended with the words "…and then, *shrimp*," and then actual shrimp, which Barnaby Beaksworth supplied from a bag he'd stopped asking questions about.

But when she taught the knife lesson, she did not do a bit. Not one. The kids noticed, the way you notice when a dog stops barking.

"A knife," said Rosa, "is the scariest thing on a street. Scarier than a big man. Scarier than a bat. You know why?"

Boba, bouncing gently in the back: "Because it's pointy?"

"Because it never misses and it never runs out." Rosa turned Señor Cuchillo over in her wing. "A fist can miss. A stick can miss. A knife touches you, it works. Every time. And it doesn't get tired. So we do not get clever with knives. We do not get brave. We get *far.*" She looked around the room. "Say the word."

"Far," said the room.

"**距離** (jùlí). *Distance.* Auntie Hù says distance is your friend. With a knife, distance is your *only* friend. Everything else I teach you today is what you do when your only friend isn't there."

Auntie Hù, on a stack of mittens by the door, said "Mm," which in her language was a whole paragraph.

---

"Rule one," said Rosa. "Coco. A knife has the reach of — what?"

"An arm?"

"An arm. That's it. That's *all.* A knife has the reach of one arm, and you have *legs.*" She pointed the rubber knife at the door. "So if you see a knife and there is room to run — you RUN. Not away from the *knife.* Toward people, toward light, toward a door that locks. You don't stop to think. You don't stop to look brave. Everything in you that says *stay and handle it* is wrong today. Go."

"That's it?" said Coco. "Run? That's the whole—"

"That is the *best* one. Everything after this is worse. Rule two."

She grabbed a boot-drying rack — a tall wire thing full of steaming mittens — and dragged it between herself and Coco with a screech.

"**Put things between.** Chairs. Tables. This. A snowbank. A trash can. Anything a knife has to go *around.* You are trying to buy one more second to get to rule one."

Piper Paddlefoot, who had been fidgeting with her glittery blue snowboard the entire lesson because she could not sit still if you paid her in fish, said, "What about a board?"

"What about it?"

Piper stood up, held the board flat in front of her like a shield, and peered over the top of it with just her eyes.

Rosa looked at her for a long moment.

"That," she said, "is the single best use of a snowboard I have seen all winter. Everyone look at Piper. Piper, never put that down."

"Slide into it," said Piper, muffled, from behind the board.

---

"Rule three," said Rosa. "**Throw things at the face.**"

"*Rude,*" said Coco.

"Very. Rude is cheap." She nodded at Auntie Hù, who nodded back; it was, apparently, a shared motto now. "A face that has a mitten coming at it closes its eyes. A closed-eyes person can't chase for one second. What do you do with one second?"

"Run," said the room.

"Then shr— no. Not yet. Rule four."

And here Rosa put down the rubber knife and looked at the room the way a grown-up looks at you before telling you something they wish you didn't need.

"Rule four is for when you cannot run. When you are in a corner, or a small room, or he is already too close. I want you to understand that rule four is *bad.* It is the worst rule. Nobody good at this *wants* rule four. But you will have it in your pocket, because a kid with a bad plan is better off than a kid with no plan."

"Arm meets *arm.*" She swung Señor Cuchillo slowly at Tato the Patacón, who had volunteered because Tato always volunteered, being twice-fried and afraid of nothing. Tato's forearm came up and met her *forearm* — not the knife — with a crunchy *whap*. "You do not block the knife. You block the *arm* that's holding it. Then—" she stepped in, and Tato stepped in, "—you *burst in*. Not away. In. Away keeps you in the reach. In gets you past it."

"Both hands." Tato grabbed her wing with both of his stubby plantain hands and hung on like a barnacle. "Both hands on the *arm*. Never — Coco, look at me — *never* grab the blade. Not to be brave. Not to *take it.* The arm. Then knee, knee — Tato, that's enough, you're very good — then either you have it, or you shove him and you *RUN*, and what do you do after you run?"

"SCAN!" said the room, and every head swiveled left-right-left like a row of lighthouses.

Tico, who had been quiet for almost four minutes, could not bear it any longer. "ROUND SIX GOES TO THE PATACÓN! WHAT A COMEBACK, FOLKS, FROM ROUND—"

"There were no rounds, Tico."

"—FROM ROUND *ZERO*—"

"Sit down, Tico."

---

Then Rosa said the fifth thing, and she said it quietly.

"You might get cut."

The room went still. Coco felt something happen along his edges — a warmth, a slow slide. One drop of blue-raspberry syrup ran down the side of his cone and landed on the floor.

"I'm not saying that to scare you. I'm saying it because if it happens, and you *didn't know it could,* you'll stop. You'll look down at it and you'll freeze, and freezing is the one thing you must not do." She crouched, one leg folded, until she was at Coco's height. "A cut is not the end. It hurts, it bleeds, it looks worse than it is, and it *does not stop your legs.* You keep going. You get away. You get to people. *Then* you press hard on it with anything — a mitten, a scarf — and you get a grown-up and you say *I need help.* In that order. Away first. Then the cut."

Coco was melting. Not a lot. But a little rainbow puddle had formed around the base of his cone, and his sunglasses had slipped, and for the first time in his entire cool, breezy life he did not feel frosty at all.

"Coach," he said. "I don't think I'm — I'm not good at scared."

"Nobody is good at scared, *tranquilo.*" (That one meant *easy, easy — calm down.*) "You're not supposed to be good at scared. You're supposed to be good at *running while scared.* Those are different sports." She stood back up on one leg. "And you know what? You're a *granizado.* Somebody swings something at you, you're going to melt a little. Fine. Melt *toward the door.*"

Coco looked down at his puddle.

"Melt toward the door," he repeated.

"Melt toward the door. Now. Everybody outside. Señor Cuchillo wants to see the lake."

---

The lake was enormous and white and empty, and the sky over it was going pink to match the coach, and it was the perfect place for the drill because it had one thing the boot room did not: *room.*

"This," said Rosa, sweeping a wing across all that ice, "is what distance looks like. Coco — stand there. I'm the bad guy. Tico, you're announcing. Piper, you're on shield. Everyone else, you are the crowd, and the crowd's job is to be *far away,* which you're all very good at."

"IN THIS CORNER," began Tico, "A GRANIZADO WITH NOTHING TO LOSE BUT HIS TOPPINGS—"

Rosa came at Coco with Señor Cuchillo, slow and floppy and grinning.

Coco ran.

He didn't think about it. He didn't do rule two or three or four. He saw a knife — a rubber one, with a smiley face, held by a flamingo he adored — and every part of him that had been listening for an hour said *legs*, and he went, sliding across the ice in a spray of powdered milk, not away from Rosa but *toward* the cluster of penguins and bears at the shore, toward Herschel with his enormous cocoa vat, toward Auntie Hù on her crate, because that's where people were.

He stopped forty feet away, breathing hard, and turned his head. Left. Right. Behind.

"HE SCANNED, FOLKS!" screamed Tico. "ROUND ONE TO THE CONE!"

"Correct," said Rosa. "That's the whole lesson. Sit down."

"That's — wait. Can I try the other ones? The rack and the throwing and the—"

"No."

"But—"

"Coco." Rosa hopped over to him on one leg, which took a while, and put a wing on his cone. "You did the *best one*. Why would I make you practice the worse ones when you just did the best one perfectly? You'll drill rule four in here—" she tapped his cone "—a hundred times, and I hope you never use it once. Rule one, you *did*. Stay frosty—"

"—and far," said Coco.

"And far."

---

Piper insisted on a shield drill anyway, because Piper had been holding a snowboard in front of her face for an hour and needed *someone* to swing a rubber knife at it. Rosa obliged. Señor Cuchillo bounced off the glittery blue board with a rubbery *boing*, and Piper peered over the top, and said, "I can't see anything, but I feel *incredible*," and Rosa said that was roughly the correct feeling.

Then everyone threw mittens at Tato's face, because rule three needed a volunteer and Tato had never once said no to anything. He stood there with his eyes shut, mittens bouncing off his crispy plantain cheeks, saying "Smash now, worry never!" between hits.

"How's that feel, Tato?"

"Like I can't chase anybody for at least one second!"

"*Exactly.*"

And then, with the sky gone fully pink and the ice gone blue and everyone's breath hanging in little clouds, Rosa stood on her one leg at the center of the lake, closed her eyes, and said the thing she always said.

"…and then, *shrimp.*"

Barnaby produced the bag.

---

That night the Taiwan crew and the Costa Rica crew piled into the back room of Boba on Ice, because the Costa Rica crew's boat-cabins were cold and the boba stand's bunks were warm, and there was a lot of yawning and very little sleeping.

Coco lay on the top bunk with his sunglasses folded on his chest and listened to Piper, below him, still explaining to Boba how a snowboard was basically a door you could carry.

"Coco," said Boba eventually, into the dark. "Were you scared? On the lake? When she came at you?"

Coco thought about the puddle. About the one drop of blue syrup on the boot-room floor.

"Yeah," he said. "I melted a little."

"But you ran anyway."

"I melted *toward the door,*" said Coco, and heard Piper laugh, and felt, for the first time all day, entirely frosty again. "Turns out that's allowed."

"Everything's allowed," said Piper sleepily, "if you're going the right direction."

Outside, the lake lay wide and white and empty under the stars — forty feet of it, a hundred, a thousand — all that beautiful distance, doing exactly what distance does, which is nothing at all, which is everything.

## Goodnight Blessing

Far is a good place to be, little one, and your bed is very far from anything sharp.
You have legs that know the way to people and a head that knows to turn and look.
Tonight there's nothing to run from — only the blanket, only the dark, only the warm.
Stay frosty. Stay far. Sleep.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **Knives are the scariest thing on the street** — they never miss and never run out. So you don't get brave or clever. You get *far.*
- **Rule 1 — RUN.** A knife has the reach of one arm. You have legs. Run *toward* people, light, and a door that locks.
- **Rule 2 — Put things between.** Chairs, tables, a snowbank, a backpack, a snowboard held like a shield. Anything a knife has to go around buys you a second.
- **Rule 3 — Throw things at the face and go.** Closed eyes can't chase.
- **Rule 4 — only if trapped:** block the *arm*, not the knife. Burst *in*, both hands on the arm, knees, then shove and run. **Never grab the blade.**
- **You might get cut. A cut is not the end.** Keep going, get away, get to people — *then* press hard on it and get a grown-up.
- **Melt toward the door.** Being scared is fine. Running while scared is the whole skill. Then SCAN.

## 💬 Talk About It

3. Why did Coach Rosa say a cut "is not the end"? What would you do right after you got away?
