# Story 3: The Smiling Stranger

**Main Character:** Mochi (Mochi)
**Guest Characters:** Barnaby Beaksworth, Auntie Hù, Teddy & Tilda Powderpuff, Herschel the Walrus, Sergeant Solveig Snowmane, the Smiling Stranger (new)
**Setting:** The Frostpeak lodge grounds on a bright snowy Saturday — the sled hill, the back path behind the lodge, and Herschel's Cocoa Hut
**Theme:** Being kind and being obedient are not the same thing. You can say NO to any grown-up — loudly — and still be a good kid.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **不要** (bú yào) = No! / Don't want to!
**Safety Skill:** Tricky people — judge the ask, not the smile; grown-ups don't ask kids for help; family code word

---

By eleven o'clock on Saturday morning, Mochi had hugged forty-one people, two seals, and a snowman.

"Forty-two," said Barnaby Beaksworth, making a mark on his napkin. "You just got the snowman again."

"He looked COLD!" said Mochi.

"He's made of snow."

"That's why he looked cold!"

Mochi was a soft, pillowy white blob dusted with potato starch, with rosy pink cheeks and arms that could stretch as long as she needed them to be — which, if you were within a hundred feet, was exactly long enough to hug you. She loved everyone. She loved everyone *immediately.* Her catchphrase was "Stick together!" and she meant it in the most literal, sticky way possible.

Barnaby, a round chinstrap penguin with an orange pizza-shaped snowboard, loved everyone too — but he loved them at the speed of snacks. He was the kind of penguin who would notice that you hadn't eaten before he noticed your name. He liked to sit at the top of the sled hill with a bag of dried fish and *watch.*

"Forty-three," he said, as Mochi hugged a passing fox.

"Forty-three is a great number," said Mochi. "It's my favorite number. Every number I'm on is my favorite number."

And that, right there, is a wonderful way to be. Hold on to that thought. We're going to need it later.

---

The man came up the back path a little after noon.

He was tall. He wore a nice warm coat. He had a friendly, easy smile — the kind that made you feel like you'd already met — and he was carrying a red leash with no dog on the end of it.

If you are trying to picture his face right now, you'll find you can't quite do it. That's all right. Nobody ever could. He looked like *someone.* He looked like a dad, or a teacher, or a nice uncle. He looked like the kind of person who returns your lost mitten.

"Hey there," he said to Mochi, crouching down so they were eye to eye. "I'm so glad I found somebody. My puppy got loose behind the lodge — little brown one, about this big, answers to Biscuit. I've been looking for an hour. You've got great eyes. Could you help me look? He'd come right to a kid."

Mochi's whole body leaned toward him like a plant toward the sun.

A *puppy.* A *lost* puppy. A puppy named *Biscuit*, who was *cold* and *scared* and *needed her.* This was the best day of Mochi's life. This was what arms were *for.*

"YES!" she said. "Oh, yes, of course, which way did—"

"Wait," said Barnaby.

He had come down the hill without his board. He was standing very close to Mochi, and he was not looking at the man's smile. He was looking at the leash.

"Wait what?" said Mochi. "Barnaby, there's a PUPPY."

"Grown-ups don't ask kids for help," said Barnaby.

He said it the way you'd say *fish live in the sea.* Like a plain fact.

The man laughed, easily. "Well, this grown-up does. I'm just a little desperate, buddy. You want to help too? Plenty of puppy for everybody."

"If you lost a puppy," said Barnaby slowly, working it out as he went, "you'd ask Herschel. He's got a loudspeaker. You'd ask the bears. You'd ask the *lodge.* Grown-ups ask grown-ups. Grown-ups don't need a mochi." He took Mochi's arm — which took a moment, because it was very long. "Come on. We're going to go tell somebody who *can* help."

"Aw, don't be like that," said the man, still smiling, standing up now, which made him very tall. "Kid, you look like you've got a big heart. You wouldn't leave a puppy out in the cold."

And Mochi — soft, sweet, forty-three-hugs Mochi — felt the sentence land on her like a hand on her shoulder. *You wouldn't.* Because she wouldn't. Because being the kind of person who *would* was the worst thing she could imagine.

"I—" she said.

"**不要** (bú yào)," said Barnaby. "It means *no.* Come on."

And he walked her, briskly, back up toward the lodge, and did not look behind them, and did not slow down until they were standing at Herschel's cocoa counter with the walrus's enormous bristly face between them and the whole rest of the world.

---

Herschel listened. Herschel's whiskers went very still. Herschel picked up the lodge phone and said three words into it, and one of them was "Solveig."

Then he poured two cocoas, slid them across, and said, "Sit. You did right."

"I didn't do *anything*," said Mochi miserably. "Barnaby did everything. I was going to *go.*" Her cheeks weren't rosy anymore. "There might really be a puppy."

"Mm," said a voice.

Auntie Hù had come in from the market with a tray of tea eggs. She set it on the counter, climbed up onto a stack of napkins, and looked at Mochi for a long, thoughtful moment.

"Let me ask you something," she said. "When he smiled — was it a good smile?"

"It was a *great* smile," said Mochi. "It was a dad smile."

"Mm. And that's the problem, isn't it. You've been told to watch out for *strangers.* Scary ones. But most strangers are lovely. And the dangerous ones—" she tapped the counter "—look like *that.* Friendly. Ordinary. They *practice* the smile. So the smile tells you nothing. Zero." She held up a small marbled finger. "You don't judge the *person.* You judge the **ask.**"

"The ask?"

"What did he want you to *do?*"

"Help him," said Mochi. "Come with him. Behind the lodge."

"There it is." Auntie Hù counted on her fingers. "*Help me. Come with me. Keep this quiet. Don't tell your parents. Your mom sent me.* Five asks. Any one of those, from a grown-up to a kid, and the answer is NO and you leave. Doesn't matter if he's nice. Doesn't matter if he's crying. Doesn't matter if there's a puppy named Biscuit." She paused. "A real lost-puppy man asks Herschel. Barnaby's right. Grown-ups ask grown-ups."

Mochi was quiet. Then she said the thing that was really bothering her.

"But it's *rude.*"

---

Auntie Hù did not laugh at her. She thought about it seriously, as if it were a very good point, which in a way it was.

"Here's a deal I want you to make with yourself," she said. "Suppose you're wrong. Suppose you say NO — loud, rude, and you walk away — and he really was just a nice man with a lost dog. What happens to him?"

Mochi thought. "He… feels bad?"

"For how long?"

"…Until lunch?"

"Until lunch. He gets over it by lunch." Auntie Hù leaned in. "Now suppose you're *right.* And you were polite instead. Then what?"

Mochi didn't answer. She didn't have to. Herschel's whiskers had gone still again.

"So," said Auntie Hù softly. "Being rude costs a stranger one bad morning. Being polite could cost you everything. That's not even a hard trade. **Rude is cheap.** Buy it every time."

"Polite," said Barnaby, through a mouthful of fish, "is not the same as obedient."

"Mm. Write that down."

---

"The other problem," said Auntie Hù, climbing off the napkins, "is that your NO is broken. Back there it came out as *I—* and a wobble. It sounded like a mouse apologizing. We're going to fix that. Everybody outside."

Which is how the entire back deck of the Cocoa Hut became, for one snowy afternoon, the loudest place in Frostpeak Valley.

"NO," said Auntie Hù. "Feet planted. Hand up. Loud enough to turn heads. Go."

"…no," said Mochi.

"That was a *maybe.* Again."

"No."

"That was *no thank you.* I could hear the *thank you* hiding in it. Again. **不要!**"

"**不要!**" said Mochi — and something happened in her chest, a little hot spark, and her arms, which usually reached *toward* things, came up flat in front of her like a wall. "**不要! NO! I HAVE TO GO!**"

Three cocoa mugs rattled on the counter. A seal fell off the railing. Somewhere up the mountain a small avalanche reconsidered and decided not to.

"Mm," said Auntie Hù, and Mochi had never been so proud of a syllable in her life.

Teddy and Tilda Powderpuff, the polar bear cubs, had wandered over and were watching with enormous interest.

"Can we do it?" said Teddy.

"We're really good at loud," said Tilda.

"We didn't mean to be—" said Teddy.

"—but it's worth it," said Tilda.

"Everybody practices," said Auntie Hù. "And while you're here, cubs — do you have a **code word**?"

They looked at each other. "A what?"

"A secret word, just your family. Silly is better. If anyone *ever* says *your mom sent me to pick you up* — anyone, even someone you know — you say, *What's the code word?* If they don't have it, they weren't sent. You go find your grown-up. No discussion."

"Ours could be *pickle-sled*," said Tilda immediately.

"Ours could be *pickle-sled* but louder," said Teddy.

"Don't tell *me,*" said Auntie Hù. "Tell your mother. Tell nobody else. Ever."

---

Now. Here is the part of the story you may have been expecting, or maybe hoping wouldn't come.

Late in the afternoon, when the light had gone gold and the sled hill was thinning out, the man with the leash tried again.

He didn't try Mochi. Mochi was on the deck, saying **不要** to a snowman for practice. He went, instead, to the two smallest people on the whole hill, who were sitting on their yellow mini-skis eating snow.

"Hey, cubs," he said, warm and easy. "Your mom sent me. She's waiting by the parking lot — she said to bring you right over."

Teddy opened his mouth to say *okay.*

Tilda got there first.

"What's the code word?" she said.

The man's smile stayed exactly the same. That was what Tilda remembered afterward — that it didn't move at all. "Come on, sweetie. She's in a hurry. Let's go."

"WHAT'S," said Tilda, standing up on her skis, "THE CODE WORD."

And when he didn't have it — when the smile finally, finally wobbled — the twins did the thing that everyone had practiced all afternoon. They did not argue. They did not explain. They turned and they *flew* — two yellow streaks straight down the hill, straight to the cocoa counter, straight into the side of Herschel the Walrus. He had been watching the whole time. Now he put one great flipper on each small head and said, in a voice like a mountain, "Solveig. Now."

Sergeant Solveig Snowmane had already been walking. She reached the man at the top of the hill before he got ten steps. What she said to him, nobody heard. What happened to him after, the grown-ups took care of. He left the valley that night and he did not come back, and that is the last this story has to say about him.

"Was he *really*—" Mochi started.

"Doesn't matter," said Auntie Hù. "The twins didn't need to know. That's the beauty of it. They judged the ask."

---

That night, Mochi lay in her bunk in the little room behind Boba on Ice and did not feel like hugging anyone, which was a new and strange feeling, like a tooth missing.

"Am I still allowed?" she asked the dark. "To hug people?"

"Forty-three today," said Barnaby's voice, from the next bunk. He'd counted. Of course he'd counted. "Of course you're allowed. You just get to *pick.* Hugs are yours. Arms are yours. You hand them out because you *want* to, not because somebody told you you'd be a bad kid if you didn't."

Mochi thought about that. About the man's sentence, *you wouldn't,* landing on her like a hand. About how the hot little spark in her chest had knocked it right off.

"Barnaby?"

"Mm-hm?"

"Come here."

Forty-four.

"There's always enough fish for one more," said Barnaby sleepily, which didn't make sense, and made perfect sense, and was the last thing either of them heard before sleep.

## Goodnight Blessing

You are allowed to be kind, little one. You are allowed to be *loud.*
Your arms are yours. Your NO is yours. Your heart opens from the inside.
Tonight there's nothing to say no to — only a pillow, and the dark, and the soft weight of the blankets.
Say yes to that. Sleep.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **Judge the ask, not the smile.** Tricky people look normal and nice. The smile tells you nothing.
- **Grown-ups don't ask kids for help.** Grown-ups ask grown-ups. Lost puppy, directions, carrying something — a real grown-up asks another grown-up.
- **Five red-flag asks:** *Help me. Come with me. Keep this quiet. Don't tell your parents. Your mom sent me.* Any one = NO and leave.
- **Polite is not the same as obedient.** You can say NO to any adult, at full volume, and walk away. **Rude is cheap** — if you're wrong, he's over it by lunch; if you're right, rude just saved you.
- **Fix your NO.** Feet planted, hand up, loud enough to turn heads: "NO! I HAVE TO GO!" Then walk to a safe grown-up — someone working, a parent with kids.
- **Family code word.** Pick a silly one. Nobody who doesn't have it was sent by your parents. Don't discuss — go.
