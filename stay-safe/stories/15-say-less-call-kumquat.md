# Story 15: Say Less, Call Kumquat

**Main Character:** Boba (Tapioca pearl)
**Guest Characters:** Counselor Kumquat, Sergeant Solveig Snowmane, Taro, Cleo Coldwater, Herschel the Walrus, Auntie Hù, Willa Wobble, Sludge, Bùdīng, Aiyu, Teddy & Tilda Powderpuff, Bjorn Bigpaws, Dàn, Aurora Frostholm, and the whole cast of both worlds
**Setting:** The morning after a smashed window at the Frostpeak Winter Night Market — then, that evening, the Frostpeak Safety Fair on the last night of Boba on Ice
**Theme:** Being innocent doesn't mean you have to explain yourself. Asking for a parent and a lawyer is smart, not suspicious — and the whole valley knows what to do now.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **律師** (lǜshī) = lawyer
**Safety Skill:** Talking to police — "I want my parent." "I'd like a lawyer." Complete sentences.

---

The window of the Frostpeak Winter Night Market's souvenir hut was in eleven thousand pieces on the snow, and every single piece was pointing at Boba.

Not literally. Glass doesn't point. But it *felt* that way, because at nine o'clock the night before, the lodge camera had recorded exactly one person bouncing past that window on the way home. A small, round, glossy black-brown person. With tiny arms. Leaving a faint sticky trail.

"That's me," said Boba, watching the footage on Herschel's little screen. "That is one hundred percent me."

"Mm-hm," said Sergeant Solveig Snowmane.

"But I didn't *do* anything! I bounced! I bounce *everywhere!* That's my whole *thing!*"

"Mm-hm," said Solveig again, and wrote something in her notebook, slowly, the way she did everything.

Boba felt his insides fizz. He had never been in trouble before. He had never even been *near* trouble before, unless you counted the time Bīng's tower fell on Finnegan, which nobody did. And the sergeant was so calm and so *big* and so quiet, and she just kept *writing*, and the silence stretched out like a piece of Mochi, and Boba could not — could *not* — stand it.

"Okay, so," he said. "Here's what happened. Let me just explain."

---

"I'd love that," said Solveig, pleasantly. "Just tell me where you were last night. Walk me through it."

And Boba opened his mouth to walk her through it, all of it, every bounce — and a voice from somewhere around his knee said:

"Say less."

Boba looked down.

On the counter beside Herschel's cocoa vat stood a kumquat. She was the size of a large marble, glossy orange, with tiny round glasses perched on her peel and a black briefcase the size of a matchbox in one hand. She had been walking past. She had stopped. She was now looking at Boba the way a cat looks at a bird that is about to do something stupid.

"I'm sorry?" said Boba.

"Say. Less." The kumquat set down her briefcase, climbed on top of it, and looked him dead in the eye. "Counselor Kumquat. Public defender. I heard the words *let me just explain* from across the room, and every hair on my peel stood up. Sergeant, is this young man being questioned?"

"I asked him where he was," said Solveig, mildly.

"Then he'd like his parent, and he'd like a lawyer." The kumquat turned back to Boba. "Say it."

"But I didn't do—"

"*Say it.*"

Boba swallowed. "I… want my parent," he said. "And I'd like a lawyer?"

"Not a question. Again."

"I want my parent. I'd like a lawyer."

"**律師** (lǜshī)," said Counselor Kumquat with satisfaction. "*Lawyer.* And you've got one. Congratulations. Sit down and stop helping."

---

Here is the strange thing, the thing that Boba would remember for the rest of his life.

Solveig *nodded.*

She closed her notebook. She didn't look angry. She didn't look suspicious. She looked, if anything, a little bit *relieved.*

"Good," she said. "Nobody should do this part alone — least of all a pearl. We'll wait for a grown-up." And she went and got herself a cocoa.

"I don't understand," Boba whispered to the kumquat. "Doesn't asking for a lawyer make me look *guilty?*"

"On television," said Counselor Kumquat. "In real life it makes you look like someone with a brain. Here's the thing nobody tells kids. Listen close, because I don't repeat myself. That's a rule. I have a lot of rules."

She counted on her tiny orange fingers.

"One. Anything you say to the police can be written down and used. Not just the guilty parts — *all* the parts. Two. You are nervous. Nervous people tell true stories *badly.* Out of order. With guesses in them." She pointed at him. "What time did you bounce past the window?"

"Um. Around nine? I think? Maybe nine-fifteen—"

"*There.* You just said three different times. You're an honest pearl, and you just gave a police officer three different times, and any one of them can be checked against a camera, and if the camera says eight-fifty, now there's a piece of paper somewhere that says *Boba lied about the time.* You didn't lie. You *guessed.* But guesses become lies on paper. Every day. To innocent people."

Boba's fizz had gone very quiet.

"Three," said the kumquat. "Innocent people ask for lawyers *because* they're innocent. They have the most to lose from a muddle. A lawyer is a translator. You speak *nervous ten-year-old.* The system speaks *paperwork.* I stand in the middle and make sure nothing gets lost going across." She adjusted her glasses. "And four. Whatever you tell *me* is private. Always. Forever. It's the law. So you can tell me the whole messy thing, and I'll help you tell *them* the short true thing."

"That's… a lot of rules."

"I told you. And here's the one for the fridge: **don't run, don't lie, don't fight, be polite. Give your name. Then say 'I want my parent' and 'I'd like a lawyer.' And then say nothing else.**" She hopped off the briefcase. "Those are complete sentences. You don't add *because.* You don't add *but I didn't.* You just stop. Stopping is the hardest thing in the world and it's the whole job."

---

Taro arrived twenty minutes later, moving at the speed of Taro — which is to say, the speed of steam. He was chunky and purple and dusty, with reading glasses on the end of his nose, and he had the calmest face Boba had ever been so glad to see.

"Good things take time to steam," said Taro, settling onto a stool. "I am here. Nobody has to hurry."

With Taro on one side and a kumquat on the other, Boba felt about four times his actual size.

"Okay," said Counselor Kumquat. "Now we do it right. Tell *me* everything. Messy is fine."

So he did. The whole bouncy night — closing the stand, saying goodnight to Auntie Hù, the shortcut past the souvenir hut, a sound like a giant's tooth cracking somewhere above him that he'd thought was the ice on the lake, home, bed.

"A sound," said the kumquat. "Above you."

"I think so? I don't—"

"Don't guess. Did you hear a sound?"

"Yes."

"Was it above you?"

"…Yes."

"Then that's what we say." She turned. "Sergeant. My client passed the hut at closing time, heard a loud crack from above, and went home. He did not touch the window. That's his statement. Do you have a second camera angle, Herschel?"

Herschel the Walrus, who had been listening with his whole enormous face, blinked. "There's the one on the roof. For the icicles."

"*For the icicles,*" repeated Counselor Kumquat, very slowly, and Boba watched Solveig's eyebrows go up.

---

There was, it turned out, a second camera.

And on it, at 9:04 — not nine, not nine-fifteen, 9:04 — an icicle the size of a canoe let go of the lodge roof and came down through the souvenir hut's window like a glass javelin. Two seconds after a small black pearl had bounced out of frame.

Cleo Coldwater, who sketched everything, had drawn that exact icicle the day before because it was "the most beautiful and threatening shape in the valley." Her sketchbook page went into Solveig's notebook. The case went into the *closed* pile.

"So I was fine," said Boba. "I was fine the whole time! I could have just *explained!*"

"Could you," said the kumquat. "You'd have said *nine, maybe nine-fifteen.* You'd have said *I heard the lake ice.* You'd have said *I was bouncing kind of fast* because you're honest and you were. And every one of those would've been a thread for someone to pull. Instead—" she tapped her briefcase "—short, true, checked, done. You were innocent *both* ways. You were only *safe* one way."

Solveig came over and crouched down, huge and white and slow, until she was eye to eye with the pearl.

"Boba. I asked you to explain because that's my job. You asked for a lawyer because that's *your* job. Both of us did it right." She held out a paw. "Slow hands, honest words. Also — sorry about the glass."

"I bounced *right under it,*" said Boba faintly.

"Then you also have very good luck," said Taro. "Which we should not rely on. Which is why we have a Safety Fair tonight."

---

The Frostpeak Safety Fair was Auntie Hù's idea, and it was the last night of Boba on Ice, and the whole valley came.

There were booths. There were *so many* booths.

Aiyu ran the **Four Coats Fashion Show**, in which volunteers modeled White (Finnegan, face in a phone, walked into a post — on purpose, he insisted), Yellow (Willa, head up, ear-muffs off, pointing out five exits), Orange (Teddy, squinting suspiciously at a snowman), and Red (Tilda, who simply yelled "PLAN!" and ran). It got a standing ovation, partly for the fashion and partly because Finnegan hit the post twice.

The twins ran the **Code Word Contest**, in which grown-ups lined up and said "Your mom sent me" and the cubs said "WHAT'S THE CODE WORD" so loudly that Herschel had to move his cocoa vat back six feet.

Dàn ran a booth with a single enormous cardboard clock on it. The sign said A CLOCK ONLY TICKS. Every kid who walked up, he'd set it to ten minutes and say, "STEP RIGHT UP and watch absolutely nothing happen!" And they'd all stand there while it ticked to zero, and nothing happened, and he'd bow.

Bjorn Bigpaws demonstrated **Sit Down After**. He stood up, said "I did not fight. I stood between, with style," and then sat down on a bench with such dignity that three seals applauded.

Aurora Frostholm ran a relay down the sled hill — "Last one down buys cocoa!" — where every station was a safety line you had to shout before you could pass. *Head up, hands free, have a plan! Don't touch, leave, tell! No, I have to go!* Magnus Meltsworth lost on purpose and bought everyone cocoa, muttering that it was his one noble act for the week.

And at the far end of the row was a booth with a hand-painted sign that said **HOW TO SAY STOP**.

Bùdīng was running it. He was wobbling. He was fine. He was *definitely* wobbling.

And beside him — awkward, dripping only a little, arms held stiffly at his sides like he wasn't sure they were his — stood Sludge.

He'd refrozen. Mostly. He looked like a slush puppy who had been left in the cold long enough to remember he had edges.

"You say stop," Bùdīng was telling a small fox, "like this. Feet planted. 'Stop. I don't like that.' And then—" he gestured at Sludge.

Sludge cleared his throat. It sounded like a drain.

"You can say stop," he said to the fox, very stiffly, like he'd practiced it in a mirror. "I'll listen."

The fox nodded solemnly and went to try it on Finnegan.

"Was that okay?" Sludge muttered.

"That was great," said Bùdīng. "You're doing great. I'm fine. We're both fine."

"I'm not sure I'm—"

"We're *fine,*" said Bùdīng, and wobbled, and grinned.

---

When it was full dark, Auntie Hù climbed onto the rim of her tea-egg pot and did not say anything, because she never did, and the whole valley went quiet anyway.

She reached up and pulled a cord. Above the Boba on Ice stand, three words came on in warm yellow bulbs, one at a time.

**ATTITUDE.**

**SKILLS.**

**PLAN.**

"Mm," said Auntie Hù, into a silence so complete you could hear the snow.

And then she pointed at Willa.

Willa Wobble had not known she'd be pointed at. She stood up anyway. She was the smallest person on the whole hill, and every face was turned to her, and her voice, when it came, was not loud. It didn't have to be.

"I used to think the smallest step is still a step was something you said to yourself when you were scared," she said. "Now I think it's a *plan.* Mine was three words. 'No. I'm leaving.'" She looked at Aiyu, and Nora, and the twins, and Bjorn, and Boba. "You've all got one too now. You know what yours is. That's all. That's the whole speech."

It was the whole speech. It was enough. The valley cheered until the icicles rattled — safely, from the *other* roof.

---

They took the sign down at midnight. Boba on Ice was closed for the season.

"We'll be back," said Boba, hugging Piper so hard he left a sticky mark. "Next winter. Let's bounce!"

"Slide into it," said Piper, blinking fast.

"You could come to Taiwan," said Mochi, hugging everyone, all at once, with arms that reached across the whole hill. "You could *all* come. Stick together!"

"There's always enough fish for one more," said Barnaby, who was crying into a bag of fish.

"Say less," said Counselor Kumquat, who was also crying, and would deny it in court.

Boba was the last one awake, sitting on the rim of Auntie Hù's cold pot, watching the three yellow words glow over the empty stand. He thought about the icicle. About the kumquat. About *nine, maybe nine-fifteen.* About the whole long season and everything he knew now that he hadn't known when the banner went up.

"Auntie Hù?" he said. "Was it a good season?"

"Mm," said Auntie Hù, in the dark. "Nothing happened. That's how you know."

## Goodnight Blessing

Head up all day. Hands free. Have a plan.
Judge the ask, not the smile. Surprises end in telling.
You can't lose a fight you're not in. Enough — and then stop.
Slow hands. Honest words. Say less.
The smallest step is still a step, and you've taken every one.
Now put on the white coat, the good kind.
You are safe tonight, and you know what to do tomorrow.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **If police stop you:** don't run, don't lie, don't fight, be polite, keep your hands where they can see them, and give your name.
- **Then say:** "I want my parent." and "I'd like a lawyer." Those are complete sentences. Don't add *because* or *but I didn't.* Then stop talking.
- **Anything you say can be used** — all of it, not just the guilty parts. Nervous people tell true stories badly, and a guess ("around nine?") can become a "lie" on paper.
- **Innocent people ask for lawyers *because* they're innocent.** It doesn't make you look guilty; a good officer will tell you so herself.
- **A lawyer is a translator** between you and the system. What you tell your lawyer is private — always.
- **These are the rules here in the valley and in most of America.** Ask a grown-up how it works where *you* live.
- **Attitude. Skills. Plan.** You've got all three now.
