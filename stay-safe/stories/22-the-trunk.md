# Story 22: The Trunk

**Main Character:** Bǎi (Passion fruit)
**Guest Characters:** Coach Rosa the Flamingo, Tico the Toucan, Cajeta the Coconut Fudge, Herschel the Walrus, Teddy & Tilda Powderpuff, Auntie Hù
**Setting:** The Pink Belt Academy in the lodge's boot-drying room, on a grey afternoon — and the inside of Señor Baúl, Herschel's old cocoa chest, rigged up as a training trunk
**Theme:** The dark is only a room. Every room has a way out, if you breathe first and look second.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **出口** (chūkǒu) = exit / way out
**Safety Skill:** Trunk escape — breathe, find the glow handle, kick the taillight, wave, bang at stops, be ready to run

---

"Bet you won't lock me in that," said Bǎi.

She was pointing at a chest.

It was a big, battered wooden chest with brass corners and a hundred years of cocoa stains, sitting in the middle of the boot-drying room where Coach Rosa the Flamingo ran her Krav Maga school — the **Pink Belt Academy**, where everyone started pink and stayed pink. A name was painted on the lid in Rosa's loopy handwriting: **SEÑOR BAÚL.** *Mister Trunk.*

Bǎi was a passion fruit — round, wrinkly, purple-brown, and full to bursting with bright orange seeds and worse ideas. She had once said "Bet you won't" to a vending machine and won. She had also, a few weeks ago under a ski lift, learned that some things aren't dares. She'd told everyone. Twice.

But this was a *chest.* A chest was clearly a dare.

Coach Rosa — seven feet of pink flamingo, standing on one leg, headband on, whistle around her neck — looked at Bǎi for a long, considering moment.

"Okay," she said. "*¡Dale!*" — which is Spanish for *go for it,* and which Bǎi had never once expected a grown-up to say back to her.

"Wait, what?"

"You said bet I won't. I will. Get in."

---

Here is the thing about Señor Baúl that Bǎi didn't know yet.

Herschel the Walrus had donated it under protest ("I *liked* that chest") and Rosa had spent three nights rebuilding it: hidden air holes along the back; a real car taillight — red plastic, dead bulb — screwed inside, bought off Dìguā for two shrimp; and, dangling from the underside of the lid, a small T-shaped handle painted with the kind of green paint that glows in the dark.

It was, in other words, a car trunk. A pretend one. With a flamingo standing on it.

"Everybody gather," said Rosa, and the Academy gathered: Teddy and Tilda Powderpuff; Cajeta the Coconut Fudge, small and round and fuzzy with toasted coconut; and Tico the Toucan, self-appointed announcer.

"LADIES AND GENTLE-CREATURES," Tico boomed, beak flashing every color at once. "TONIGHT, in the WOODEN CORNER — a PASSION FRUIT. In the — well, the same corner, actually — a CHEST."

"Tico."

"Beak the news, Coach. It's my whole job."

Rosa turned to the class. "Today is a bad-day lesson — the kind you pack like an umbrella and hope stays dry." She tapped the lid with one pink toe. "Remember the van. Auntie Hù was right: the grab is your best moment, you fight *there*, you never get in the car. But we are Krav Maga. We plan for the *second-best* day too." She looked at Bǎi. "Suppose the worst thing happened. You're in the trunk. What now?"

Bǎi opened her mouth.

"Don't tell me," said Rosa. "Show me. Get in."

Bǎi got in.

The lid came down.

---

It was very dark.

Not lodge-at-night dark. Not under-the-blanket dark. *Dark* dark, where your eyes keep trying and get nothing back. The wood smelled like a hundred years of cocoa. She could hear her own breathing, and it sounded fast.

"Okay," she said out loud, to nobody. "Okay. This is fine. This is a dare. I'm *winning* this—"

Her breath got faster. Her seeds rattled. And a small cold thought arrived, the way small cold thoughts do in the dark: *what if there isn't enough air.*

*Tap tap.* Rosa's toe on the lid.

"Bǎi." Her voice came through the wood, muffled and calm. "What is your breathing doing?"

"…Going fast?"

"Mm-hm. First lesson. **Breathe.** There is air. There is always more air than you think. Panic is the thing that uses it up — panic breathes fast and shallow and takes twice as much. So you make it slow. In through the nose, four counts. Out through the mouth, four counts. *Tranquilo.* Easy. Do it."

Bǎi did it. In, four. Out, four. It was surprisingly hard while also being sure you were about to run out of air. She did it anyway. The dark didn't get any lighter. But it stopped pressing on her.

"Better," said Rosa's voice. "Now. You have been in there thirty seconds and you have done the most important thing already. Everything else is just *looking.* Second lesson. Reach up."

---

Bǎi reached up. Her stubby little arm bumped the underside of the lid, and then—

There it was. Right in front of her face. She'd been staring at it the whole time without seeing it, because she'd been too busy breathing fast: a small T-shaped handle, glowing the soft ghostly green of a nightlight.

"Oh," she said.

"You see it?"

"It *glows.*"

"Every car in America made since about the year 2002 has one of those inside the trunk," said Rosa. "By law. A release handle that glows in the dark, so anybody stuck in there can find it. Pull it, the trunk opens. That is the whole trick. The trunk is *built* to let you out."

"Then why doesn't everybody just—"

"Because in the dark, scared, breathing fast — you don't *look.* You panic and kick and you never see the glowing thing six inches from your nose." Rosa's voice went gentle. "That is why we breathe first. Breathing is how you get your eyes back."

"So I just pull it?"

"Not yet. *This* trunk, I've locked from outside, because I am mean and it is a drill. In a real one — yes. You pull it. But suppose the handle's broken. Suppose somebody cut it off. What now?"

Bǎi thought. In, four. Out, four.

"Now I want everybody *outside* to answer this one," said Rosa. "Cajeta. Where are you?"

"IN HERE," came a tiny muffled voice from somewhere else entirely.

---

This is when the Academy learned that Cajeta had climbed into Herschel's empty marshmallow tin and pulled the lid down "to practice."

"Cajeta," said Rosa. "Why."

"Solidarity," said the tin.

"WE HAVE A SECOND TRUNK, FOLKS," announced Tico.

"Fine. Fine. You're in a car too. Both of you. Somebody's driving. What do you do?"

"Count the turns," said Cajeta's tin, immediately. "Left. Left. A bump. We went over a bump. Now right. I hear — is that a duck? There's a duck. We're near the lake."

"There is no duck, we're in the boot room."

"I'm *committed,* Coach."

Rosa's whistle made a sound that might have been a laugh. "She's right, though. **Count the turns. Listen for sounds.** Trains. Water. Big road or little one. If you get out and somebody asks *where did they take you,* you'll have *something.* And it gives your brain a job. A brain with a job doesn't panic."

"Left, left, bump, right, duck," said Bǎi, in the dark. She actually felt better. It was ridiculous. It worked.

"Now the real one," said Rosa. "Third lesson. Feet."

---

"Where are the taillights on a car, Teddy?"

"In the back!"

"Tilda, more specific."

"The back *corners!* Low down!"

"Correct. Bǎi. You're lying in a trunk. Your feet go toward the back corners. What's there?"

Bǎi shuffled around in the dark. Her feet bumped something hard and plasticky, low, in the corner.

"Kick it," said Rosa. "Don't be polite about it. Real taillights are plastic. They break. Kick it *out.*"

Bǎi kicked. *Thunk.* Kicked again. *Thunk — crack.* Something gave way, and a thin grey slice of boot-room light came in through a hole the size of her fist.

She stared at it. It was the best thing she had ever seen.

"Now what's the hole for?"

"To… see out?"

"To be *seen.* Put your hand through it. Wave."

Bǎi stuck her stubby purple arm through the hole in Señor Baúl and waved.

"AND THERE IT IS, FOLKS," Tico screamed. "A HAND! A *WAVING* HAND! I HAVE NEVER BEEN SO MOVED IN MY LIFE!"

"Every car on the road behind you sees that hand," said Rosa. "Every one. Somebody calls the police. If you can't kick the light out — you *pull the wires.* Yank everything in there. The lights go dead. A police officer pulls that car over for having no taillights, and *that's* your moment."

"And if none of that works?"

"Then you *wait* for the car to stop. A red light. A gas station. You feel it stop — and you **bang and yell.** Both fists, both feet, as loud as anything. Not while it's moving, when nobody can hear you over the engine. *When it stops.* People are walking past. People hear a trunk yelling. People *look.*"

"HELP!" Bǎi tried, experimentally. "I'M IN THE TRUNK!"

"Good. Words. Say what it is. And Bǎi — last thing. The most important thing after breathing."

"What?"

"The lid *is* going to open. Sometime. And when it does — you are not lying there. You are *ready.* Feet under you, hands up, and the second there is light, you are *gone.* Out and running. Toward people. Toward lights. You don't stop to look at who opened it. **出口** (chūkǒu). *Exit.* The way out. Every room has one. That's yours."

Bǎi got her feet under her. She got her hands up, the way Rosa had taught them all on the very first day — *two feet down, hands up* — which is a strange thing to do in a cocoa chest, and she did it anyway.

"Ready?" said Rosa.

"Ready."

The lock clicked.

---

The lid came up and Bǎi came out of Señor Baúl like a cork out of a bottle — over the edge, onto the floor, three rolling steps toward the door, and *then* she turned around and looked, the way Rosa said, scanning left-right-left like a small purple periscope.

The whole Academy was staring at her.

Tico burst into tears. "SHE'S OUT, FOLKS. SHE'S OUT AND SHE'S *BEAUTIFUL.*"

"Two feet down," said Rosa, and put her second foot down for a second, which for Rosa was a standing ovation. "Hands up. Then—"

"*Shrimp,*" said the entire class.

"—shrimp." She unhooked a small bag from the wall and tossed one to Bǎi, who caught it, looked at it, and ate it, because that is what you do when a flamingo throws you a shrimp.

Herschel the Walrus, watching from the doorway with his whiskers doing something complicated, cleared his throat. "My chest has a *hole* in it."

"It's a *taillight,*" said Rosa. "I'll fix it. Tomorrow. After shrimp."

"CAN I COME OUT NOW," said the marshmallow tin.

---

That evening, Bǎi sat on the rim of Auntie Hù's tea-egg pot — the warm iron rim where the small ones were allowed to sit on cold nights — and turned a tea egg over and over in her hands without eating it.

"You're quiet," said Auntie Hù. "You're never quiet."

"I was scared," said Bǎi. "In the box. Really scared, for like a whole minute. And it was a *drill.* Rosa was standing *on* it."

"Mm."

"So what's a real one like?"

Auntie Hù never made a thing smaller than it was.

"Worse," she said. "Much. And that's why you did it today. If the worst day ever comes — and it almost certainly won't — a piece of you has already been in the dark, already breathed slow, already knows where to reach. You don't have to be brave *then.* You were brave *today.* Then just borrows it."

Bǎi thought about that. In, four. Out, four.

"The glowing handle," she said. "Does *every* car have one?"

"Nearly every one built in the last twenty-some years. Not the old ones, maybe. Which is why you check."

Bǎi's wrinkly purple face began, very slowly, to do the thing it did best.

"Auntie Hù," she said. "Bet *you* won't go find the handle in Dìguā's truck before bed."

"Mm," said Auntie Hù, and climbed down off the pot, and went. Bǎi watched her go with enormous satisfaction. It was, she decided, a *very* good dare. It was maybe the best one yet — the kind where everybody who takes it wins.

---

Late that night, when the lamps were low and the boot room was dark and Señor Baúl sat in the corner with his taillight-hole letting in a thin stripe of moonlight, Bǎi lay in her bunk and thought about tomorrow.

Tomorrow she was going to find every kid in Frostpeak Valley and say: *Bet you can't find the glowing handle in your mom's car by dinnertime.* And they'd all go look. And they'd all find it. And they'd all know.

She fell asleep breathing slow — in, four, out, four — with her hands up under the blanket like a very small, very round person who knew exactly where the exit was.

## Goodnight Blessing

Every dark room has a way out, little one, and you know how to find it: breathe first, then look.
Tonight the dark is soft and friendly and has a door on every side.
Nothing to kick, nothing to count, nothing to wait for.
Just in, four — and out, four — and sleep.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **Never get in the car** — that's always the first and best answer. This lesson is for the second-best day.
- **Breathe first.** There's more air than you think; panic is what uses it up. In for four, out for four. Breathing gives you your eyes back.
- **Find the glow handle.** Nearly every car made since 2002 has a glow-in-the-dark trunk-release handle inside. Practice finding it in your family's car — with a grown-up, trunk open, in the daylight.
- **Feet to the back corners — kick out a taillight.** Then stick your hand through and wave. Or pull the wires so the lights go dead and police stop the car.
- **Bang and yell when the car STOPS** — at lights and gas stations, when people can hear. Say what it is: "HELP! I'M IN THE TRUNK!"
- **Count turns, listen for sounds.** It gives your brain a job, and a brain with a job doesn't panic.
- **Be ready for the lid.** Feet under you, hands up. The second there's light — go. Toward people and lights. Then scan.
