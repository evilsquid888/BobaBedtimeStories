# Story 4: Never Get in the Van

**Main Character:** Boba (Tapioca pearl) and Piper Paddlefoot (Emperor Penguin)
**Guest Characters:** Auntie Hù, Cleo Coldwater, Bjorn Bigpaws, Herschel the Walrus, Sergeant Solveig Snowmane, the Smiling Stranger
**Setting:** The service road behind the Frostpeak lodge at dusk — a one-way plow lane between the gear sheds and the back of the Winter Night Market
**Theme:** The moment someone tries to take you is your best moment. Be loud, be rude, be a problem — and never, ever get in the car.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **救命** (jiùmìng) = HELP! (literally "save my life")
**Safety Skill:** Abduction resistance — fight at the grab, scream words, go heavy and loose, run against traffic toward people and lights

---

"I am," said Boba, "the fastest thing in this valley."

"You are a *marble,*" said Piper Paddlefoot.

"A fast marble."

"I have a *snowboard.*"

"I have *legs.* Tiny ones. Very efficient."

They were standing at the top of the service road behind the lodge, where the plows had scraped a long one-way lane between the gear sheds and the back of the night market. Boba was a round, glossy black-brown tapioca pearl with huge eyes and a faint sticky trail behind him whenever he got excited, which was always. Piper was an emperor penguin with a glittery blue snowboard and a planning problem, which is to say she launched first and planned somewhere around the second turn.

"The rules," said Piper, strapping in. "Top of the lane to the market lanterns. Loser refills the tapioca vat."

"The tapioca vat is the size of a *hot tub.*"

"Then don't lose. Slide into it!"

"Let's BOUNCE!"

They went. Piper carved. Boba *pinged* off a snowbank, off a shed, off a startled seal, and for one glorious second was actually in the lead — and then hit a patch of glare ice and became a very fast, very sticky, completely uncontrolled bouncing ball heading straight for a parked van.

---

The van was white. It had been idling there a while; there was a puddle of melt under the engine. Its back doors were open just a crack.

Boba stopped himself two inches from the bumper, arms out, spinning. "HA! *Stopped!* Did you see that? Piper, did you—"

"Hey," said a friendly voice. "You dropped this."

A man had stepped around the side of the van. He was tall. He wore a puffy grey parka, which was not the coat he'd worn on the sled hill last week, but the smile was the same, and the smile was the thing — warm, easy, familiar, a *dad* smile. If you try to picture his face you'll find you can't. He held out a glove. A child's glove. Purple.

"That's not mine," said Boba. "I don't have — I don't *wear* —"

He didn't finish. He was thinking, with a small cold part of his brain that Auntie Hù had been training all week, *I'm behind the lodge. I'm alone on a one-way lane. The nearest people are the market. That's orange.*

He never got to red.

The man's hand closed around him.

---

Now, here is the thing about a tapioca pearl: he is small. He is round. He fits, with terrible ease, inside a grown-up's fist. Boba felt the fingers close and felt himself lifted, and felt the whole world tilt toward the crack in those back doors, and everything in him — every polite, cheerful, *let's-be-friends* atom — said: *Don't make a fuss. It's a misunderstanding. Go quietly and it'll be fine.*

And then, very clearly, in the exact voice she used at the tea-egg pot, he heard Auntie Hù.

*Which moment is your best moment?*

They'd done this one on Wednesday. He'd gotten it wrong. He'd said *later, when they're not looking.* And Auntie Hù had said *Mm*, the disappointed Mm, and explained:

*Right now. The grab. You will never have better odds than right now — outside, near people, before the doors close. The car is the worst place in the world. Once the doors shut, everything gets harder. So you don't wait. You don't go along. You make yourself the biggest, loudest, heaviest, most impossible problem he has ever tried to pick up.*

*Be rude,* she'd said. *Be so rude.*

So Boba was.

---

The first thing he did was go *loose.*

Not stiff — stiff is easy to carry, stiff is a suitcase. He went soft and heavy and slippery, the way a tapioca pearl gets when it's been in the milk tea too long, and the fist that had been holding a marble was suddenly holding a fistful of warm sticky goo that *squirmed.*

The second thing he did was use his voice. Not a scream — screams sound like playing, screams get ignored. *Words.*

"**救命** (JIÙMÌNG)! **HELP!** THIS IS NOT MY DAD! I DON'T KNOW HIM! **CALL 911!**"

He'd practiced that too. *Say what's happening,* Auntie Hù had said. *People hear a shriek, they think 'kids.' People hear THIS IS NOT MY DAD, they turn around.*

The third thing that happened was not Boba's doing at all.

It was a glittery blue snowboard, traveling at a speed Piper would later describe as "not a *lot* of planning, honestly," hitting the man squarely in the side of the knee.

---

Piper had seen it from thirty feet back. She'd seen the fist close. She'd had, by her own account, no plan whatsoever, and she would later insist that this was the one time in her life a lack of planning had been *exactly* right.

"Slide into it!" she'd yelled — and slid into him.

The man's leg went out. His grip went slack. Boba popped free like a cork, hit the ice, and *bounced.*

"BOBA! Which way?!"

And Boba, upside down in midair, saw the lane.

It was a one-way lane. The van was pointed downhill. Auntie Hù's voice again: *You run AGAINST traffic. He can't follow you in a car if you're going the wrong way up a one-way street. You run toward people. You run toward lights.*

"UP!" Boba yelled. "UP THE LANE! TOWARD THE MARKET!"

"That's — Boba, that's the wrong way—"

"THAT'S THE POINT!"

So they ran. Uphill, against the plow lane's arrows, a tapioca pearl and an emperor penguin hauling a snowboard, and behind them a van door slammed and an engine roared and the van — could not follow. Not up. Not that way. It lurched, stalled, tried to reverse, and by then Boba and Piper were already screaming their way around the corner of the gear shed and into the back of the Winter Night Market, still yelling the same four words at the absolute top of their lungs:

"**HELP! THIS IS NOT MY DAD!**"

---

And the market turned.

That's the thing Boba remembered afterward, more than the fist, more than the van. He'd always thought a crowd was a wall, a thing that closed over you. But a hundred heads turned at once — penguins, bears, seals, a fox mid-bite — because a hundred people had heard *words*, and words mean something.

Bjorn Bigpaws was first. He simply rose, all seven feet of polar bear, and started walking toward the service road without appearing to hurry, and the crowd parted around him like snow off a plow.

Herschel the Walrus was second, with the lodge phone already at his whiskers.

And Cleo Coldwater — small, teal-boarded, artistic Cleo, who was never without her sketchbook — had been sitting on the gear-shed roof drawing the sunset. She had seen the whole thing from above. And instead of screaming, she had done the one thing she was best at in all the world.

She had *drawn* it.

The van. Its color. The dent in the rear door. And, in careful teal pencil across the bottom of the page: the license plate.

"Every slope tells a story," she said later, handing it to Sergeant Solveig, who looked at it for a long moment and then said, quietly, "This one's going to have an ending."

---

The van was gone by the time Bjorn reached the lane. That's all right. Solveig had the plate, and a plate is a name, and a name is an address, and by morning the grown-ups had taken it from there. The man in the grey parka did not come back to Frostpeak Valley. He isn't in the rest of this story, and he doesn't get to be.

It has more to say about two friends, shaking, on the steps of the Cocoa Hut.

Piper's flippers wouldn't stop trembling. She kept looking at her board like it belonged to someone else.

"I hit him," she said. "I actually — I didn't even *think*, I just—"

"You *slid into it,*" said Boba. He was still faintly gooey. He didn't care.

"I was so scared."

"Me too."

"You didn't *sound* scared. You sounded like a *fire alarm.*"

"Mm," said a voice by their feet. "Scared and loud. That's the correct combination."

Auntie Hù had climbed the steps with two tea eggs, still warm, and set them down one in front of each. She sat. For a while nobody said anything.

Then Piper asked the question.

"Auntie Hù. Why do they—" She stopped. Started again. "Why does someone *do* that? Take a kid? What do they *want?*"

---

Auntie Hù didn't look away. She didn't make it smaller than it was. That was the thing about her: she thought you could carry the truth if it was handed to you carefully.

"You're old enough to hear this plainly," she said, "so I'll say it plainly. A grown-up who tries to take a child away from where they're safe — that grown-up wants to *hurt* them. Sometimes to hurt their bodies, in the ways no grown-up is ever allowed to touch a kid. Sometimes worse." She let that sit. "That is why the answer is never *go quietly and it'll be okay.* It won't be okay. There is no version of that story where getting in the van makes it better. So you fight *there.* On the lane. In the open. Where people can hear you. You be the rudest, loudest, most impossible kid that man has ever met, and you never, ever get in the car."

Boba's huge eyes were huger. "Even if he says he'll hurt you if you don't?"

"*Especially* then. He's telling you what the car is for." She tapped the step. "Outside, you have a chance. Outside, you have Piper and a snowboard and a hundred people who turn around when you yell *this is not my dad.* Inside, you have nothing. So you spend everything you've got *outside.* Every single time."

Piper was quiet for a long moment.

"That's the scariest thing anyone's ever told me," she said.

"Mm. Now tell me the *other* thing I told you."

Piper thought. "…That it's rare?"

"It's *rare.* Most people you meet in your life will be kind. You'll walk ten thousand roads and nothing will happen on nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine of them. That's the truth too." Auntie Hù picked up her egg. "But you don't get to know in advance which road is which. So you live in yellow, you know your exits, you keep your NO loud — and then you go have a *wonderful* time on all the other roads. Being ready isn't the same as being afraid. Being ready is what *lets* you not be afraid."

---

They ate the eggs. Cleo came and sat with them, and Bjorn, who had walked the whole lane twice and found nothing but Piper's goggles, and Herschel, who brought cocoa in mugs so large Boba could have used one as a hot tub.

"For the record," said Boba eventually, "I was winning the race."

"You were a bouncing ball of *panic* heading for a *van.*"

"A *fast* bouncing ball of panic."

"You have to refill the tapioca vat."

"We never finished! It doesn't count!"

"Boba," said Piper, "you ran *up* a *one-way street.* You ran toward the *market.* You went the wrong way on purpose, screaming, and you were right, and I'm never going to say this again so listen: you're the fastest thing in this valley. I'll do the vat."

Boba's eyes filled up. He was, after all, a very sticky sort of person.

"We'll both do the vat," he said.

"Stick together," said Cleo. "That's what the mochi would say."

---

That night, Boba lay in his bunk behind Boba on Ice with his tiny arms wrapped around his own round self and listened to Piper breathing in the next bunk over — she'd asked to sleep at the stand tonight, and nobody had asked why.

He thought about the fist. He thought about how every soft cheerful piece of him had wanted to *go along*, and how one small hard trained piece of him had said *no* instead — and how that piece had been enough. It hadn't needed to be big. It had just needed to be *loud.*

"Piper?" he whispered.

"Mm-hm."

"You know how you launch before you plan?"

"Boba, I am *aware.*"

"Don't ever fix that."

A long pause. Then, very sleepily: "Slide into it."

"Let's bounce," Boba whispered back, and closed his enormous eyes, and slept the whole night through without bouncing even once.

## Goodnight Blessing

Your voice is big, little one — bigger than you. Bigger than any van.
You know which way to run now, and you know the whole valley turns around when you shout.
So tonight your legs can rest, and your voice can rest, and the doors are all locked, and they lock from the inside.
Let's bounce — softly — into sleep.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **Fight at the grab.** The moment someone tries to take you is your *best* moment — outside, near people, before any doors close. You will never have better odds. Don't wait for "later."
- **Never get in the car.** Not if he asks nicely, not if he threatens you. The car is the worst place there is. Spend everything you've got *outside.*
- **Scream WORDS, not screams.** "HELP! THIS IS NOT MY DAD! CALL 911!" tells people what's happening. A shriek sounds like playing; words turn heads.
- **Go heavy and loose.** Don't be a suitcase. Squirm, drop your weight, kick, grab a pole, bite if you have to. Be the most impossible thing he's ever tried to carry.
- **Run *against* traffic,** toward people and lights. A car can't follow you the wrong way up a one-way street.
- **Be rude, be loud, be bold.** In danger, polite is the enemy. Being rude costs nothing. Going quietly could cost everything.
- **It's rare — and you're ready.** Most roads are safe. Being ready is what lets you not be afraid on all of them.
