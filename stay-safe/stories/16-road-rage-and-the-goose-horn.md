# Story 16: Road Rage and the Goose Horn

**Main Character:** Dìguā (Sweet Potato)
**Guest Characters:** Don Café, Boba, Teddy & Tilda Powderpuff, Sergeant Solveig Snowmane, Coach Rosa the Flamingo
**Setting:** The Frostpeak Valley highway at dusk — a long snowy two-lane road from the warehouse toward town, and then the brightly lit front steps of the Frostpeak Valley police station
**Theme:** You can't control the other driver. You can control where you drive, what you lock, and who you call.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **警察局** (jǐngchájú) = police station
**Safety Skill:** Road rage — don't engage, lock up, don't go home, go to the police station, never get out; a truck is a thing

---

Don Café was sipping himself.

This is not as strange as it sounds. Don Café was a roasted coffee bean, his thermos was full of coffee, and when he raised the little cup to his lips in the passenger seat of the charcoal cart-truck he liked to say, "Ahh. Family." Everyone had stopped asking.

"Comfortable, Don Café?" said Dìguā, from behind the wheel.

"Slow roads and warm cups," said the coffee bean. "Good things brew slowly, mae."

"HONK THE GOOSE," said Teddy and Tilda from the back seat — *belted* this time, thank you very much, Tilda with both paws on her buckle like it was a trophy — and Dìguā, who was a soft touch for cubs, honked the goose.

*HWAAAANK.*

Boba, riding on the dashboard like a tiny black hood ornament, nearly fell off from laughing. "That horn is the best thing in this whole valley."

"That horn," said Dìguā, patting the dash, "has never once let me down."

He was going to be right about that in about four minutes.

---

The sun slid behind the ridge, the snow went from gold to blue, and Dìguā clicked on his one working headlight. Behind them, a pair of headlights appeared in the mirror. Then got bigger. Fast. Then filled the mirror entirely.

"Uh," said Boba. "There's a car very close to us."

There was. A sleek dark car had come up so tight that Dìguā could see only its windshield and the shape of a driver behind it. It flashed its lights. Then it honked — not a goose honk, a long, mean, *get-out-of-my-way* honk.

Teddy turned around to look.

"Eyes front, cub," said Dìguā, quietly. He didn't speed up. He didn't slow down. He didn't lift a single rough brown finger off the wheel. "Everybody look at the road ahead. Nobody look at him."

"But he's *right there* —"

"That's why we don't look."

The car swung out, roared past them in a spray of slush, cut back in front so close that Dìguā had to ease off the gas — and then, just to make sure everyone understood how angry he was, the driver stomped his brakes. Red lights flared. The truck dipped. The tapioca sacks in the back slid forward with a *whumpf.*

"He's *crazy,*" said Tilda.

"Maybe," said Don Café mildly, and took a sip of himself. "Or he is late. Or he is sad. Or somebody was rude to *him* an hour ago and he has been carrying it around like a hot coal, looking for somewhere to put it down." He capped his thermos. "It does not matter which. We are not going to be the place he puts it down."

---

Dìguā had grown up beside a street cart in Taipei, and a street cart sees people at their worst — honking at scooters, red in the face over a parking spot. He had learned one thing from them. You could not fix them. You could only decide not to be part of their evening.

"Okay," he said, calm as a sweet potato in warm charcoal. "We're going to do this exactly right, and then Rosa's going to say it was Krav Maga even though nobody moved."

"Rosa says *everything* is Krav Maga," said Boba.

"Rosa's usually right. Rule one." Dìguā tapped his own temple. "*Don't engage.* No eye contact. No hand signals. No honking back — Teddy, I see that paw, put it down. We don't race him. We don't brake-check him back. We don't *teach him a lesson.* A man who's this angry is looking for a partner to dance with, and we are politely, boringly, *not dancing.*"

"That's so *unfair,*" said Tilda. "He started it!"

"He did. And if we finish it, we both lose." The dark car had dropped back behind them again. It was staying there. "Rule two. Don Café, would you?"

Don Café reached across, very slowly, and pressed the lock button on the door. *Click.* All four doors. Then he rolled his window up the last inch it had been down.

"Doors locked. Windows up," said Dìguā. "Always. The truck is a shield. It's three thousand pounds of metal with us inside it. Nobody gets to open that shield but us."

"Rule three?" said Boba, who had started counting on his tiny fingers.

Dìguā's face did something complicated. "Rule three is the one everybody gets wrong."

---

"Rule three," he said, "is we don't go home."

Silence in the cab.

"But home's *right there,*" said Teddy. "The market's ten minutes. We could just—"

"And he'd follow us right to it." Dìguā shook his head. "If we park at Boba on Ice, this fellow parks right behind us. Now he knows where we sleep. Now the angry stranger has our *address.*" He let that sit. "No. When somebody's following you angry, you go somewhere with *lots of people and lots of lights.* A busy gas station. A fire station. Or the very best place in the whole valley — **警察局** (jǐngchájú). The police station. You drive straight up to the front door of the police station, and you'd be *amazed* how fast an angry man remembers he has somewhere else to be."

"That's *sneaky,*" said Tilda admiringly.

"That's *smart,*" said Don Café. "Slow, and smart."

"Rule four. Boba, you're the passenger with the best eyes." Dìguā jutted his chin at the mirror. "Read me his plate."

Boba stood up on the dash, squinted back at the dark car, and read the plate out loud, letter by letter, in his punchiest voice.

"Tilda. You're the passenger with the best memory. Say it back."

Tilda said it back perfectly. Twice.

"Don Café — the phone's in the cup holder. You call 911. Tell them where we are, tell them a car's been tailgating and brake-checking us and it's following us, give them the plate, and tell them we're heading to the station." He kept his hands exactly where they were. "The driver drives. The passengers call. Never the other way around."

Don Café dialed, gave the plate in his slow warm voice, and said "we are going to the police station" as calmly as if ordering breakfast.

---

The traffic light at Ridge Road went red.

Dìguā rolled up behind a minivan and stopped — but not close. Far enough back that Boba, looking over the hood, could see the minivan's back tires, with a truck-length of empty snow between.

"Why'd you stop so far away?" Teddy asked.

"Because if I can see his tires," said Dìguā, "I can get out. If I'm right up on his bumper, I'm boxed in. You always leave yourself a way out at a light. Always."

Behind them the dark car had pulled up close again. Very close.

And then its door opened.

The driver got out. In the mirror he was just a shape — big, coat-wrapped, walking toward the truck through the slush, one arm already coming up to point.

"Dìguā—" Boba's voice went very small.

"Nobody opens anything," said Dìguā. He said it the way you'd say *the sky is blue.* "Nobody rolls down a window to explain. Nobody gets out to talk. There is no talking. Tilda, what do we do when a stranger says *come here*?"

"We DON'T," said Tilda.

"Same thing. He's a stranger who wants us to come here. We don't." He reached one hand toward the center of the wheel. "And if a stranger walks up to your locked truck at a red light in the dark, you do the loudest, rudest, most boring thing there is."

He leaned on the goose.

*HWAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAANK.*

It did not stop. It went on and on, like an entire flock of outraged geese being surprised at once, echoing off every house on Ridge Road. Porch lights came on. A dog barked. Somebody opened a door to see what on earth.

The man in the mirror stopped walking.

He stood there in the slush, in the light of six porches, being looked at.

The light turned green. The minivan pulled away. And Dìguā, who had left himself exactly one truck-length of room, turned the wheel, eased into the gap, and drove calmly around the corner onto the road that led straight to the station — still honking, all the way, like a goose that had been badly wronged and wanted the whole valley to know it.

---

The police station was a squat stone building with every window lit, and Sergeant Solveig Snowmane was already on the front steps with her arms folded when the truck pulled up and, at last, stopped honking. The silence was enormous.

"I heard the goose," said Solveig, "from two miles out."

"Good," said Dìguā. "That was the idea."

"We've got the plate. A unit picked him up at the Ridge Road light — he was still standing there arguing with a porch." She looked into the cab. Her eyebrow — the one with the scar — went up a notch. "Everybody belted."

"EVERYBODY," said Tilda, and raised her buckle like a medal.

"You did that right," Solveig said. "Every piece. Didn't engage, locked up, didn't go home, called it in, left yourself room, stayed in the truck. A lot of grown-ups would have gotten out to have a *word.*" She shook her big white head. "The word is never worth it."

"What would've happened," Boba asked, "if he'd tried to get *in?* Like — if he'd wanted the *truck?*"

Dìguā opened his mouth, but it was Don Café who answered, slow and sure.

"Then we give him the truck."

Everyone turned to look at the coffee bean.

"Keys out the window. Everybody out the *other* side. Walk away and let him have it." Don Café patted the dashboard, kindly, the way you'd pat a good horse. "She is a fine truck, mae. She is a *thing.* A truck can be found, or fixed, or replaced. You cannot brew a fresh pot of *Tilda.*" He tipped his little straw hat. "Good things brew slowly. Nothing brews at all if you are hurt over a thing."

"Did you just call my truck a *thing,*" said Dìguā.

"I called her a *fine* thing."

"...I'll allow it."

---

They drank cocoa on the station steps. The angry man, they learned, had had a very bad day, and was going to have a slightly longer one now.

Coach Rosa the Flamingo arrived, having heard the goose from the lodge, and listened to the whole story standing on one leg with her eyes narrowed.

"So," she said, when it was over. "You did not look at him."

"Nope."

"You locked the doors."

"Yep."

"You did not go home. You went to the *policía.* You left room at the light. And when he came to the window, you made a noise so loud and so rude that six houses woke up, and then you left."

"That's about it."

Rosa nodded slowly. "That," she announced, "is the finest Krav Maga I have seen all winter. Zero punches. Zero kicks. *Perfect* Krav Maga." She raised one wing. "And then, shrimp."

"Told you," said Dìguā to Boba.

"I never doubted," said Boba, who had.

---

They drove home the slow way, all belted, no one behind them. Teddy fell asleep first. Tilda held out longest, guarding her buckle, and then she was gone too, and the cab was quiet except for the engine and Don Café's thermos, softly clinking.

"Dìguā?" Boba said, half asleep on the dash.

"Kid."

"Were you scared? When he got out?"

Dìguā drove a while before he answered. The market lanterns came into view, gold in the blue dark, nobody behind them.

"Sure," he said. "That's why I honked. Scared and *loud*, that's the right way round. Scared and *out of the truck* — that's the wrong one." He pulled into the lot and turned off the campfire engine. "Anybody can lose their temper, kid. Being the one who *doesn't* — who locks the door and drives to the lights — that's not weak. That's the whole game."

He carried the cubs in one at a time, and Boba last, sticky and snoring. Then he went back out into the cold and patted the dash where the goose lived.

"Fine thing," he told her. "You're a fine thing."

*Hwaank,* said the goose, very softly, or maybe he imagined it.

## Goodnight Blessing

Lock the doors of the day, little one. Roll up the windows. Let the angry cars go by.
You don't have to answer every honk. You don't have to win every road.
Drive slow toward the lights — and the warmest light of all is right here, on your pillow.
Buckle in. Sleep.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **Don't engage.** No eye contact, no gestures, no honking back, no racing, no brake-checking. An angry driver wants a partner — don't be one.
- **Lock the doors, windows up.** The car is a shield. Nobody opens it but you.
- **Don't go home.** An angry driver who follows you home learns where you live. Drive to a **police station**, a fire station, or a busy, brightly lit place.
- **The driver drives; the passengers call 911.** Give your location, what's happening, and the plate.
- **At a stop, leave room.** If you can see the tires of the car ahead, you can pull away.
- **If they get out and walk up: stay in, open nothing, lean on the horn, drive away when it's clear.** Never get out to "have a word." The word is never worth it.
- **A truck is a thing.** If someone wants the car — keys out the window, everyone out the other side, walk away. You are not a thing.
