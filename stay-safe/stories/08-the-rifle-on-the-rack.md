# Story 8: The Rifle on the Rack

**Main Character:** Teddy & Tilda Powderpuff (Polar Bear cubs)
**Guest Characters:** Gallo & Pinto, Magnus Meltsworth, Coach Rosa the Flamingo, Auntie Hù
**Setting:** Magnus Meltsworth's ancient truck on the back-ridge road, and his hunting cabin high above Frostpeak Valley — one wood stove, one bear-sized hammock, one very small teacup
**Theme:** A gun at a friend's house is still a gun. Don't touch it, leave the room, and tell — even when the one reaching for it is your friend.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **鎖起來** (suǒ qǐlái) = lock it up
**Safety Skill:** Guns in a home or vehicle — don't touch, leave, tell; "unloaded" isn't a real word; if your friend picks it up, leave fast and get the adult

---

Magnus Meltsworth's truck had been built before the invention of comfort, and it wanted you to know that.

"Seatbelts," rumbled Magnus.

"There's only one seatbelt," said Teddy.

"Share it."

Teddy and Tilda Powderpuff, the polar bear cubs, shared it. Gallo and Pinto — a grain of fried rice in a tiny palm hat and a glossy black bean with a tiny wooden spoon — sat on the dashboard, because they were roughly the size of the dashboard's dust. The truck coughed, groaned, considered retirement, and lurched up the back-ridge road toward Magnus's hunting cabin, where he had agreed — *grudgingly*, at great length, with a speech about how this was his one noble act for the week — to take the four of them for a night of cocoa and stars.

"Rise and pinto!" sang Gallo, bouncing. "A road trip, *hum!* I've never been in a truck! I've never been in a *mountain!* I've never—"

"Gallo," said Pinto gently. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing *enthusiastically.*"

Teddy twisted around in the shared seatbelt to look at the back of the cab. And that is when he saw it, resting on a rack behind the seat, long and dark and very still.

"Is that a—"

"Yes," said Magnus, eyes on the road. "Don't."

That was the whole conversation. It was, as it turned out, exactly the right one.

---

Here is a thing about polar bear cubs: their paws are curious. Their paws are curious *before* their brains are. Teddy's paw was already drifting back toward the rack, the way a paw drifts toward a cookie jar, and he did not entirely notice it drifting until a very small wooden spoon tapped it — *tink* — from the dashboard.

"Better together, mae," said Pinto quietly, which was his catchphrase and also, right now, a way of saying *hey.*

Teddy looked at his paw. He put it back in his lap.

"I was just gonna *look,*" he said.

"You can look with your eyes," said Pinto. "Eyes don't have fingers."

"Mm," said Magnus, in a tone that suggested he'd been holding his breath for the last quarter mile. "The bean is correct. That rifle stays on the rack. The rack is a *don't.* You don't touch the rack, you don't touch what's on the rack, you don't lean on the rack, you don't *breathe* at the rack. Am I clear?"

"You're clear," said Tilda.

"You're *loud,*" said Teddy.

"I am a large bear in a small truck," said Magnus. "Everything I do is loud." The truck hit a rut and something in the engine went *bwong.* "That was not me."

Gallo, who had gone quiet — which for Gallo was a medical event — started to hum. A small, nervous, tuneless hum, the kind you hum when you've just realized the world has things in it that you didn't know were there.

"You okay, Gallo?" said Tilda.

"I'm *great,*" said Gallo, humming. "I'm *hum*—wonderful. I just — I didn't know trucks had those."

"Some do," said Magnus. "Mine does. Hunters hunt. It's for elk. It's never once been pointed at anything that could talk, and it never will be." He glanced in the mirror. "And it's *still* a don't. Those two things are both true."

Gallo thought about that for the rest of the drive, and hummed.

---

The cabin was exactly what you'd expect from Magnus: one room, one wood stove, one enormous hammock strung wall to wall that was clearly the bed, one table, one chair, and — inexplicably — one very small, very delicate china teacup with roses painted on it, sitting alone on the windowsill.

"Don't ask about the teacup," said Magnus.

Nobody asked about the teacup. Everybody looked at the teacup.

Magnus lit the stove with a grunt, hung his coat on a nail, and went out back to fetch cocoa water from the pump. The cabin filled with the smell of pine smoke and the sound of Gallo, recovered now, narrating the hammock. "It's a *bed* but it's a *swing!* Pinto! It's a bed-swing!"

"I see it, mae."

"Can we get in it?"

"Ask Magnus."

"Magnus is outside!"

"Then we wait," said Pinto, in the tone of a bean who has waited for many things.

Tilda, meanwhile, was hungry. Tilda was frequently hungry. And there was a chest of drawers by the stove, and chests of drawers are where grown-ups keep marshmallows — everyone knows that — so she padded over and pulled open the top one.

Socks.

Second drawer. Matches, a compass, a very old sandwich.

Third drawer.

Tilda stopped.

It was a pistol. Just lying there, in the drawer, on a folded rag, the way you'd leave a wrench. Not shiny like on TV. Dull. Heavy-looking. Ordinary. And that was the part that made her stomach go strange — how *ordinary* it looked, next to the matches and the sandwich, like it belonged there.

"Um," said Tilda. "Guys."

---

Everybody came to look. That's the thing about a drawer — a drawer is an invitation.

"Whoa," said Teddy.

"Oh," said Gallo, and stopped humming.

Pinto said nothing at all. He just went very still, the way a bean does.

And here is where it got hard — harder than the truck. Because in the truck, Magnus had been *right there*, saying *don't*. But now Magnus was outside, and the drawer was open, and there were only two cubs and a grain of rice and a bean, and nobody had said *don't* yet, and the pistol just sat there being ordinary.

"It's probably not even loaded," said Gallo. He said it kindly, the way Gallo said everything — trying to make it smaller, trying to make everyone feel better. "Right? He wouldn't just leave a loaded—"

Pinto's tiny wooden spoon came up.

"We don't know that," he said. "Nobody ever knows that."

"But—"

"Gallo. *Nobody* ever knows that. Not us, not Magnus, not a hunter, not a police bear. 'Unloaded' isn't a real word. It's a *guess* with a nice hat on." Pinto's voice was soft and completely certain. "Every gun is loaded. That's the rule. Always. Because the one time it isn't the rule is the one time that matters."

Gallo looked at the drawer. His freckle drooped.

"I didn't know that," he said. "I'm sorry. I really didn't know that."

"Now you do." Pinto touched his arm. "Better together, mae. That's what together is *for.*"

And Teddy — Teddy, whose paw had drifted toward the rack an hour ago — heard his own voice say, from somewhere behind his ears: "Okay. So we close the drawer and—"

"*No,*" said Tilda.

Everyone looked at her.

"We don't *close* it. We don't *touch* it. Closing it is touching it. Auntie Hù said — she said moving it is touching it, remember? The cold thing in the snow?" Tilda backed away from the chest of drawers, one step, two. "We leave the room. We get the grown-up. That's the whole thing. We don't fix it. We don't tidy it. We *go.*"

"But it's *Magnus's house,*" said Teddy, miserable. "It's *rude.* He'll think we were *snooping*—"

"We *were* snooping."

"—and I don't want him to be mad at us, he's finally being *nice*—"

"Teddy." Tilda was already at the door. "Which is worse? Magnus a little mad, or *that* drawer with nobody watching it?"

Teddy did not have an answer to that. Nobody did.

"We didn't mean to open the drawer—" said Tilda.

"—but we're telling," said Teddy, and they went.

---

They found Magnus at the pump, filling a kettle, humming his own tune, which was much lower than Gallo's and sounded like a truck going uphill.

"Magnus," said Tilda.

"Cocoa's coming. Patience is a—"

"Magnus, there's a gun in your drawer and we opened it and we didn't touch it and we need you to come inside right now."

The kettle stopped.

Magnus Meltsworth turned around very slowly. Teddy braced for the bellow — the *what were you doing in my drawers*, the *how dare you*, the grumpy-bear roar that everyone in Frostpeak Valley had heard at least once. He braced so hard he shut his eyes.

It didn't come.

When he opened his eyes, Magnus had gone a very strange color for a polar bear. Grey, almost. His enormous shoulders had dropped. He set the kettle down on the snow with great care, as if it were the teacup.

"Which drawer," he said. His voice was quiet.

"The third one."

"Is it still open?"

"Yes. We didn't close it. Tilda said closing is touching."

"Tilda is right." Magnus put one huge paw on each cub's head, the way Herschel did, and looked at Gallo and Pinto on Teddy's shoulder, and said the four words that Tilda would remember longer than anything else about that night:

"You did that right."

Then he walked inside, and shut the drawer himself, and stood there for a while with his back to them, not saying anything.

---

That night, after the cocoa — which Magnus made with great seriousness and served, for reasons that were never explained, in the tiny rose teacup, passed around, one sip each — he did something none of them expected.

He got a big iron box out from under the hammock. It had a lock on it, and dust on it, and it had clearly not been opened in a long time. He put the pistol in it. He went out to the truck in the dark and came back with the rifle from the rack and put that in too, and a second box of small heavy things went in a different drawer, with its own lock.

"**鎖起來** (suǒ qǐlái)," said Pinto softly, watching. "Lock it up."

"That's what the box is for," said Magnus, not looking up. "It's always been what the box is for. I got—" He stopped. He turned the key. "I got lazy. I live alone. I told myself *nobody comes up here.*" He looked at the four of them sitting in a row in his hammock like birds on a wire. "And then somebody came up here."

"Are you mad?" asked Teddy, very small.

"At *you?*" Magnus made a sound that was almost a laugh. "Cub, you're the only ones in this cabin who did everything right tonight. I'm the one who left a *don't* in a drawer next to a sandwich." He shoved the iron box back under the hammock with his foot. "This is my *second* noble act for the week. Don't tell anyone. It'll ruin my reputation."

"We won't," said Tilda.

"We *might,*" said Teddy.

"—but it'll be worth it," they said together, and Magnus's grump cracked right down the middle, and he laughed for real, a sound like an avalanche deciding to be friendly.

---

Now, a word to whoever is reading this out loud, because Magnus would want it said and would never say it himself.

If your kid is going to a friend's house — a cabin, an apartment, a grandparent's, anywhere — it is a perfectly ordinary, perfectly polite question to ask the grown-up there: *Do you have guns in the house, and are they locked up?* You'd ask about a pool. You'd ask about a dog. This is the same question. Nobody good will mind. And guns in a home belong the way Magnus's ended up that night: locked, unloaded, with the ammunition somewhere else and its own lock. That's the grown-up's job. The kid's job is the four things Tilda knew: **don't touch, leave the room, tell, don't try to fix it.**

Okay. Back to the cabin.

---

The stars came out over the back ridge, more stars than the cubs had ever seen, because Magnus's cabin was above the lodge lights and the sky up there had nothing in the way. They lay in the hammock, all four of them and eventually Magnus too — which made the hammock touch the floor and creak like a ship — and looked up.

"Pinto?" said Gallo, very sleepy. "I said a wrong thing today. About the — about *not loaded.*"

"You said it and then you learned it. That's the right order."

"I don't like knowing about it."

"Nobody does, mae." Pinto's tiny spoon rested on Gallo's shoulder. "That's why we know it *together.* So you don't have to carry it by yourself."

Gallo hummed — a real hum this time, a happy one — and was asleep before the end of it.

---

Two days later, back at the Pink Belt Academy, Coach Rosa the Flamingo heard the whole story from the twins, standing on one leg with her eyes half closed, which meant she was either listening very hard or asleep. When they finished she opened both eyes.

"So," she said. "The gun was in the drawer. You did not touch it. You left the room. You got the bear. The gun went in the box." She tilted her long pink head. "How many Krav Maga moves was that?"

The twins looked at each other. "…Zero?"

"*Zero moves.*" Rosa spread her wings. "Do you know how rare that is? A perfect technique. Not one hand raised. Nobody hurt. The most dangerous thing in the room stayed exactly where it was and then went somewhere safer. I could not do better with a *thousand* moves." She dropped her voice to a whisper. "Don't tell the others. They'll want to skip class."

"Mm," said Auntie Hù, from the tea-egg pot, which meant she'd been listening the whole time and agreed with every word.

"Then shrimp," said Rosa, and produced two.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **A gun at a friend's house is still a gun.** Same four rules as anywhere: **don't touch, leave the room, tell a grown-up, don't try to fix it.**
- **"Unloaded" isn't a real word.** Nobody ever knows that — not you, not your friend, not the grown-up. Treat every gun as loaded, always.
- **"Just looking" with hands is touching.** Closing the drawer is touching. Moving it "somewhere safe" is touching. Look with your eyes and leave.
- **A rack is a "don't."** In a truck, on a wall, in a case — you don't touch the rack or anything on it.
- **If your friend picks it up: don't grab for it, don't argue over it.** Leave the room fast and get the adult. A tug-of-war over a gun is the worst thing that can happen.
- **Telling isn't snooping, and it isn't rude.** A little embarrassment beats a big accident every time.
- **For grown-ups:** ask about guns before a playdate the way you'd ask about a pool. Guns in a home belong locked, unloaded, ammunition stored separately.
