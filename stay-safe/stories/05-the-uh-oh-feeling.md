# Story 5: The Uh-Oh Feeling

**Main Character:** Yángmáo (Starfruit)
**Guest Characters:** Cleo Coldwater, Auntie Hù, Herschel the Walrus, Nibbles & Splash
**Setting:** The frozen river path from the Frostpeak lodge to the fish market at dusk, the lamps just coming on and the sky going purple
**Theme:** Your gut doesn't need a reason. You don't need proof to leave.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **走吧** (zǒu ba) = let's go / let's get out of here
**Safety Skill:** Trust your gut — leave without a reason; walk with purpose; look at the person so he knows he's been seen

---

Yángmáo made a wish on her first point and walked into a lamppost.

"Ow," she said, dreamily.

She made a wish on her second point — for the lamppost to be somewhere else — and walked into it again.

"*Ow,*" she said, slightly less dreamily.

Yángmáo was a starfruit. Sliced, she was a perfect five-pointed star, pale green-gold and see-through at the edges like stained glass, with tiny star-shaped freckles that glowed a little in the dark. Every point was a wish. That was her catchphrase and also, unfortunately, her entire method of getting through the day. She wished for good weather. She wished for extra mango on her shaved ice. She wished, quite often, that she would stop walking into things, and then she walked into things while wishing it.

"Third time's the charm," said Cleo Coldwater, who was sitting on a snowbank with her sketchbook open, drawing the lamppost. Cleo was a macaroni penguin with a magnificent spray of yellow feathers on her head and a teal snowboard with gold racing lines, and she had never in her life *not* been drawing something. "Or you could go *around* it. Every slope tells a story, Yángmáo. This one's called *Starfruit Meets Pole, Chapter Three.*"

"I'm wishing for a happy ending," said Yángmáo.

"You're *walking* into a lamppost."

"Both things can be true."

---

They were on their way to the fish market. Cleo needed a new pencil and Yángmáo needed, in her own words, "to see what the sky does over the river at dusk," which was the kind of errand only a starfruit would think of as an errand. The river path was the shortcut: a long, curving trail along the frozen river, with lamps every fifty steps that were just flickering awake, one by one, like a row of sleepy eyes.

It was beautiful. The sky was going purple. The snow on the river had a crust of ice on top that caught the last light and threw it back pink. Yángmáo made four wishes in a row and, by some miracle, didn't hit anything.

And then Cleo, who noticed things because drawing *is* noticing, said quietly: "Yángmáo. Don't turn around. There's someone behind us."

"There's *lots* of someones behind us," said Yángmáo. "It's a path."

"There was. Now there's one." Cleo's pencil hadn't stopped moving. She was drawing the river. She was also drawing, in the corner of the page, without looking, a figure. "He's been behind us since the lodge. When we stopped at the lamppost — all three times — he stopped too. Tied his boot. Looked at his phone. Tied the *other* boot."

Yángmáo felt it then. Right in the middle of her star, in the soft place where all five points met. A small, cold *uh-oh.* Like a bell that somebody had flicked with a fingernail.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, I don't like that."

"Me neither."

"Maybe he's just—"

"Maybe," Cleo agreed. "Doesn't matter."

---

Now, here is where Yángmáo did the thing that starfruits do. She tried to wish it away.

*I wish he's just going to the fish market too. I wish he's a nice man with slow boots. I wish I'm being silly. I wish—*

"Yángmáo," said Cleo, still walking, still drawing. "What are you doing?"

"Wishing."

"For what?"

"For it to be fine."

"And if it's not?"

Yángmáo didn't have a wish for that. She had five points, and none of them covered *and if it's not.*

"We could turn around," said Cleo.

"That's *rude.* He'd know we—" Yángmáo stopped. "He'd know we noticed."

"Yes," said Cleo. "Good."

And Yángmáo, who was dreamy and hopeful and wished on everything, heard something in Cleo's voice she'd never heard before. Cleo wasn't scared. Cleo was *deciding.* She had noticed, and she was making a plan, and she wasn't waiting for permission or proof or a good enough reason. Cleo had put on, Yángmáo realized, the orange coat — the one Aiyu had told them all about from the night market. *Something's off. You don't know what yet. Make a plan.*

"The fish market's around the next bend," said Cleo. "Lights. People. Herschel's cousin runs the herring stall. That's the plan. We walk there, we walk *in*, and we do it like we mean it."

"And him?"

"He can do whatever he wants. We're not in charge of him." Cleo tucked her pencil behind her ear. "**走吧** (zǒu ba). Let's go."

---

Something you should know about Auntie Hù, the tea egg who minded the crowd at the night market: she had a whole speech about *how to walk*, and she'd given it to the boba crew on their second night in the valley, and Yángmáo had been wishing on a lantern at the time and missed most of it.

But she'd caught the shape. And the shape came back to her now, on the river path, in the purple dusk.

*Head up.* Not down at your feet, not down at your phone. Up. So you can see, and so you look like someone who sees.

*Shoulders back.* Take up your space. Small and hunched says *I hope nobody notices me.* Tall says *I already noticed you.*

*Hands free.* Nothing to fumble. Nothing to drop. Cleo, without a word, closed her sketchbook and slid it into her pack.

*Walk like you have somewhere to be.* Because you do. Not running — running is for red. This is orange. Orange walks fast and steady and doesn't stop at lampposts.

And the last one, the one Auntie Hù had said was the hardest and the most important: *Look at him.*

"I can't," Yángmáo whispered. "If I look, he'll know I—"

"Know what?"

"That I'm scared."

"You're not scared," said Cleo. "You're *paying attention.* Those look the same from the outside and they feel completely different on the inside. He doesn't get to know which one you are." She glanced sideways at Yángmáo. "People who want an easy target want someone who *hasn't seen them.* The second you look, you're not easy anymore. You're a witness. Witnesses have faces."

Yángmáo took a breath that went all the way down to the bottom point of her star.

She turned her head. She looked.

He was ordinary. That was the first thing. A man in a dark coat, forty steps back, hands in his pockets. He looked up as she looked back, and their eyes met across the snow, and for one long second neither of them did anything.

"Good evening," said Yángmáo.

She said it *loud.* She said it clear and steady and friendly-not-friendly, the voice of somebody saying *I see you, and now you know I see you, and we both know I'll remember your coat.* It was the least dreamy sound she had ever made in her life. It surprised her so much she almost walked into a lamppost.

The man's step hitched. Just slightly. Then he nodded — a small, ordinary nod — and kept walking.

Cleo and Yángmáo kept walking too. Faster. Around the bend, into the light.

---

The fish market was a wall of noise and lanterns and glorious, appalling smell. Herschel's cousin, a walrus even larger than Herschel, was shouting the price of herring to nobody in particular. Nibbles and Splash, the seal siblings, were sitting on a crate arguing about whether a fish could be *too* fresh.

"It can't," said Nibbles.

"It can if it's still swimming," said Splash.

"That's just a fish."

"That's my *point.*"

Cleo walked straight to the herring stall and stood in the middle of the light, in the middle of the crowd, in the middle of the largest walrus in the northern hemisphere, and only then did she let out the breath she'd been holding.

Yángmáo turned around.

The river path was empty. The man in the dark coat wasn't there. He'd peeled away somewhere between the bend and the market — turned back, or taken the upper trail, or vanished into one of the side streets. Gone.

"Was he—" Yángmáo started, and then couldn't figure out how to finish.

"I don't know," said Cleo.

"Was he really *following* us, or was he just—"

"I don't know."

"But then how do we know if we—"

"Yángmáo." Cleo put a flipper on her arm. "We don't. We're never going to. That's the *deal.* You don't get to find out. You don't get a receipt that says *yes, you were right to leave.* Maybe he was the nicest man in the valley with the slowest boots in the world. Maybe he wasn't." She shrugged, and it was the calmest shrug Yángmáo had ever seen. "We left. We're here. We're fine. That's the whole story. **You don't need proof to leave.**"

"But if he *was* nice, I was rude to him."

"You said *good evening.*"

"…Loudly."

"You were loudly polite," said Cleo. "He'll survive. If he was nice, he's forgotten already. If he wasn't — then *good evening* was the best thing you ever said." She grinned. "Auntie Hù would say *rude is cheap.* You didn't even buy rude. You bought *good evening* at full volume. That's practically free."

---

They bought Cleo's pencil. They bought, because Yángmáo wished for it and Nibbles was selling it, one small fried fish on a stick, which Yángmáo ate while looking at what the sky did over the river at dusk, which was: purple, then deep blue, then stars.

"Cleo," she said, after a while. "The wishing didn't work."

"No."

"I wished so hard for it to be fine."

"I know."

"And the thing that worked was… walking. Just walking. Into the light." Yángmáo turned the fish stick over in her hand. "A wish isn't a plan, is it."

Cleo had her sketchbook out again. She was drawing the market — the lanterns, the crates, Splash falling off one. In the corner of the page, small, she'd left the figure from the river path, just a few lines, unfinished. She looked at it for a moment. Then she wrote a title under the whole drawing, in her neat, slanted hand.

*We Went Inside.*

"A wish is a *hope,*" she said. "Hopes are good. I hope things all the time. But a plan is *where's my light, where's my crowd, who's my adult.* You can wish *and* plan. You just have to do them in the right order."

Yángmáo looked at the title. Then she took Cleo's pencil — the new one — and, very carefully, drew a small five-pointed star next to it.

"What's that for?"

"That's my wish," said Yángmáo. "For next time. That we do it exactly the same."

---

They walked home the long way, along the lit street, with the lamps all awake now and the market noise fading behind them. Yángmáo walked with her head up. She walked with her hands free. She walked like somebody who had somewhere to be, which was: bed.

She did not walk into a single lamppost.

"New record," said Cleo.

"I wished for it."

"You *looked* for it."

"Both things," said Yángmáo, yawning, "can be true."

And that night, in the bunk room behind Boba on Ice, the little bell in the middle of her star — the one somebody had flicked on the river path — went quiet, and stayed quiet, and let her sleep. It had done its job. It had rung once, when it needed to, without waiting for a reason. And she had listened.

That's all a bell is for.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **Your gut is a smoke alarm.** It doesn't need a reason to go off, and you don't need a reason to listen. A tight stomach or an "uh-oh" is enough.
- **You don't need proof to leave.** You'll almost never find out if you were "right." That's fine. Leaving is always allowed.
- **A wish is not a plan.** A plan is: *Where's my light? Where's my crowd? Who's my adult?* Walk toward all three.
- **Walk like you have somewhere to be.** Head up, shoulders back, hands free, steady pace. Put the phone and the sketchbook away.
- **Look at the person.** A clear look and a loud "Good evening" says *I see you and I'll remember you.* Easy targets are people who haven't noticed. You're not easy.
- **Rude is cheap.** Crossing the street, turning around, walking into a shop, being "too loud" — if he was nice, he's forgotten by dinner. If he wasn't, you just won.
- **Orange coat = make a plan.** Something feels off, you don't know what yet — that's exactly when you decide where you're going *before* anything happens.
