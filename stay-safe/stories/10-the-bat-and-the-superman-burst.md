# Story 10: The Bat and the Superman Burst

**Main Character:** Zhīma (Sesame ball)
**Guest Characters:** Coach Rosa the Flamingo, Slowy the Sloth, Bīng, Tico the Toucan, Herschel the Walrus, Finnegan Flipsworth
**Setting:** The Pink Belt Academy — the lodge's boot-drying room — and the frozen lake outside it, on a bright cold morning with the boot dryers humming
**Theme:** When something is swinging at you, backing away keeps you in the danger. Sometimes the safest direction is straight in — and then straight out.
**Mandarin Spotlight:** **衝** (chōng) = charge / burst
**Safety Skill:** Defending against a swinging stick or bat — run if you can; if trapped, burst in along the arm, control it, take it, run, scan

---

Bīng arrived at the Pink Belt Academy wearing a cape.

It was not a real cape. It was a Cocoa Hut towel, the big striped kind, knotted around his glittering shaved-ice shoulders and trailing behind him across the boot-drying room floor, where it soaked up a puddle and immediately got heavier.

"I HEARD," Bīng announced, "that today's lesson is called *SUPERMAN.*"

"The Superman *burst,*" said Coach Rosa the Flamingo, from her perch on an upturned boot bin. She was, as always, seven feet of flamingo balanced on one leg like a very tall pink question mark. "Different."

"I AM READY TO FLY."

"You are ready to *burst.* Nobody flies. Flying is for—" Rosa's eyes drifted shut. Her head sank an inch. There was a long, peaceful pause in which a boot dryer clicked off and every kid in the room held their breath. Then she snapped awake mid-word. "—shrimp. Where was I. Excellent. Two feet down, hands up! Everybody up!"

Zhīma was already up. Zhīma had been up since before the room existed. He was a sesame ball — golden-fried, perfectly round, crusted all over in white seeds that rattled when he was excited, which was now — and if there was one thing in the entire world Zhīma knew how to do, it was *go toward things.*

"What are we bursting *at?*" he asked, bouncing.

Rosa reached behind the boot bin and produced a long blue foam pool noodle.

"Señor Fideo," she said gravely. "Herschel's pool noodle. Today, he is a bat."

Tico the Toucan, perched on a coat hook with his enormous rainbow beak, cleared his throat. "IN THIS CORNER," he boomed, "weighing NOTHING, from the LODGE STORAGE CLOSET — SEÑOR FIDEEEEEO!"

The noodle bowed. It was very floppy. It was going to be a long morning.

---

Here is a thing to understand about a swinging stick, whether it's a bat, a branch, a pipe, or a very determined pool noodle.

The *end* of it is the fast part. The end of a swing moves faster than anything else in the room — faster than you can duck, faster than you can think. And the place the end swings *through* is a big curve in the air, like a rainbow drawn sideways. Rosa called that curve the **arc.**

"Slowy," said Rosa. "Come stand in the arc."

Slowy the Sloth had come up from Costa Rica on the mail-boat and had, by all accounts, only just finished getting off it. He was mossy-green and fuzzy, with long slow arms and a permanent gentle smile and sleepy half-moon eyes. He moved like honey in January.

"Puuuura…" said Slowy, beginning to stand, "…viiiida."

It took a while. Finnegan started a slow clap, stopped because it was rude, then started again because it was, honestly, motivating. Eventually Slowy stood four steps in front of Rosa and Señor Fideo.

"Now," said Rosa. "I am going to swing this very slowly at you. Very, very slowly. What do you do?"

Slowy considered the question with his whole body.

"Back… up," he said.

"Show me."

Rosa raised the noodle over her shoulder like a baseball bat. She swung — *gently, so gently, at Slowy speed* — and Slowy backed up. One slow step. Two.

And the noodle followed him. Of course it did. That was the whole point. The arc was *long,* and Slowy, backing straight away, stayed right inside it, right where the fast end lived — and *bonk*, the noodle came down on his fuzzy head in the softest, slowest bonk in the history of bonks.

"Puuura… vida…" said Slowy. "…Ow."

"Mm," said Rosa — she'd picked that up from Auntie Hù and used it *constantly* now. "You see? When you back up, you back up *into the swing.* The arc is faster than your feet. You can't out-back a bat."

"Then what do you DO?" said Bīng, cape dripping.

"First thing?" Rosa held up one pink wing. "You *run.* Far. Sideways and away. A stick has a reach of one arm plus one stick, and that's it. If you can get out of that reach, you never have to know the rest of this lesson. **Rule zero.** Best fight is the one you're not in."

"And if you *can't* run?" said Zhīma, who was practically vibrating.

Rosa smiled the long slow pink smile of a flamingo who has been waiting for exactly that question.

"Then," she said, "you go *in.*"

---

"**衝** (chōng)," said Rosa, and wrote it on the fogged-up window with one pink feather-tip. "It means *charge.* Burst. Go. Auntie Hù taught me the word last night over eggs. It's the best word in Mandarin."

She lined them up in front of a padded mat and held Señor Fideo over her shoulder again.

"Here is the secret of the arc," she said. "The *end* is fast. But the *arm* — the part right next to the body — is slow. And close to the body, the stick is barely moving at all. So when you can't run, you don't go *away* from the fast part. You go *toward* the slow part. The exact moment the arm goes *back* to swing — that's your moment. You burst *in.*"

She demonstrated, in slow motion, on Finnegan, who had volunteered without being asked.

"Arms out in front of you — *like Superman flying.*" Rosa stretched both wings forward. "Your forearms meet *his arm.* Not the stick. Never the stick. The stick is where the hurt is. His *arm* is where the *control* is. Then you're body to body — too close for him to swing anything — and you wrap that arm up tight with both of yours, like hugging a tree you're very angry at."

"Can I say something?" said Finnegan, from inside the wrap.

"No. Then — one knee, hard. *Hard parts to soft parts.* Then you take the stick, and you throw it as far as you can, and you *run,* and you *SCAN.*" She let Finnegan go. He fell over out of habit. "Everybody: **SCAN.**"

Every head in the room whipped left, right, over the shoulder, like a field of sunflowers that had heard a noise. Finnegan scanned from the floor.

"So it's," said Zhīma slowly, working it out, "*in* — arms out — arm not stick — wrap — knee — take — throw — run — scan."

"And then?"

"…Shrimp?"

"*And then shrimp.* Zhīma, you're up. *¡Dale!*"

---

Here is a thing to understand about Zhīma. He had spent his whole life rolling *into* things — snowbanks, topping gardens, danger. Auntie Hù had been trying all season to teach him, the hard way and the soft way, that rolling in is not always the right move. But *this* was a lesson that said: *the going-in part is right. Just pick the moment, and pick the stop.*

Rosa raised Señor Fideo. Zhīma planted both feet — *two feet down, hands up* — and watched her shoulder.

The noodle went back.

And Zhīma went *in.*

Not rolling — bursting. Arms straight out ahead like a golden-fried Superman, forearms meeting Rosa's pink wing at the wrist and shoving it up and away before the noodle had moved six inches. Body to body, sesame seeds pressed against pink feathers. Both arms wrapped the wing like a hug. One knee, *thump*, into the padded mat Rosa was holding against her side. Then his little crunchy hands closed on Señor Fideo, yanked, and *threw* — and the pool noodle sailed across the boot-drying room, bounced off a dryer, and landed in a boot.

Zhīma ran three steps, stopped, and *scanned* — left, right, over the shoulder.

"…Did I—"

"*That,*" said Rosa, and she was not sleeping now, not even a little, "is the best burst I have seen since a pelican in Puntarenas. You did not wait. You did not back up. You went to the *arm.* You *stopped.* You *scanned.*"

"AND HE STUCK THE LANDING, FOLKS," bellowed Tico. "THE CROWD IS ON ITS FEET!"

Zhīma's seeds rattled so hard three of them fell off. "I roll into things," he said, a little dazed. "This is my whole *life.*"

"No," said Rosa. "Rolling into things is your *habit.* Bursting is your *skill.* The difference is: you *choose* the moment, and you *choose* the stop."

---

"MY TURN," said Bīng.

He strode to the mat with his cape streaming behind him — it had picked up two more puddles and a boot lace — and took his stance. Sunglasses on. Chin down. Hands up.

Rosa raised the noodle. Bīng watched the shoulder. The noodle went back.

Bīng *burst.*

He was, for one shining instant, magnificent — arms straight out, glittering, a mountain of shaved ice in full flight—

—and the cape caught on the end of Señor Fideo.

Which meant that when Bīng went forward, the noodle came *with* him. The cape went taut. Bīng went *up*, briefly, then *around*, and then landed flat on his back in the middle of the mat with a pool noodle on his chest and a towel over his face, dripping.

"THE CAPE," said Bīng, from under the towel, "WAS A MISTAKE."

"Mm," said Rosa.

"I WOULD LIKE TO TRY AGAIN. WITHOUT THE CAPE."

"That," said Rosa, unknotting it, "is the second-best sentence anyone has said in this room today."

Without the cape, Bīng's burst was — Rosa would later admit — *dramatic in the correct direction.* Arm, wrap, knee, a puff of shaved ice, the noodle into a *different* boot, and a scan so theatrical that Tico gave him a score.

"NINE POINT FIVE," said Tico. "POINTS OFF FOR THE FIRST ONE."

"THE FIRST ONE WAS ART."

---

The last one up was Slowy.

The room got quiet in a different way. Rosa raised Señor Fideo, and — because she understood something about sloths — she swung *slower* than Slowy. So slow that Finnegan whispered, "Is it happening?" and Zhīma whispered, "I think it's happening."

The noodle went back.

Slowy's long arms came up. Out. Straight ahead. It took a while. His forearms drifted through the air like two green branches in a very gentle wind, and met Rosa's wing — the *arm*, not the noodle — with the softest, most deliberate *pat* anyone had ever seen.

He leaned in. Body to body. Wrapped the wing. Raised one knee — slowly, majestically, like a drawbridge — and touched it to the mat.

He took the noodle.

He turned. He looked at the far side of the room. He *threw*, and the noodle traveled perhaps four feet and landed with tremendous dignity on Finnegan.

Then Slowy took three slow steps. Stopped. And scanned — left… right… over the shoulder… — with his sleepy half-moon eyes.

"Puuura," he said, "…viiida."

The room *exploded.* Tico fell off the coat hook. Bīng wept openly into what remained of the cape. Zhīma bounced so high he hit a boot dryer. And Coach Rosa, standing on one leg, blinked twice, looked away, and blew her whistle three times in a row for no reason at all, which everyone in the Pink Belt Academy understood perfectly.

"Slowy," she said, when she could. "Do you know why that was perfect?"

Slowy thought about it.

"Because…" he said, "…I went… in."

"Because you went in. Not fast. Not slow. *In.*" Rosa hopped down off the boot bin. "Speed is nice. But the *direction* is the lesson. Toward the arm. Away from the arc. Then out. Anybody can learn that. Even a sloth. *Especially* a sloth." She blew her tiny whistle once, softly. "And then—"

"*Shrimp,*" said the whole room.

There was, in fact, shrimp. Barnaby had brought it. There is always enough for one more.

---

That night Zhīma lay on the boot-room mats, right under Señor Saco, who swayed very slightly overhead like a sombrero'd moon. His seeds were finally still. He turned the lesson over like a stone in his hand.

All his life people had told him to slow down. Don't roll in. Hold back. But today somebody had looked at the thing he was made of — the going-toward — and said: *that's not a flaw. That's a tool. Here's when.* The trick wasn't to stop being a sesame ball. The trick was to *choose.* In, when in is right. Out, the second out is possible. Then look around.

"Roll with it," he whispered to the ceiling, and then, because it was new and he liked how it sounded: "*Chōng.*"

Across the room, Bīng murmured in his sleep. It sounded like "the cape was a mistake."

It was. It was a wonderful mistake. Zhīma closed his eyes and went in — to sleep, which is the one place you should always, always burst toward.

## Goodnight Blessing

Arms out, little one — you reached for the whole day.
Now fold them in. The room is quiet; nothing's swinging.
You know which way to go when it counts: in, then out, then look around.
Tonight the only direction is down, into the pillow. Burst toward sleep. Go.

## 🛡️ What We Learned

- **Rule zero:** if you can run from someone swinging a stick, run — sideways and *far.* A stick reaches one arm plus one stick, no more.
- **Don't back straight up.** The end of a swing is the fast part, and backing away keeps you inside its arc. You can't out-back a bat.
- **If you're trapped, burst IN** the moment the arm goes back — arms straight out like Superman, toward the *arm*, never the stick.
- **Wrap the arm** with both of yours, body to body, so nothing can swing. One knee — hard parts to soft parts.
- **Take it, throw it far, RUN.** Then **SCAN** — look left, right, and behind for more trouble and for your exit.
- **Choose the moment, choose the stop.** Going in is a tool, not a habit. The switch goes off the second you're free.
- **This is a story, not a class.** The real moves get learned from a real coach, in a real gym, with a real pool noodle.
