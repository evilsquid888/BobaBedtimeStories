# Story 10: Tai and the Batter Fish

**Main Character:** Tai Taiyaki (taiyaki)
**Guest Characters:** Hong Dou, Dora the Dorayaki
**Setting:** A street stall
**Theme:** Fill with sweetness
**Word Spotlight:** **taiyaki** (tie-yah-kee) — fish-shaped cake

---

On a corner of the night market, where the lanterns swung like sleepy fireflies, there was a little stall that smelled like warm cake and happy mornings. Behind the counter stood **Tai Taiyaki** — a plump golden cake shaped exactly like a fish, with crispy fins, a curl of a tail, and two cheerful eyes baked right into his face. He was, in fact, a **taiyaki** (tie-yah-kee) — a fish-shaped cake — which is a very dignified thing to be, even when you are also delicious.

"Today," Tai announced, flapping a fin, "we are going FISHING."

His friend Dora the Dorayaki blinked. Dora was a round golden pancake-sandwich — two fluffy little pancakes hugging a layer of sweet filling in the middle — with bright curious eyes and crumbs of yesterday's snack still on her cheek. "There's no water here."

"Exactly!" said Tai. "We're going to fish *for* fish... by *making* fish... out of batter." He grinned his baked-in grin. "Best kind of fishing. Nobody gets wet, and at the end, you get to eat the catch."

Dora gasped with delight. This was clearly the greatest sentence she had ever heard.

---

From a cozy jar near the back, a small ruby bean hopped onto the counter. This was **Hong Dou**, the red bean — round, glossy, and the color of a tiny sunset.

"Did somebody say taiyaki?" Hong Dou bounced twice. "Because I am the SWEET part. No taiyaki is complete without me. I'm basically the treasure inside the treasure chest."

"She's not wrong," whispered Tai. "She's a little dramatic. But she's not wrong."

Hong Dou bowed so low she nearly rolled off the counter, which made Dora giggle.

"All right, little fishers," said Tai, rolling up his crispy sleeves. "If we want to catch a whole **xuexiao** (shweh-shyaow)... no wait, that means *school* like a building." He tapped his fin to his chin. "I mean a school of fish! A SCHOOL OF FISH. The swimmy kind." He laughed at himself. "Mandarin is tricky before breakfast."

"Step one," he said grandly. "We stir the batter."

Dora grabbed a big bowl. Inside went flour, a splash of milk, a crack of egg, a spoon of sugar, and a pinch of magic-that-is-actually-baking-powder. (Tai called it magic. It made the fish puff up. Close enough.)

"Now STIR," said Tai. "But gently. Batter is shy. If you whisk it too hard, it gets lumpy and grumpy."

Dora stirred slow circles. *Swish, swish, swish.* The batter went from sleepy and clumpy to smooth and silky, like pancake-pudding, like a pale golden pond.

"Ooooh," said Dora. "It looks like rainy-day clouds."

"That's a perfect batter," Tai said proudly. "Smooth as a lullaby. Not too thick, not too thin — it should pour like syrup that's in no particular hurry."

Hong Dou peered into the bowl. "It looks at me," she whispered. "It's going to become my cozy little blanket. I can FEEL it."

"You're a very poetic bean," said Tai.

"Thank you. I work on it."

---

Now came the part that needed careful hands and a careful grown-up, because the next bit was HOT — and Tai was very firm about this.

"Listen, fishers," he said, suddenly serious in a kind, twinkly way. "The fish mold is a metal pan, and a grown-up is going to make it nice and hot on the stove. Hot things are not toys. So we cheer the grown-up on, and we let THEM handle the heat. Deal?"

"Deal!" said Dora.

"Deal!" said Hong Dou, who was, after all, made of sweetness and good sense.

So a wonderful grown-up — let's call her Auntie Mei, because she had flour on her nose and twinkles in her eyes — stepped up to the warm stove. She brushed the fish-shaped mold with a little oil so the cakes would slide out happily. Then she heated it until a tiny drop of batter sizzled — *ssss!* — like the pan was saying hello.

"That sizzle," said Tai, hopping with joy, "means it's READY. The fish are about to be born!"

Dora handed Auntie Mei the bowl, and Auntie Mei poured the batter into the fish-shaped holes — just enough to coat the bottom, like filling a tiny pool.

"Not too much yet!" coached Tai. "We need room for the treasure."

And here Hong Dou drew herself up to her full and very small height.

"It is MY time," she announced.

Auntie Mei scooped a soft spoonful of sweet red bean paste — smooth, jammy, the color of cozy — and laid it gently right down the middle of each batter fish.

"That's me!" Hong Dou cried, delighted. "I'm the heart! I'm the cozy center! I'm the surprise that makes you go *mmmf* in the middle!"

"You are the filling," Tai agreed warmly. "And that, dear Dora, is the whole secret of taiyaki. You **fill it with sweetness**. A taiyaki without filling is just a sleepy little cake. But with the sweet in the middle?" He kissed his fin. "It's a treasure."

Then Auntie Mei poured a little MORE batter right on top of the red bean, tucking Hong Dou in completely, like pulling a golden blanket over a sleepy bean.

"Goodnight, treasure," whispered Dora.

"Goodnight," sighed Hong Dou, very cozy and very surrounded by cake.

---

"And NOW," said Tai, fins trembling with excitement, "the grand FLIP."

Auntie Mei closed the two halves of the mold together — *clunk* — pressing the top fish onto the bottom fish so they hugged into one whole fish. Then she flipped the whole mold over on the stove so it could cook on both sides.

"Why do we flip it?" asked Dora.

"So both sides get golden!" said Tai. "Nobody wants a fish that's tan on top and pale on the bottom. We want him toasty all the way around. Fair and square. Fish and... square." He paused. "I'll keep working on the jokes."

"Please don't," said Hong Dou from inside the mold, muffled and happy.

They waited. And waiting, it turns out, is the hardest part of fishing.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.* The cakes sizzled softly. The whole stall filled with the smell of warm sugar and toasted cake, the kind of smell that makes your tummy sit up and pay attention.

"Patience," said Tai, doing a little fishing-mime with an invisible rod. "You can't rush a golden fish. If you open the mold too early, he comes out pale and sad and squishy. But if you wait..." He sniffed the air. "Just a little longer..."

"This is the fishing part, isn't it," said Dora, wiggling with the waiting.

"This is the fishing part," Tai agreed. "Every fisher knows: the river decides when. We just hold the rod and believe." He winked. "And smell the lovely smells while we wait, which is a perk that real fishing absolutely does not have."

Hong Dou, snug inside her cake, gave a tiny muffled cheer. "It's getting toasty in here! Cozy-toasty! Best nap I've ever had!"

Dora pressed her nose to the counter. Hong Dou hummed a cozy tune from inside her cake. Auntie Mei peeked carefully under the mold — *just a peek* — and smiled.

"Golden," she said.

"GOLDEN!" everyone shouted.

---

Auntie Mei popped open the mold, and out tumbled the most beautiful fish-shaped cakes you ever saw — crispy-edged, sunshine-gold, with little baked smiles and curly tails, steaming and proud.

"It's ALIVE!" cheered Dora.

"It's DELICIOUS," corrected Tai, "which is even better."

She let them cool just a moment (because fresh-from-the-mold means HOT, and we are wise fishers). Then Tai handed Dora a warm taiyaki of her very own.

"Now," he said, eyes twinkling, "for the most important rule in all of taiyaki history." He leaned in like he was sharing a great secret. "You always bite the tail first."

"Why the tail?" asked Dora.

"Because then," whispered Tai, "you save the BELLY for last — and the belly is where all the sweet red bean is hiding. You eat your way toward the treasure. You save the best bite of all."

So Dora bit the tail. *Crunch.* Warm and crispy and cakey. She chewed her way along — *nibble, nibble* — until she reached the middle, and there it was: a burst of soft, sweet, jammy red bean, warm as a hug.

"MMMF!" said Dora, exactly as promised.

"THAT'S ME!" said Hong Dou proudly, from the new taiyaki she was peeking out of. "Sweet in the middle, every single time."

Tai looked around at his cozy little stall — at the warm pan, the flour on Auntie Mei's nose, the lanterns swinging, his friend with red bean on her chin and the biggest smile in the whole night market.

"You know," he said softly, "the best part of a taiyaki isn't even the eating. It's the *sharing*. A fish you make with friends always tastes the sweetest."

And so they shared the whole school of batter fish — one for Dora, one for Auntie Mei, one for the lantern-seller next door, and three for Hong Dou (because she insisted she needed to "quality check the filling"), until everyone's tummies were warm and round and content.

## How to Make taiyaki 🍽️
*Ask a grown-up to help with anything hot or sharp!*
1. Stir the batter — flour, milk, egg, sugar, and a little baking powder — gently into a smooth, syrupy mix.
2. Have a grown-up brush the fish mold with oil and heat it until a drop of batter sizzles.
3. Have the grown-up pour batter into the bottom of each fish shape (just enough to coat it).
4. Add a spoonful of sweet red bean paste in the middle of each fish.
5. Pour a little more batter on top to cover the red bean completely.
6. Have the grown-up close the mold, then cook and flip it so both sides turn golden.
7. Pop the finished taiyaki out, let it cool a moment, then bite the tail first and save the sweet belly for last!
