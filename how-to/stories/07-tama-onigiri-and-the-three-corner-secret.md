# Story 7: Tama and the Three Corners

**Main Character:** Tama Onigiri (onigiri)
**Guest Characters:** Niko
**Setting:** A park picnic
**Theme:** Care matters
**Word Spotlight:** **onigiri** (oh-nee-ghee-ree) — rice ball

---

Tama Onigiri was a plump white triangle of rice the size of a small fist, wrapped halfway round with a crisp black belt of seaweed, and on this bright park morning he was bouncing across the picnic blanket like a snowball that had drunk three cups of sunshine. He had three soft corners, two squinty happy eyes, and a smile so wide you could practically see his rice grains grinning.

"Picnic! Picnic! PICNIC!" he sang, doing a little triangle twirl that knocked over the napkins, the chopsticks, and a perfectly innocent dumpling.

"Sorry, dumpling," said Tama, helping it back up.

Niko Nigiri laughed from his spot on the blanket — a tiny pillow of moonlight rice with a glossy fish-slice blanket — and patted the grass beside him.

"Tama," he said, "you promised to teach me your *biggest* secret today."

Tama froze mid-twirl. His three corners wiggled with delight.

"You mean," he gasped, "the Secret of the Three Corners?"

---

This was the most exciting thing that had ever happened to Tama, because nobody had *ever* asked him to teach them anything. Usually Tama was the one learning — last week Niko had taught *him* how to be gentle, and he was still very proud of his gentle hands, thank you very much.

But **onigiri** (oh-nee-ghee-ree) — the rice ball? *That* was Tama's whole entire family. That was practically his name.

"I will teach you everything," Tama announced, standing very tall on his pointiest corner. "But first you must learn the One Big Rule of onigiri."

"Gentle hands?" guessed Niko.

"Even *bigger* than gentle hands," said Tama. He leaned in close and whispered, very seriously, "**Care matters.** An onigiri is just rice and a little salt and a little love — so the love is the part you can *taste*. If you make it grumpy, it tastes grumpy. If you make it with care, it tastes like a hug you can hold."

Niko's eyes went round and shiny. "I want to make a hug I can hold," he said.

"Then we'd better make some rice," said Tama, "and rice needs a hot pot — which calls for the ancient signal of the rice-ball clans." He cupped his little hands around his mouth, took an enormous breath, and hollered, "IS THERE A KIND PERSON WITH OVEN MITTS IN THIS PARK?" — because some things are simply a Grown-Up's job, and a hot, steamy, bubbling pot of rice is at the very tippity-top of that list.

---

Now, the wonderful thing about a park picnic is that Grown-Ups travel in herds. Before Tama's holler had even finished bouncing off the trees, a Grown-Up appeared with a sun hat and a very kind nose, oven mitts already on.

The Grown-Up had brought a fat pot of short, sticky rice, cooked that very morning until it steamed like a tiny cloud machine. The Grown-Up scooped it into a wide bowl and set it down between them.

"Step one," said Tama, hopping up to the bowl, "is the rice — but here is the part *everybody* forgets. The rice must **cool down** first."

"Why?" asked Niko.

"Two reasons," said Tama, counting on his corners. "One: hot rice goes *yowch* on your hands, and we do not make onigiri with yowchy hands. Two: hot rice is too soft and sleepy to hold its shape — it needs to rest a teeny minute and gather itself, like waking up slowly on a Sunday."

So they waited. Tama tried very hard to wait patiently, which for Tama meant only flipping over four times and humming a small song. The steam curled up and floated away on the breeze, and at last the rice was warm-and-friendly instead of hot-and-yowchy.

"Now," said Tama, "the secret handshake of every rice ball: **wet, salty hands.**"

He dipped his little hands in a bowl of cool water, gave them a shake — *flick flick* — and then pinched a few grains of salt and rubbed it gently between his palms.

"The water," he explained, "is so the rice doesn't stick to you and turn you into a rice mitten. The salt is so the rice tastes like *something*. Wet hands, salty hands, happy onigiri. Now you try!"

Niko dipped his hands. He flicked — *flick flick* — and a drop of water landed right on Tama's nose.

"Perfect aim," said Tama, blinking. "Ten out of ten. Couldn't have done it better myself."

---

"Now we make the very first one," said Tama, scooping up a small mound of warm rice into his cupped palm. "About one happy handful — not too big, or it'll be a rice boulder, and nobody can finish a boulder."

He cradled the rice the way Niko had taught him, soft and kind. Then he pressed his thumb into the very middle and made a cozy little dent.

"What's the dent for?" asked Niko.

"This," said Tama proudly, "is the treasure room."

From a little dish the Grown-Up had set out, Tama plucked a wrinkly ruby-red **umeboshi** (oo-meh-boh-shee) — a pickled plum, sour and bright as a tiny sunrise — and tucked it snug into the dent.

"You can hide a plum," he said, "or a flake of tuna, or anything cozy. The treasure room keeps a happy little surprise right in the middle, so every bite is a present."

Niko cupped his own rice and pressed his thumb in — *too* hard, and the rice squished out the sides like a yawning starfish.

"Oops," he giggled.

"That's okay!" said Tama brightly. "*Care* doesn't mean *perfect*. It means you try with a soft heart and you don't give up. Squish it back together and make another dent — gentle this time."

Niko tried again, softly, and made the tidiest little dent you ever saw. He nestled a flake of tuna inside and folded the rice over the top to hide the treasure away.

"Now it has a secret," he whispered.

"Now it has a secret," Tama agreed, very pleased.

---

"And now," said Tama, his corners practically vibrating, "the most famous part. The part *I'm* named after." He took a deep, dramatic breath. "We make the **three corners.**"

He cupped both palms together, the rice nestled between them like a sleepy egg, and showed Niko the magic.

"Press your hands into a little roof shape — like a tiny house — to make the *first* corner up top. Then turn the rice a third of the way round and press again for the *second* corner. Then turn it once more and press for the *third*. One, two, three — turn, press, turn, press, turn, press."

Tama's hands moved in a slow, careful triangle dance. *Turn, press. Turn, press. Turn, press.* And there, in his palms, sat a plump and perfect little three-cornered onigiri.

"It's a TRIANGLE!" Niko shouted, so loud a nearby pigeon fell off its branch.

"Care and corners," said Tama. "That's the whole trick."

Niko turned and pressed, turned and pressed, turning his rice ball gently round and round. His first corner was a bit lumpy. His second corner leaned like a sleepy tower. But his third corner came out *just right* — and somehow all three together made the sweetest, roundest, most lovingly wonky little triangle in the entire park.

"Mine looks like it's giving me a hug," said Niko softly.

"That," said Tama, "is because you made it with care. Lumpy corners and all. Now for the very last touch."

---

From a crackly sheet the Grown-Up handed over — carefully, no sharp knife needed, just a tidy strip already torn to size — Tama wrapped a black belt of **nori** (noh-ree) — roasted seaweed — around the bottom of each triangle.

"The nori belt," he said, "is so your fingers stay clean *and* so the onigiri has a little jacket to keep it cozy. Pat it on gently. There. Now it's dressed for the picnic."

Two finished onigiri sat on the blanket, plump and proud — one tidy and one lovingly wonky — each wearing a crisp seaweed jacket, each hiding a happy secret in its treasure room.

"We made onigiri," breathed Niko.

"We made hugs you can hold," said Tama.

They wet their hands and made a whole little family of them after that — plum ones and tuna ones, tidy ones and wobbly ones, a teeny one for a passing ladybug who looked hungry. They wrapped each in its nori jacket and lined them up in the picnic basket, neat and snug, all three corners pointing up at the bright blue sky.

"Aren't we going to eat them?" asked Niko, peering into the basket.

"Ah," said Tama, gently closing the lid, "that's the *other* wonderful thing about onigiri. They keep. They travel. They wait, snug in their little jackets, until exactly the moment somebody needs a hug they can hold. These are for *tomorrow's* picnic — and tomorrow, every bite will taste like today's sunshine."

So instead of eating, they traded compliments, which turned out to be nearly as filling.

"Your corners," said Niko solemnly, "are the proudest corners in this entire park."

"Your treasure-room dent," said Tama, just as solemnly, "was the tidiest I have ever seen, and I have seen my whole family's."

The ladybug, who had already nibbled her teeny one down to the last grain, said nothing at all — but she bowed twice before flying off, which everyone agreed was the highest compliment of the afternoon.

"Tama," said Niko, "your secret is the best secret."

Tama beamed all three of his corners at once.

"It's not really a secret," he said. "It's just care. You put it in, and you get it right back out." And he leaned back against the packed picnic basket, warm and happy and prouder than any rice ball had ever been, and watched the sunshine wobble through the leaves.

## How to Make onigiri 🍽️
*Ask a grown-up to help with anything hot or sharp!*
1. Ask a grown-up to cook short, sticky rice in a hot pot, then let it cool until it's warm-and-friendly (not hot-and-yowchy!).
2. Wet your hands with cool water, then rub a little salt between your palms so the rice won't stick and the onigiri tastes just right.
3. Scoop one small handful of warm rice, press a gentle dent in the middle, and tuck in a pickled plum or a flake of tuna — then fold the rice over to hide your treasure.
4. Cup your hands into a little roof shape and press to make a corner; turn the rice a third of the way and press again, and once more — turn, press, turn, press — until you have three corners and a tidy triangle.
5. Wrap a strip of nori (seaweed) around the bottom, then pack your onigiri up snug for a picnic — they keep beautifully and taste like sunshine tomorrow!

## Goodnight Blessing

Tuck your worries away like a plum in a treasure room, and let the day fold gently closed. You made it three corners strong, and tomorrow is waiting, bright and sticky-sweet. Rest now, snug as rice in its cozy seaweed jacket. Goodnight.
