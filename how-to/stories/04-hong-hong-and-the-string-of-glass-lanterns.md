# Story 4: Hong Hong and the Glass Lanterns

**Main Character:** Hong Hong (candied fruit)
**Guest Characters:** Boba
**Setting:** A winter market
**Theme:** Tough then sweet
**Word Spotlight:** 冰糖葫蘆 (bing tang hulu) = candied hawthorn on a stick

---

On the coldest night of the whole winter, when the market lanterns puffed little clouds of steam and everybody's breath looked like dragon smoke, a tiny ruby-red fruit went rolling down the icy lane shouting, "Make way! Make way! Sparkly fellow coming through!"

That was **Hong Hong** (hong hong) = red red, a row of round hawthorn fruits dressed head to toe in a coat of crackly glass sugar. Each fruit gleamed like a polished marble, and when he wobbled, the whole string of him went *tick-tick-tick* like a wind chime made of candy. He was, he would happily tell you, the shiniest thing in the market.

"Shine outside, sweet inside!" Hong Hong sang, skidding to a stop and bonking — *gloop* — straight into a soft, dark, jiggly someone.

That someone was Boba, a glossy tapioca pearl bundled in a scarf three times too big. "Oof," said Boba, bouncing twice. "You're cold and pointy and also extremely loud."

"I am festive," said Hong Hong proudly. "And tonight, my squishy friend, I am going to teach you the greatest winter treat in all of Taiwan."

---

Boba untangled herself from her enormous scarf. "Greater than warm soy milk?"

"Greater," said Hong Hong, "than even *that*. We are making **冰糖葫蘆** (bing tang hulu) = candied hawthorn on a stick. Strings of fruit dressed in glass sugar, so they shine like little lanterns you can eat."

Boba's whole pearly body lit up. "Lanterns I can EAT? Why has no one told me about this sooner? Where do we begin?"

"We begin," said Hong Hong grandly, "with a bath."

"A *bath*?" Boba clutched her scarf. "It's freezing!"

"Not for *us*, you wobbly noodle. For the fruit." Hong Hong led her to Auntie Lotus's fruit stall, where bowls of fresh red hawthorn berries sat plump and bright. "First we wash them. Cold water, gentle hands. Roll them around — *swish, swish* — until every speck of market dust floats away."

So they washed the berries, and Boba splashed so enthusiastically that she gave herself a little soap-bubble hat and didn't notice for a full minute. A passing peanut-mochi pointed and giggled, and Boba bowed grandly as if the hat were the height of winter fashion, which made the whole stall snort with laughter.

"Now," said Hong Hong, "the most important part that *everyone forgets*. We dry them. Bone dry. Every single one."

"Why does it matter?" asked Boba, patting a berry with a cloth.

"Because," said Hong Hong, lowering his voice like he was sharing a great secret, "water and hot sugar are sworn enemies. One drop of water on a berry, and later the sugar will hiss and sputter and refuse to stick. Dry fruit equals shiny fruit. That is the law."

Boba dried each berry like she was tucking it into bed. "There. Cozy and dry. Now what?"

"Now we line them up on sticks." Hong Hong nudged over a bundle of clean bamboo skewers, the long thin kind. "We poke each berry onto the skewer — *pop, pop, pop* — until we have a neat little tower of fruit. Four or five per stick. Like beads on a string."

Boba threaded her first berry and held it up, beaming. "It looks like a tiny red traffic light!"

"Wait until there are five of them," said Hong Hong. "Then it looks like a whole row of traffic lights all stuck on stop, which is the most delicious kind of traffic jam in the world."

Boba giggled and threaded another, and another, until her first skewer stood up straight and proud. She held it like a wand and tried to do a magic spell. Nothing happened, except a berry nearly slid off, and Hong Hong yelped, "Careful! No magic until the sugar! Magic comes *later*!"

"Skewers are sharp at the tip," Hong Hong reminded her, "so this is a job where we *ask a grown-up to help* with the pointy poking. No squishy fingers in the way."

Right on cue, Auntie Lotus leaned over the stall — a tall, kindly winter melon with frost on her shoulders — and helped them push the trickiest berries on, holding each stick steady. "Lovely beads," she rumbled. "Now bring them to the fire when you're ready, little ones. The sugar part is *my* job."

Boba lined up her skewers like proud little soldiers. "We did it! They're ready!"

"They're ready for the *tough* part," said Hong Hong, and for just a moment, even the shiniest fruit in the market looked a teensy bit nervous. "The sugar."

---

Auntie Lotus set a heavy pot on the stove, and into it went sugar and a splash of water — clear and plain and boring-looking. "Now we wait," she said, "and we do *not* stir, and we do *not* touch. This pot gets hotter than anything in the whole market. Hotter than soup. Hotter than tea. This is a grown-up's job, and a grown-up's job *only*."

"So we just... watch?" Boba whispered, tiptoeing backward until she was a safe, cozy distance from the stove. Hong Hong tiptoed back beside her. Even the bravest, shiniest fruit in the market knew that hot sugar was not a thing to crowd around.

"We watch," said Hong Hong, "and here's the magic. The sugar bubbles. First big lazy bubbles — *blub... blub.* Then fast tiny ones — *blibblibblib!* Auntie Lotus watches the color and tests it until it reaches the 'hard-crack' stage. That's when a drop of syrup, dripped into cold water, turns into a hard, glassy thread you can *snap*."

Boba's eyes went wide as moons. "How does she *know* when it's ready?"

"Patience," said Auntie Lotus, "and a careful eye. Too soon, the sugar stays sticky and chewy. Too late, it turns brown and bitter. Just right, and it sets like glass." She dripped a tiny bead of syrup into a cup of cold water. *Tink.* It hardened into a thread, and she snapped it crisp between two frosty fingers. "There. Hard-crack. Now — *quickly*, little ones, but stand back, because this is hot, hot, hot."

This, you should know, is the moment Hong Hong loved best. Because the syrup looked plain and dull and not the least bit special — but it held a secret. Something tough and clear was about to become the sweetest thing of all.

"Roll!" cried Hong Hong. "Roll the skewers!"

Auntie Lotus took each skewer — keeping every squishy friend at a safe, cozy distance — and tilted it sideways into the bubbling pot, rolling it round and round so the hot sugar wrapped each berry in a thin, even coat. *Swirl, swirl, swirl.* The dull syrup clung on and went suddenly, dazzlingly clear, like the berries had been dipped in liquid glass.

"Oooooooh," breathed Boba.

"Told you," said Hong Hong smugly, sparkling extra hard.

One by one, Auntie Lotus laid the glistening skewers down on a tray she'd brushed with a thin slick of oil. "The oil keeps them from sticking," she explained. "Now the cold does the rest." And out in the frosty winter air, the magic finished all by itself: the clear sugar cooled, and hardened, and set into a smooth shining shell. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* You could hear it growing crisp.

Boba reached out a careful pearly hand. "Is it... can I...?"

"Wait for it to cool, my impatient dumpling," said Hong Hong. "Hot sugar is for grown-ups; cooled sugar is for *snacking*. Count to a slow hundred."

Boba counted to a slow hundred. Then she counted to a fast ten just to be safe. Then she picked up a finished skewer of bing tang hulu — five ruby berries glowing under their glassy coats, catching the lantern light, every one a tiny red lantern she could hold in her hand.

"Now," said Hong Hong softly, "the very best part. The crunch."

Boba bit down.

*CRUNCH.*

The glass-sugar shell shattered into a hundred sparkles — *tough!* — and then underneath came the fruit — *sweet!* — sour and bright and juicy all at once.

Boba chewed. Boba blinked. Boba did a small, slow, delighted wobble that made her whole scarf swish.

"It's tough on the outside," she said in wonder, "and sweet on the inside. Just like the *making* of it. The hot part was scary, and the waiting was hard, and then — *crunch* — the sweet showed up after all."

"That," said Hong Hong, glowing ruby-bright, "is the whole entire secret of winter."

---

They shared the rest under the lanterns. Hong Hong handed skewers to a shivering sticky-rice cake, to a sleepy peanut-mochi, to Auntie Lotus, and to three tiny dumplings who lined up clutching their coins. Every one of them bit down — *crunch!* — and every one of them did the small, slow, delighted wobble.

"Shine outside," sang Hong Hong, "sweet inside!"

And the whole frosty market chimed back *tick-tick-tick*, glowing like a string of little eaten lanterns, all the way down the lane.

## How to Make bing tang hu lu 🍽️
*Ask a grown-up to help with anything hot or sharp!*
1. Wash fresh hawthorn berries (or other firm fruit) in cold water, then dry them completely — bone dry, so the sugar will stick.
2. Carefully poke 4 or 5 berries onto each bamboo skewer, like beads on a string (ask a grown-up to help with the sharp tip).
3. A grown-up boils sugar and a little water in a pot — no stirring — until it reaches hard-crack stage (VERY hot: when a syrup drop in cold water snaps like glass).
4. A grown-up rolls each skewer in the hot syrup so every berry gets a thin, even coat, then lays them on an oiled tray.
5. Let them cool in cool air until the sugar sets hard and glassy — then crunch!
