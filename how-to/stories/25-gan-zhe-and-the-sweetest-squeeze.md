# Story 25: Gan Zhe and the Squeeze

**Main Character:** Gan Zhe (sugarcane juice)
**Guest Characters:** Mango, Auntie Cane
**Setting:** Auntie Cane's juice stall, night market, festival night
**Theme:** Being a good helper is its own skill
**Word Spotlight:** **ganzhe zhi** (gahn-juh jr) — sugarcane juice

---

On the lantern-brightest evening of the whole year, just as dusk turned the sky the color of plum tea and the night market flickered awake stall by stall, Gan Zhe was lounging on his wobbly stool under the striped awning, doing what he did best — which was, to be honest, lounging.

Gan Zhe was a sugarcane. Not a *little* sugarcane. A *tall* one, so green-gold and so long that he had to fold himself in half just to fit under the awning, with leafy hair flopping over one eye like a curtain that had given up. He yawned at the first stars. He said the thing he said a hundred times a day:

"Squeeze the day."

"Squeeze the *what*?" said a round, golden voice, and there was Mango — plump, sunset-orange, so juicy-looking that two passing bees stopped to apologize for staring. "I came to learn the sweetest drink in the market!"

And that is exactly when the drums started.

*BOOM. Boom-boom. BOOM.*

Down at the market gate, a festival parade was pouring in — lion dancers, lantern-carriers, and behind them a crowd so big it looked like the whole town had decided to be thirsty at the same time.

Auntie Cane burst out from the back of the stall. She was the oldest, kindest, most enormous sugarcane in the whole market, with reading glasses on her nose and arms like polished green broomsticks.

"Festival night," she said, rolling up her sleeves. "No lessons tonight, sweet ones. Tonight we *work*."

---

"But I don't know how!" squeaked Mango.

"Then here is the fastest lesson in the history of juice," said Auntie Cane, and she walked them down the stall, pointing as she went, quick as a drumbeat.

"This drink is **ganzhe zhi** (gahn-juh jr) — sugarcane juice, and it gets made in four moves. *One:* the canes get **rinsed**, because sugarcane grows in the dirt, snug as a worm in a sock, and nobody orders a glass of mud. *Two:* I **peel and press**. I strip the tough skin off with my blade — it tastes like a fence — cut the cane into short pieces, and feed them into the press." She patted a heavy machine with two metal rollers. It went *whirrrr* in its sleep, like a dog dreaming of chasing things. "The rollers *chomp*. They flatten cane like pancakes and squeeze the juice right out. Blade and rollers are grown-up work, full stop. The press is the *stall's* job — it belongs to me alone."

"And three and four?" said Mango, bouncing.

"*Three:* the juice comes out full of soft little fibers, so it gets **strained** through a fine mesh until it's silky. *Four:* it gets **poured over ice**, cold and bright, and handed to a happy customer." Auntie Cane straightened her glasses. "Rinsing, straining, icing, pouring, and keeping the line cheerful — those jobs are *yours*. Your jobs are the rest. Can you do them?"

Gan Zhe sat up so fast his leaf hair flipped backward. "We can absolutely—"

"Yes!" shouted Mango, who had no idea if they could.

"Good," said Auntie Cane, "because here they come."

---

The crowd hit the stall like a warm, hungry wave.

"Two juices!" "Four juices!" "Seven juices, and one for the lion, he's been dancing!" "Is the lion allowed juice?" "He's a *dancer*, he's allowed anything!"

Auntie Cane planted herself at the press and became a machine herself — peel, *chunk-chunk-chunk*, feed the rollers, *whirrrr*, juice streaming into the pitcher like sunshine deciding to become a liquid. She never hurried and she never stopped, which it turns out is the same thing as being very fast.

Gan Zhe took the strainer station, pouring cloudy green juice through the mesh into pitcher after pitcher and watching it come out the other side smooth and silky instead of fuzzy. Mango took the wash-tub, scrubbing muddy canes under the cool tap — top to bottom, like tickling a very long, very serious cucumber — and stacking them clean and squeaky at Auntie's elbow.

Water went everywhere. It went on the awning. It went on the stools. It went, somehow, *up*, which water is not supposed to do, and came back down on Gan Zhe's head.

"You're very good at being splashy," said Gan Zhe, blinking.

"It's right next to being squished," said Mango, "on my list of talents."

For ten whole minutes, it worked. The line moved. The juice flowed. Gan Zhe even allowed himself one small proud lean against the counter.

Never lean.

---

The first thing to go wrong was the strainer.

Gan Zhe poured, and instead of a silky green ribbon, the juice just… sat there. The mesh had clogged solid with cane fibers — a soggy pale haystack — and fresh juice pooled on top, going nowhere, while the pitcher under it stayed empty and the line got longer.

"It's stuck!" Gan Zhe shook it. Bad idea. Juice sloshed onto his feet.

"Don't shake it, *swap* it!" Mango came skidding over, grabbed the spare strainer off its hook, and slapped it into his hands. Then she carried the clogged one to her tub, knocked the fiber-wad out — *thwap* — rinsed the mesh clean, and hung it back up. "You strain with one, I rinse the other. We take turns, like a seesaw!"

"A juice seesaw," said Gan Zhe, already pouring again. "You're a genius."

"I contain multitudes," said Mango. "Mostly juice, but also multitudes."

The seesaw worked beautifully — swap, strain, rinse, swap — right up until Gan Zhe scooped into the ice cooler and his scoop went *scrape* against the bottom.

The ice was gone.

A market on festival night does not want warm sugarcane juice. A market on festival night wants its juice *cold*, and seventeen faces were watching him hold an empty scoop.

"Mango," said Gan Zhe, very calmly, "hold the line. I'll be back in ninety seconds."

And he *ran* — unfolded to his full broom-handle height and went flying through the crowd, leaf hair streaming, ducking lanterns, hurdling a stroller, apologizing to a noodle stall he sideswiped — all the way to the shaved-ice stall three rows over, where his friend Bing took one look at his face, asked zero questions, and buried him in a borrowed bucket of ice and a cheer of "**Jiayou!** (jyah-yo) — go, go, you can do it!"

Back at the stall, Mango faced the line alone. So she did the only thing she knew how to do, which was to be extremely Mango at it.

"While we wait," she announced, "fun fact! Sugarcane is a *grass*. You are all about to drink fancy grass! Second fun fact: the sweetness is already inside the cane — the press doesn't add anything, it just lets it *out*. Third fun fact: I once splashed water so hard it went *up*."

The line, which had been grumbling, started grinning. One little kid asked for fun fact number four. (It was also about splashing.)

Then Gan Zhe thundered back in with the ice bucket, the cubes went *tink-tink-tink* into the cooler, and the cold came back to the corner of the market — at the exact moment Mango spun around to cheer, bumped the counter with one round hip, and watched the entire tower of paper cups tip slowly, gracefully, horribly toward the juice puddle on the floor.

Time went syrup-slow. Mango dove. Gan Zhe dove. The lion dancer at the front of the line dove, possibly out of habit.

Mango got there first, catching the tower against her belly like a goalkeeper, cups fanning out in her arms, exactly *one* cup escaping to land in the puddle with a sad little *plip*.

"Lost one," she panted. "Saved forty."

"Best catch in market history," said the lion dancer solemnly, and the whole line applauded.

---

After that, something clicked.

Mango rinsed and stacked. Auntie peeled and pressed, *whirrrr*. Gan Zhe strained and iced and poured, the green juice swirling cold and bright around the cubes. Mango ran each cup down the line two at a time — "One for you, one for you, fun fact, you're drinking grass!" — and the stall stopped being three workers and became one long, smooth, happy machine, lantern light flashing off frosty cups.

They poured for the lion dancers. They poured for the lantern-carriers. They poured for a grandmother who said it tasted exactly like festival nights when *she* was small, which made Auntie Cane pretend she had something in her eye. Twice more the strainer clogged, and twice more the juice seesaw saved it without anyone even saying a word — swap, strain, rinse, swap — which is how you know a team has stopped being new at something.

And then, somewhere past the middle of the night, the drums went home. The crowd thinned to a trickle, the trickle thinned to nobody, and the market dimmed to its sleepiest self — just lantern-glow, and crickets, and the soft *shff, shff* of stalls sweeping up. Under the striped awning, two exhausted helpers lay draped over their stools like wet noodles, while Mango weakly recited fun facts to no one. ("Fun fact. I cannot feel my arms.")

Auntie Cane pressed one last cane through the rollers, strained the juice herself, poured it over the last of Bing's ice, and set a single tall cup on the counter with three straws.

"Tonight," she said, "I watched a strainer get rescued, an ice emergency get outrun, and a cup tower get caught out of the air. You know what that tells me?"

"That we're splashy?" said Mango.

"That helping is a *skill*," said Auntie Cane. "Anyone can stand around wanting to help. But knowing your jobs, doing them right when everything's going wrong, and keeping the line smiling while you do — that takes real practice, same as the press. The press is my skill. Tonight you earned yours."

Gan Zhe smiled his slow, sun-warmed smile. "We didn't make the sweetness. We just helped it get where it was going."

"That," said Auntie Cane, "is the whole job."

So the three of them leaned together under the awning and shared the last cold cup of **ganzhe zhi**, sip by sip by sip, while the lanterns burned low and gold all down the empty market. The juice was cool from the ice, smooth from the strainer, and sweet — gently, naturally sweet — because the sweetness had been waiting inside the cane all along, and tonight a whole festival had gotten to taste it on time. Mango got a little green mustache. She did not even try to be splashy. She was too busy being proud.

"Squeeze the day," murmured Gan Zhe, eyes already closing, leaf hair flopping back over one eye where it belonged.

"Squeezed it," said Mango, around the last sip. "Every. Last. Drop."

## How Sugarcane Juice Is Made (and How You Can Help) 🍽️
*The cane press is a stall machine with sharp blades and chomping rollers — that part is for grown-up pros only. But a great helper does everything else!*
1. **Rinse:** Sugarcane grows in the dirt, so the stalks get scrubbed clean under cool water first. (Helper job!)
2. **Peel and press:** A grown-up strips off the tough outer skin, cuts the cane into short pieces, and feeds them through the press, where rollers squeeze out the pale green juice. (Grown-ups only — watch from a safe, comfy distance.)
3. **Strain:** The fresh juice is poured through a fine mesh strainer to catch the soft cane fibers — and if the mesh clogs, rinse it and keep going. (Helper job!)
4. **Ice and pour:** The smooth juice goes over a tall glass of ice, cold and bright. Hand it over with a smile — keeping the line happy is a helper job too!

## Goodnight Blessing
Sleep soft and sweet, little one. You don't have to run the big machine to matter — the rinsing hands, the steady pour, the catch at just the right moment, those are what keep the whole night running. So rest now, like a market after festival, all lantern-glow and quiet, and know that helping well is a skill you carry everywhere. Goodnight.
