# Story 25: Gan Zhe and the Squeeze

**Main Character:** Gan Zhe (sugarcane juice)
**Guest Characters:** Mango
**Setting:** A juice stand
**Theme:** Sweetness is close
**Word Spotlight:** 甘蔗汁 (ganzhe zhi) = sugarcane juice

---

On the sleepiest, sunniest corner of the night market stood a little juice stand with a striped awning and a wobbly stool, and on that wobbly stool lounged Gan Zhe, who was — and this is the absolute truth — a sugarcane.

Not a *little* sugarcane. A *tall* one. Gan Zhe was so tall and so green-gold that he had to fold himself in half just to fit under the awning, and his leafy green hair flopped right over one eye like a sleepy curtain. He stretched, he yawned, he soaked up the warm evening sun, and he said the thing he said about a hundred times a day:

"Squeeze the day."

"Squeeze the *what*?" said a round, golden voice.

Gan Zhe peeked out from under his leaf hair. Bouncing toward the stand was Mango — plump, sunset-orange, and so juicy-looking that bees followed her around just to apologize for staring. She plopped onto the second stool, completely out of breath and completely delighted.

"I heard," panted Mango, "that you make the sweetest drink in the whole market. And I want to *learn it tonight*."

Gan Zhe smiled his slow, sun-warmed smile.

"You came to the right cane," he said. "Tonight, my mango friend, you learn to make **甘蔗汁** (ganzhe zhi) = sugarcane juice."

---

Mango wiggled so hard with excitement that she nearly rolled clean off the stool. "Where do we start? Do we squish things? I'm *excellent* at being squished, it's practically my whole personality."

"We start," said Gan Zhe, "the way all good sweet things start. Slow."

He reached behind the stand and pulled out a bundle of fresh sugarcane stalks — long, knobbly, green-and-purple poles as tall as a broom and twice as proud. They clattered against each other like wooden wind chimes.

"Step one," said Gan Zhe, holding one up to the lantern light. "We **rinse the cane**. Always. Sugarcane grows in the dirt, snug as a worm in a sock, so it comes to us a little muddy and a little dusty and altogether *crunchy* in ways juice should never be crunchy."

He turned on the little tap at the side of the stand, and a cool ribbon of water came pouring out.

"Hold a stalk under the water," he said, "and rub off every speck of mud, top to bottom, like you're tickling a very long, very serious cucumber."

Mango grabbed a stalk and scrubbed it with both stubby arms. Water went everywhere. It went on the awning. It went on the stool. It went, somehow, *up*, which water is absolutely not supposed to do, and came right back down on top of Gan Zhe's leaf hair.

"You're *very* good at being splashy," said Gan Zhe, blinking a droplet off his nose.

"It's right next to being squished," said Mango proudly. "On my list of talents."

When the cane was clean — shiny, green, and squeaky — Gan Zhe set it down gently on the cutting board and got a serious, important look on his face. He even tucked his leaf hair behind one ear, which for Gan Zhe was practically a tuxedo.

"Now," he said. "The next part is the *grown-up part*."

Mango leaned in. "Ooooh. Is it the squishing?"

"It is the peeling, the cutting, and the pressing," said Gan Zhe. "And every single bit of it uses a sharp blade and a strong machine. Sugarcane skin is tough as a turtle's bath sandals, and the press has rollers that *chomp*. So we do what every smart cook does when something is sharp or strong."

"We…close our eyes and hope?" guessed Mango.

"We **ask a grown-up to help**," said Gan Zhe, and he rang the little brass bell on the counter — *ding!*

Out from the back of the stand came Auntie Cane, who was the oldest, kindest, most enormous sugarcane in the whole market, with reading glasses perched on her nose and arms like polished green broomsticks. She had run this juice stand since before Gan Zhe was even a sprout.

"Sharp work?" said Auntie Cane, rolling up her sleeves.

"Sharp work," nodded Gan Zhe.

"Step away from the blade, sweet ones," she said cheerfully, "and watch the master."

So Mango and Gan Zhe hopped back to a safe, comfy distance — close enough to *see*, far enough to be cozy — and Auntie Cane went to work.

First she **peeled** the tough outer skin off the stalk with quick, sure strokes, and the cane underneath shone pale and wet, like the inside of a secret.

"The skin is too hard to drink," she explained, "and tastes like a fence."

Then she **cut** the peeled cane into short, fat pieces, *chunk, chunk, chunk*, until she had a tidy pile that looked like a stack of pale-gold firewood.

"Smaller pieces," she said, "fit the press better. Big pieces make the machine grumble, and nobody likes a grumbly machine. Or a grumbly auntie."

"Or a grumbly mango," whispered Mango. "Though I've never tried it. I'm too busy being splashy."

Last came the best part. Auntie Cane fed the cane pieces — one by one — into the **press**, a heavy machine with two metal rollers that turned with a deep, happy *whirrrr*. The rollers squeezed each piece flat as a pancake, and out the bottom, like sunshine deciding to become a liquid, came a stream of pale green juice, trickling into a pitcher below.

"*There* it is," breathed Mango. "The squeeze!"

"The squeeze," agreed Gan Zhe, glowing. "That, my friend, is the whole heart of sugarcane juice. The sweetness was inside the cane the *entire* time. We didn't add it. We didn't cook it. We just…let it out."

He looked at Mango with his slow, kind smile.

"Sweetness isn't far away, you know. It's not something you have to chase across the whole market. It's *close*. Usually it's already right inside the thing in front of you. You just have to squeeze gently and let it come."

Mango sniffled, just a tiny happy sniffle. "That's the nicest thing a vegetable has ever said to me."

"I'm technically a grass," said Gan Zhe.

"Even *better*," said Mango.

Auntie Cane finished pressing, gave the pitcher a proud little pat, and handed it over the counter. "All yours, sweet ones. The sharp part's done. The rest is yours to finish — and it's the fun part."

Gan Zhe set the pitcher down between them. The juice inside was pale and cloudy green, beautiful and a little bit *floaty*, because it was full of tiny soft cane fibers — little threads of sugarcane that had hitched a ride during the squeeze.

"Almost ready," said Gan Zhe. "Step two. We **strain out the fibers**, so the juice is smooth and silky instead of fuzzy and ticklish."

He set a fine mesh strainer over a tall, frosty glass, and *slowly*, *carefully*, he poured the green juice through it. The strainer caught the little fibers — a soft pale tangle, like the world's tiniest haystack — and what dripped through into the glass below was smooth, clear-green, and glistening.

"You try the next pour," he said.

Mango lifted the pitcher in both arms, stuck out her tongue in concentration, and poured. She only spilled a *little*. (It went up. Then it came down on Gan Zhe.)

"Splashy," sighed Gan Zhe, fondly.

"It's my gift," said Mango.

Now there was just one thing left, and it was Mango's *favorite* kind of thing — the cold, clinky, marvelous kind. From the cooler, Gan Zhe scooped a great heap of ice cubes and tumbled them into two tall glasses, where they cracked and settled with happy little *tink-tinks*.

"Last step," he announced. "We **pour the strained juice over the ice**…"

He poured. The green juice swirled around the ice, frosting the glass, turning cool and bright and beautiful.

"…and then," said Gan Zhe, handing Mango a glass and lifting his own high, "we *gulp*."

"FINALLY," cried Mango, "a step I was BORN for!"

They clinked their glasses together — *ding!* — and they gulped.

And oh, it was good. It was the sweetest, freshest, greenest sip in the whole wide market: cool from the ice, smooth from the strainer, and sweet — so naturally, gently sweet — from the cane itself, the way it had been all along. Mango's eyes went wide as moons. A little green mustache appeared on her lip. She did not even *try* to be splashy. She just sat in the warm lantern light, completely, perfectly happy.

"Gan Zhe," she said softly, "the sweetness really *was* close."

"It always is," said Gan Zhe, leaning back on his wobbly stool, leaf hair flopping over one eye. "You just have to know how to squeeze the day."

And under the striped awning, with the warm night humming around them, two sweet friends finished every last drop.

## How to Make sugarcane juice 🍽️
*Ask a grown-up to help with anything hot or sharp!*
1. Rinse the sugarcane stalks well under cool water, scrubbing off all the dirt and dust.
2. Ask a grown-up to peel off the tough outer skin, cut the cane into short pieces, and press them through a sugarcane press to catch the green juice.
3. Pour the juice through a fine strainer to remove the soft cane fibers.
4. Pour the strained juice over a glass of ice — and gulp it down!
