# Story 12: Feng and the Gift Boxes

**Main Character:** Feng (pineapple cake)
**Guest Characters:** Bao Bao
**Setting:** A pastry kitchen
**Theme:** Sharing is a gift
**Word Spotlight:** 鳳梨酥 (fenglisu) = pineapple cake

---

On a snug golden evening in the cosiest pastry kitchen in all of Taiwan, a small square fellow named Feng was wearing a paper gift box on his head like a hat and pretending to be a very fancy chimney. Feng was a **鳳梨酥** (fenglisu) = pineapple cake — a plump buttery little brick with a crumbly golden crust and a sweet sticky pineapple heart hiding right in the middle, the kind of treat people in Taiwan tuck into ribboned boxes to give to someone they love. He smelled like sunshine and butter and a tiny bit of holiday.

"Ahoy!" Feng called, and the box slid off his head and *bonk*ed onto the flour bin. "Bao Bao! I have made a decision! Tonight we make a whole tray of pineapple cakes, and we GIVE THEM ALL AWAY."

Bao Bao the baker looked up from her mixing bowl, floury apron dusted to the elbows. "All of them?" she said. "Even the ones we get to taste?"

"*Especially* those," said Feng. "Sharing is the best part. It's the whole point of a gift box, Bao Bao — a box is just a hug you can carry."

---

Bao Bao, who was a pale-gold pillowy bun with the patient, doughy smile of someone who had never once been in a hurry, dusted off her paws and grinned. "Well, if we're giving them away, we'd better make them properly. Good things rise with time, Feng. Even pineapple cakes. Come — let me show you, step by step, the way my grandmother taught me."

Feng clapped his crumbly little hands so hard a flake fell off his corner. He picked it up and ate it. Nobody saw. (Everybody saw.)

"The first part of a **鳳梨酥** (fenglisu) = pineapple cake is the part everyone forgets about," said Bao Bao, leading him to the stove. "The *jam*. The golden heart. We make that from real pineapple, all chopped up and cooked down slow with a little sugar until it turns thick and sticky and amber, like a tiny sunset you can hold in a spoon."

On the stove sat a big heavy pot, and in it bubbled a pile of bright pineapple, hissing and steaming and smelling like a beach holiday.

"Can I stir it?!" said Feng, hopping.

"You can *cheer* for it," said Bao Bao gently, "from this exact spot, right here, two whole steps back. That pot is **HOT** — hot enough to say a very rude word to your fingers. Simmering the jam is a grown-up job." She waved her flour-dusty paw, and Grandma Lo, the kindly old taro-root who ran the kitchen's stove, shuffled over with a long wooden spoon.

"Jam duty?" said Grandma Lo, her purple cheeks crinkling. "My favorite kind of slow."

So Grandma Lo stirred, and stirred, and stirred, while Feng cheered from two steps back and Bao Bao explained that you couldn't rush it, no matter how much it smelled like a vacation. The pineapple slowly went from wet and splashy to thick and glossy and golden, until the spoon left a little trail when it dragged across the bottom.

"That trail," said Bao Bao, "is how you know it's done. Wet and runny means *not yet*. Thick and trail-leaving means *yes, please*." Grandma Lo scooped the amber jam into a dish to cool, and the whole kitchen sighed a warm pineappley sigh.

"While that cools to warm-not-hot," said Bao Bao, "we roll the jam into little balls — but only once it's cool enough for our paws, which is *also* a thing we let a grown-up decide. No burnt fingers in my kitchen. We give gifts; we do not give ourselves owies."

When the jam was cool and snug, the two friends rolled it between their palms into small sticky golden balls — *roll, roll, plop* — a whole shining row of tiny pineapple suns lined up on a tray. Feng made his perfectly round. Then he made one shaped like a star, because he is who he is.

"Now," said Bao Bao, rubbing her doughy paws together, "the **OUTSIDE**. The buttery golden crust. That's the cozy blanket the jam gets wrapped in." She pulled out a big bowl and began to gather things into it like a treasure hunt.

"First, **奶油** (nǎiyóu) = butter," she said, scooping in a soft pale lump, "nice and squishy and room-warm — so it likes being mixed. And a little spoon of sugar, for sweetness." She creamed them together until they went fluffy and pale, like a little cloud that had eaten breakfast.

"Then, **雞蛋** (jīdàn) = egg," she went on, cracking one in — *tap-tap, plop* — and giving it a beat. Feng tried to crack one too and got most of it in the bowl and a fascinating amount on his own head. "A confetti hat!" he declared. Bao Bao laughed so hard she had to hold the bowl.

"And last," she said, "the **麵粉** (miànfěn) = flour. This is the part that turns our cloud into dough." She sifted it in, soft and snowy, and folded it all together — gently, gently, no rushing — until it came together into a smooth, pale-gold, pillowy dough that smelled like the inside of the best hug in the world.

"Good things rise with time," she said, patting it. "We let the dough rest a moment, soft and sleepy, while we get our molds ready."

The molds were little square frames, each stamped with a pretty pattern — flowers and swirls and tiny lucky words — so that every cake would come out looking like a present even before you wrapped it.

"Now the wrapping," said Bao Bao. "Watch. This is my favorite part." She pinched off a piece of dough and flattened it into a soft little round in her palm. Then she set one golden jam ball right in the middle. "And we fold the dough up and *over* the jam — all the way around — until the sweet heart is completely tucked inside. Pinch it shut. Now it's a little stuffed pillow, and nobody can even see the treasure."

"Like a secret!" gasped Feng. "Like a present that's already wrapped on the inside!"

"*Exactly* like that," said Bao Bao warmly.

Feng tried his own. He flattened the dough. He plopped in the jam. He folded the edges up — and one corner of jam peeked cheekily out the top like it was waving hello. "Mine leaked," he said, his crust drooping.

"Mine leaked my first hundred times," said Bao Bao. "That's not a mistake, Feng — that's just a cake that's excited to share itself early. Here." She gently pinched the gap closed for him and smoothed it round. "Soft dough always gives you a second chance. Patch it up, and nobody will ever know."

Then came the *stamp*. Bao Bao tucked each wrapped pillow snug into a square mold and pressed it down — *squidge* — so it filled the frame and took the pretty pattern. Out popped a perfect little square cake, all neat corners and stamped swirls. Feng pressed his. Out came a slightly lopsided square with a wonderfully crisp pattern that read, faintly, *good fortune*.

"Now they bake," said Bao Bao, "in a hot, hot oven, until the crust turns the color of late-afternoon sun. And the oven, my friend—"

"—is a GROWN-UP JOB," sang Feng, already two steps back, hands behind his crumbly back, the very picture of a cake who had learned his lesson.

"Look at you," said Bao Bao, proud as a risen loaf. Grandma Lo slid the tray into the warm oven, and the friends waited, and the whole kitchen filled with the smell of butter and sunshine and pineapple, the smell that means *something good is coming*.

When the cakes came out — golden as treasure, patterns crisp, corners proud — Grandma Lo set them on a rack. "Cool first," she said. "Always cool. A warm gift wrapped too soon makes a soggy box. Patience, little square one."

So they cooled. And while they cooled, Feng and Bao Bao folded the gift boxes, tucked a cake snug into each one, and tied them up with thin red ribbon, until the table was crowded with little golden presents, each one a buttery hug waiting to be carried somewhere.

"There," said Feng softly, looking at the rows and rows of boxes. "Enough for the whole night market. The noodle stall. The lantern lady. Grandma Lo's sister three streets over. Everyone."

"And one," said Bao Bao, sliding a single ribboned box across the table to him, "to keep. Because the one who shares is *also* allowed a treat. That's the rule."

So under the warm kitchen lamp, surrounded by a city of little golden gift boxes, the two friends opened that one box together and split the cake right down the middle. The crust crumbled buttery-soft. The pineapple heart glowed amber-sweet. And it tasted — every crumb of it — like the very best part of being a gift: the giving *and* the sharing, both at once.

"A box is just a hug you can carry," said Feng, with crumbs on his happy chin.

"Then we made a whole city of hugs tonight," said Bao Bao, and she pulled her little square friend into a soft, doughy, pillowy squeeze.

## How to Make pineapple cake 🍽️
*Ask a grown-up to help with anything hot or sharp!*
1. A grown-up simmers chopped pineapple with a little sugar in a hot pot until it's thick, sticky, and golden, then lets it cool. Once it's cool enough, roll the jam into small balls.
2. Cream together soft butter and sugar, beat in an egg, then mix in flour to make a smooth dough; let it rest.
3. Flatten a piece of dough in your palm, set a jam ball in the middle, and fold the dough all the way around to seal the jam inside.
4. Press each wrapped ball into a square mold to shape and stamp it. A grown-up bakes them until golden, then lets them cool — wrap them up and give them away!
