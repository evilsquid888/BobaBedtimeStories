# Story 18: Cao and the Jelly Garden

**Main Character:** Cao (grass jelly)
**Guest Characters:** Yeguo
**Setting:** A herb garden
**Theme:** The quiet refreshes
**Word Spotlight:** 仙草 (xiancao) = grass jelly

---

In the coolest, shadiest corner of the whole night market, where the lanterns glowed soft and low, lived the wobbliest dark cube you ever did meet. His name was Cao, and he was made of **仙草** (xiāncǎo) = grass jelly — a smooth, jiggly, midnight-dark little fellow who shimmered like a pond at twilight and smelled faintly of cool green leaves. When he walked, he went *wibble, wobble, wibble*, and when he stood still, he was so dark and shiny you could almost see your own sleepy face in him.

"Aaaah," Cao sighed happily, stretching out in the shade. "This is my favorite kind of busy. The kind that's mostly sitting."

That was when a bright bouncy voice came rolling through the herb garden gate. "Cao! Cao! Are you back here being quiet again?"

It was Yeguo — a round white coconut jelly, pale and dewy and squishy as a moonbeam, who jiggled with so much excitement she practically fizzed. "Teach me!" she begged. "Teach me to make the cool dark wobble! I want to be *refreshing* too!"

"Then you've come to the perfect place," said Cao, smiling slow. "Tonight, little coconut, we make jelly out of a garden."

---

"The wonderful thing about grass jelly," said Cao, leading Yeguo down a path between leafy green rows, "is that it begins as a *plant*. Not a powder. Not a potion. A real, true, growing-in-the-dirt herb." He stopped beside a tall, scraggly bunch of dried leaves and stems, dusky-green and rustly. "This is **仙草** the plant — xiancao, the 'immortal herb.' Smell it."

Yeguo leaned in and sniffed. "It smells like... a forest after rain. Like the inside of a quiet."

"Exactly like that," said Cao, very pleased. "And the whole trick of making jelly is teaching that quiet how to wobble."

Yeguo bounced. "How? HOW?"

"First," said Cao, "we make the garden into a soup." He gathered an armful of the dried herb and carried it to a big pot near the garden's little cooking corner. "We're going to simmer these leaves in water for a long, long, *long* time — until the water turns dark as a night sky and tastes like the whole forest steeped into a cup."

"How long is long?" asked Yeguo.

"Longer than a song. Longer than two songs. Long enough that the herb has nothing left to give." Cao's eyes twinkled. "Which means it's the perfect job for someone tall, patient, and not in a hurry."

Just then a long shadow stretched across the garden. "Did someone say *not in a hurry*?" drawled a slow, sun-warmed voice. It was Gan Zhe, the sugarcane-juice friend, tall and green-gold with leaf hair flopping over one eye, ambling in like a lazy afternoon. "That's me. I'm so unhurried I once took a whole week to finish a yawn."

"Gan Zhe!" cried Cao. "You're the grown-up we need for the hot bit."

"The hot bit is *mine*," agreed Gan Zhe, "because a bubbling pot is for tall grown-up hands, not little wobbly ones. **小心** (xiǎoxīn) = be careful, you two — you stay back from the heat and let me do the simmering." He filled the pot with water, tucked the dark herb in, and set it over the flame to bubble gently. "Now we wait. Squeeze the day, little jellies. Squeeze the day."

So they waited, and the garden filled with the deep green smell of simmering herb, like a forest having a warm bath. Yeguo could not stop jiggling. "Is it ready? Is it ready? Is it ready *now*?"

"Patience reels in the good stuff," said a new voice — and there at the garden pond sat Xia Xia the prawn, whiskers twitching, fishing line dipped lazily into the water. "I'm just here for the quiet, but I'll lend you a rule, free of charge: the best things never come the second you want them. They come the second they're *ready*. Those are almost never the same second."

"That is annoyingly wise," said Yeguo, and everybody laughed.

At last the water had gone deep and dark and inky, smelling rich as the whole green night. "Now," said Cao, "we strain out the leaves. The garden gave us all its flavor — we thank it and let it go." Gan Zhe poured the steaming dark liquid carefully through a strainer, and all the soggy leaves stayed behind while the smooth dark juice ran through, glossy and clean. "Look at that color," Cao breathed. "Dark as my own tummy. We're nearly there. But right now it's just *tea*. Tea doesn't wobble. We have to give it the wobble."

"How do we give it the wobble?!" Yeguo nearly burst.

"With a little magic powder called cornstarch," said Cao, holding up a small white scoop. "But here's the secret of secrets: you can *never*, ever, *ever* dump dry powder straight into hot liquid."

"Why not?" gasped Yeguo.

"Because it gets shy," said Cao solemnly. "Dry powder hits hot juice and clumps up into lumpy little hiding balls, and then your beautiful jelly is full of pebbles. So instead, we make a **太白粉水** (tàibáifěn shuǐ) = cornstarch-water — a smooth, milky slurry." He spooned the cornstarch into a bowl of cool water and stirred until it went silky-white and pourable, with no lumps at all. "See? Cool water first. *Then* it can join the warm pot without getting nervous."

"Slurry," Yeguo whispered, delighted. "Best word in the whole garden."

"Now the big moment," said Cao. "Gan Zhe — back to a gentle simmer, please." The dark herb-tea began to steam again, and Cao called up, "Pour the slurry in slow, and stir, stir, stir, the whole time!" Gan Zhe trickled the milky cornstarch-water into the dark pot in a thin stream while stirring round and round, and Cao narrated like a sports announcer: "And it's thickening — it's getting glossy — it's going from *runny tea* to *silky pudding-soup* — oh, this is the good part, Yeguo, watch closely!"

Sure enough, the dark liquid grew thicker and glossier with every stir, coating the spoon in a smooth shiny ribbon. "When it's thick enough to coat the spoon and barely drip," said Cao, "it's done. Then we let it simmer one minute more so the cornstarch loses its raw taste, and then —" he clasped his hands — "we take it off the heat."

Gan Zhe lifted the pot away from the flame. The dark, glossy jelly-soup steamed and gleamed.

"Now comes the hardest part of all," said Cao.

"More waiting?" groaned Yeguo.

"More waiting," Cao agreed gently. "We pour it into a flat dish and let it *cool*. As it cools, it sets — it goes from soup, to soft, to a wibble-wobble block all on its own. No more stirring. No more heat. Just stillness doing the quiet, patient work that nobody can rush." He helped pour the dark liquid into a wide pan, where it lay smooth and shining like a little midnight lake. "This is the part the whole garden is best at," he said. "Being still. Being cool. Letting the quiet do the magic."

So they sat in the shady garden while it set. Deng Deng the paper lantern drifted over and hung herself from a branch, glowing soft and gold. "A little light glows warmest," she said shyly, casting a cozy ring of warm over the cool dark dish. Xia Xia kept fishing. Gan Zhe yawned his famous week-long yawn. And Yeguo — who had never in her bouncy life sat still for so long — discovered something surprising. "Cao," she whispered. "The quiet. It's... nice. It's like a cool drink for the *inside* of me."

"That," said Cao, smiling his slow dark smile, "is the whole secret of grass jelly. The quiet refreshes."

And then, at last, Cao pressed one fingertip to the surface of the dish — *boing* — and it bounced right back. "It's set," he breathed. "It wobbles."

"This part," said Cao, "needs the grown-up one more time, because cutting is sharp work." Gan Zhe took a butter knife and sliced the dark jelly into neat little cubes, *snick snick snick*, each one shiny and trembling. Then Cao got to do the fun part: he tumbled the cool dark cubes into bowls — *plop plop plop* — and poured cold creamy milk over the top, and tucked in a spoonful of round black boba pearls for good measure. The dark jelly bobbed and bounced in the pale milk like little night-time stones in a moon-pond.

"Cool, dark, sweet, and wobbly," said Cao. "Eat."

Yeguo took her first cold spoonful — soft jelly, cool milk, chewy boba — and her whole squishy body went *shiver-jiggle* with joy. "Oh," she sighed. "OH. It's so *cool*. It's so *quiet-tasting*. Cao, I'm refreshed all the way down to my coconut!"

Everyone got a bowl. Deng Deng glowed. Gan Zhe finally finished his yawn. Xia Xia caught nothing but smiled anyway. And the whole shady garden sat together in the soft lantern light, spoons clinking, jelly wibbling, the night cool and green and calm all around them.

"You know the secret now," Cao whispered to Yeguo, snuggling close in the dark.

"Simmer the garden," she yawned. "Then... let the quiet do the rest."

And the herb garden went cool, and still, and softly glowing.

## How to Make grass jelly 🍽️
*Ask a grown-up to help with anything hot or sharp!*
1. Have a GROWN-UP simmer the dried grass jelly herb in water for a long time, until the water turns dark and tastes of the herb.
2. Have the grown-up strain out the leaves, keeping the smooth dark liquid.
3. Stir cornstarch into a little cool water to make a smooth slurry (never dump dry powder into hot liquid — it gets lumpy!).
4. Have the grown-up pour the slurry into the simmering liquid while stirring constantly, until it thickens and coats the spoon, then simmer one minute more.
5. Pour it into a flat dish and let it cool until it sets into a wobbly block.
6. Have a grown-up cut it into cubes, then serve cold with milk or boba!

You only have to be cool, and quiet, and refreshed all the way down to your sleepy toes. Let the stillness do its gentle magic. Sweet dreams, little wobble. Goodnight.
