# Story 9: Tako and Yaki and the Flip Game

**Main Character:** Tako and Yaki (takoyaki)
**Guest Characters:** Tai Taiyaki
**Setting:** A festival stall
**Theme:** Patience makes it round
**Word Spotlight:** **takoyaki** (tah-koh-yah-kee) — octopus balls

---

At the busy end of the festival, where the lanterns bobbed like sleepy fireflies, there lived two round little friends who shared one job and one name. They were Tako and Yaki, and together they were **takoyaki** (tah-koh-yah-kee) — octopus balls — two golden-brown spheres, freckled with crispy bits, steaming and dribbly and warm as a hug. Tako had a tiny curl of octopus winking out of his middle. Yaki had a single brave fleck of scallion stuck to his cheek like a beauty mark.

"We," announced Yaki, wobbling proudly on his little wooden boat-tray, "are the roundest snacks at the whole festival."

"We are *also*," whispered Tako, "the dizziest. Somebody flipped us three hundred times this morning and now I see two moons."

A bell jingled. A new friend was rolling up to their stall — and Tako and Yaki had absolutely no idea their whole evening was about to get gloriously, deliciously tricky.

---

The newcomer was a fish. Sort of. He was shaped like a fish but flat as a postcard, golden-crusted, and warm-smelling, with a swirl of sweet red-bean peeking from his tail.

"I'm Tai Taiyaki!" he said, doing a little fishy bow. "I'm a sweet fish-shaped cake. And I have a *terrible* problem. Everyone at this festival can make their own snack — except me. I tried to make takoyaki once and ended up with octopus *soup*."

Tako gasped. "Soup? Round things are not supposed to be soup!"

"That," said Yaki kindly, "is because making takoyaki is a flip game. The trickiest flip game at the festival. But don't worry, Tai — we'll teach you. By the time the lanterns burn low, you'll be making perfect golden balls."

Tai's whole crust glowed. "Truly? You'd teach me?"

"Truly," said Tako. "But first — we need a grown-up. Because takoyaki involves a *very* hot pan, and a hot pan is a job for big hands, not little fishy fins."

Right on cue, the stall's grown-up cook ambled over, wiping her hands on her apron. "Did somebody say takoyaki lesson?" she beamed. "I'll handle the hot bits. You three handle the fun bits. Deal?"

"DEAL!" all three shouted, so loudly a passing lantern jumped.

---

"Step one," said Yaki, pointing his little scallion-fleck like a teacher's pointer, "is the **mazeru** (mah-zeh-roo) — the mix. We make the batter. Tai, grab that big bowl."

Tai hugged the bowl with both fins. Inside went flour, a splash of egg, and a pour of dashi broth that smelled like the sea on a sunny day.

"Now *whisk*," said Tako. "Round and round! The batter wants to be smooth and runny, like a sleepy little river — no lumps allowed."

Tai whisked. He whisked enthusiastically. He whisked so enthusiastically that a wave of batter leaped out of the bowl and landed *splat* on his own nose.

"I've made a hat!" he announced.

"You've made a *mess*," giggled Yaki, "but the batter is perfect. See how it ribbons off the whisk? Smooth as a lullaby. That means it's ready."

"How do I know when it's smooth enough?" asked Tai, peering nervously into the bowl. "What if there are secret lumps hiding at the bottom, plotting against me?"

"Lift the whisk up high," said Tako, "and let the batter pour back down. If it falls in a long silky ribbon — *zzzzip* — like a tiny golden waterfall, it's ready. If it goes *plop-plop-plop* in lumpy blobs, keep whisking."

Tai lifted the whisk. The batter fell in a long, glossy ribbon, smooth as a sleepy river. "Zzzzip!" he whispered, delighted, lifting it three more times just to watch.

"That's the one," said Tako. He leaned in to inspect. "Smooth as a lullaby. Or smooth as Tai's very silly batter-nose right now."

Tai wiggled his batter-hat proudly. "Round one of the flip game: complete."

---

"Now for the **takoyakiki** (tah-koh-yah-kee-kee) — the takoyaki pan," said the grown-up cook, setting down a black metal pan covered in little round dimples, like a tray full of tiny golden moons-to-be. "This part is mine, because it's about to get *very* hot. Watch with your eyes, not your fins."

She brushed each little dimple with oil until they all shone. Then — *tssssss!* — she poured the runny batter in, filling every dimple right to the brim, so it sloshed over the edges into one big bubbly sheet.

"Wait — it's all connected!" said Tai, alarmed. "How does *that* become balls?"

"Patience, little fish," said Tako. "First, the treasure goes in."

The grown-up dropped a tender chunk of cooked octopus into each dimple — *plip, plip, plip* — and then sprinkled green scallion and crunchy little bits over the whole sizzling sheet.

"Octopus in every house," said Yaki dreamily. "A scallion on every roof. This is the part where I always get hungry for *myself*, which is a very strange feeling, let me tell you."

Tai stared at the bubbling pan, completely lost. "It still looks like soup with lumps. I'm going to make octopus soup AGAIN."

"You're not," said Tako, "because here comes the magic. Here comes the actual flip game. Grown-up — may we have the skewers?"

---

The grown-up handed each of them a thin wooden skewer (and gave Tai's a careful look). "Pointy end stays *away* from noses," she said. "We poke the batter, not our friends."

"Understood," said Tai, very seriously, pointing his skewer directly at his own nose, then quickly turning it the right way. "Understood NOW."

"Watch me," said Yaki. He slid the tip of his skewer around the edge of one dimple, cutting the connecting batter loose. Then — with a quick, gentle *turn* — he flipped the half-cooked blob a quarter spin, tucking the runny top down into the dimple so it became the new bottom.

The flat little puddle rolled over... and became *rounder*.

"OHHHH," breathed Tai.

"You don't flip it all at once," explained Tako. "You turn each one a little, then a little more, again and again, round and round, until the runny insides cook and every single ball becomes perfectly round. It's not a flip — it's a *hundred* tiny flips. That's why it's called the flip game."

Tai picked up his skewer. He poked. He turned. His first ball flipped over — *lopsided*, with a tail of batter trailing behind it like a comet.

"It looks like ME," he said proudly. "It has a fish tail!"

"Turn it again," coached Yaki gently. "Tuck the tail in. There you go — round and round — patience makes it round."

"But what if I turn it too fast?" fretted Tai, his skewer hovering.

"You can't rush a ball into being round," said Tako wisely. "If you flip too soon, the runny middle splooshes out and you get — "

"Octopus soup," whispered Tai with great horror.

"*Octopus soup,*" Tako agreed. "So we go slow. A little turn. Let it cook. A little turn. Let it cook. Each spin tucks more of the wet batter against the hot pan, and *that's* the bit that puffs up round and crisp."

Tai turned it again. And again. The comet-tail tucked itself away. The lopsided lump plumped up. And then, suddenly, sitting in the dimple was a perfect, golden, glistening *sphere*.

Tai Taiyaki, the flat little fish who had never once in his life made anything round, had made a ball.

He was so happy he flipped *himself* over in a circle, and the grown-up cook laughed so hard she had to sit down on an upturned bucket.

---

Round and round the three of them went, turning and tucking, turning and tucking, until the whole pan was full of glossy golden takoyaki, each one steaming and round and crackly-crisp on the outside, molten and soft within.

"Last step," said the grown-up, lifting them carefully onto a little boat-tray. "And this one's safe for fins. Top them, then eat them *hot* — that's the whole point of takoyaki."

Tai drizzled brown, glossy takoyaki sauce over the top in happy little zigzags. Yaki added a swish of creamy mayo. Tako sprinkled feathery dancing flakes called **katsuobushi** (kah-tsoo-oh-BOO-shee) — bonito flakes — that wiggled in the steam like they were alive.

"They're WAVING at me," gasped Tai.

"They always do," said Tako warmly. "That means they're happy. That means they're done."

---

The four of them sat on the upturned buckets behind the stall, lanterns glowing gold above, and shared the whole boat-tray of takoyaki between them — blowing on each one first, because hot is hot, and a burnt tongue is no fun at any festival.

"Careful — let it cool a tick," said the grown-up. "Inside a takoyaki is hotter than the outside lets on. That's the last little trick: they keep a warm secret in the middle."

Tai blew on his patiently. Then he bit into a takoyaki he had made *all by himself*. The crispy outside crackled. The soft inside steamed. The little chunk of octopus said hello.

"It's round," he whispered, eyes shining. "I made something round. And it tastes like the *sea* and *home* and *winning a game* all at once."

"That," said Yaki softly, scallion-fleck glowing, "is exactly what the flip game tastes like when you win it."

And outside, the festival twinkled on, full of round and happy things.

## How to Make takoyaki 🍽️
*Ask a grown-up to help with anything hot or sharp!*
1. Whisk flour, egg, and dashi broth into a smooth, runny batter with no lumps.
2. Have a grown-up brush oil into the dimpled takoyaki pan and pour the batter in to fill every dimple.
3. Drop a piece of cooked octopus into each dimple, then sprinkle scallion (and crunchy bits) over the top.
4. Using a skewer, turn each ball a little at a time, round and round, until they cook into perfect golden spheres.
5. Top with takoyaki sauce, a swish of mayo, and dancing katsuobushi (bonito flakes) — then blow on each one and let it cool a moment, because the inside stays extra hot!
