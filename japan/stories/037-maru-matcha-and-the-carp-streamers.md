# Story 37: Maru Matcha and the Carp Streamers

**Main Character:** Maru Matcha
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A spring sky filled with koinobori carp-shaped windsocks
**Theme:** Swim bravely against the wind and you'll grow strong
**Japanese Spotlight:** koinobori (koy-noh-BOH-ree) — carp streamers

---

On the breeziest, bluest morning of spring, Maru Matcha stood at the top of Whisk Hill and watched the whole sky go fishing.

Not for fish, mind you. For wind.

All across the valley, families had tied great fabric fish to tall bamboo poles, and now those fish gulped the breeze and went swimming — right there in the air, with nothing underneath them but cozy spring sunshine and the smell of warm dumplings drifting up from town. Red ones. Blue ones. One enormous black one as round as a happy belly. They wiggled and flapped and flipped their tails, swimming up the wind like the whole sky had turned into a river and forgotten to tell anyone.

Maru Matcha — a small jade-green friend with a soft swirl of matcha-cream hair and calm half-moon eyes — twirled her tiny bamboo whisk and smiled her quiet smile.

"**koinobori** (koy-noh-BOH-ree) — carp streamers," she murmured, tasting the word like a sip of warm tea. "Brave little fish that swim on the wind." She breathed in slow. "Whisk slow. Feel calm."

That was when she heard the smallest, wobbliest sniffle in all of spring.

---

It came from behind a clover patch at the bottom of the hill. Maru Matcha padded down — calm as ever, because Maru Matcha never hurried, not even toward an emergency — and found a tiny dumpling friend hugging a folded-up carp streamer to its chest like it was the most precious and the most terrifying thing in the entire world.

The little dumpling was no bigger than a teacup, soft and pale, with worried eyes and a bamboo pole far, far too tall lying in the grass beside it.

"Oh, hello there," said Maru Matcha gently. "That's a very fine carp you've got."

"It's m-mine," the little one whispered. "Grandmother gave it to me. She said I'm big enough now to fly my own koinobori this spring." The dumpling's lip trembled. "But I'm not. Look how tiny I am. And look how BIG the wind is."

Right on cue, a gust came whooshing down the hill, and the little dumpling squeaked and ducked, and the folded carp flapped open just enough to show a flash of brilliant red.

"My name's Mame-bo," the dumpling added in a tiny voice. "And I'm too scared to do it. The wind will push my fish around. It'll get tossed and tumbled and it'll never swim properly, and everyone will see, and — and —" Mame-bo hid behind the carp entirely. "Maybe I'll just keep him folded up safe. Folded-up fish can't fail."

Maru Matcha sat down in the clover. She did not say *don't be silly*. She did not say *hurry up*. She simply set down her little whisk, looked up at all the brave fish dancing in the sky, and said the calmest, coziest thing.

"Folded-up fish can't swim, either."

Mame-bo went very still. That was a fact the little dumpling had not considered.

For a long, soft moment they watched the sky together. A round blue carp the size of a rowboat swam by overhead. Below it, a smaller green one — clearly a beginner, all wobbles and flops — kept catching the breeze sideways and spinning in dizzy circles.

"See that little green one?" Maru Matcha pointed with her whisk. "Flopping about like a noodle in soup?"

"He looks silly," Mame-bo admitted.

"He looks *brave*," Maru Matcha corrected, ever so gently. "Yesterday he was probably folded up in a drawer, frightened of this exact wind. Today he's up there spinning circles in the whole wide sky." Her half-moon eyes crinkled. "Silly is just brave with the training wheels still on. Everybody swims wobbly first. Even me."

Mame-bo peeked up at her. "*You* were wobbly?"

"Oh, terribly." Maru Matcha gave her swirl a modest little wiggle. "The first time I whisked matcha, I made such a mess I had green foam on the ceiling. Took three days to find it all." She sighed contentedly. "Everything good starts a little messy, little one."

---

Mame-bo peeked out. "But what if the wind is too strong?"

"Hmm." Maru Matcha tilted her swirl thoughtfully. "Let me tell you a small secret about carp." She leaned in, half-moon eyes twinkling. "A real carp — a living one, swimming in a real river — does not float along easy. A carp swims *against* the current. It pushes up waterfalls. It works and wiggles and never gives up, and *that* is exactly why we celebrate it." She nodded slowly. "The koinobori doesn't fly because the wind is gentle, little one. It flies because it's brave enough to face the wind that isn't."

Mame-bo blinked. "So... the wind isn't the problem?"

"The wind," said Maru Matcha, with the tiniest deadpan smile, "is the whole point. Without it, your carp just takes a very long nap on a stick."

Mame-bo giggled — one small, surprised giggle that popped out before the worry could stop it. And a giggle, as everyone knows, is the first crack in a wall of fear.

"Come," said Maru Matcha, rising. "Let's raise your fish together. Slowly. The right way." She picked up one end of the enormous bamboo pole. "Whisk slow. Feel calm. We do not rush a carp."

---

It turned out that raising a koinobori is harder than it looks, especially when one of you is a teacup-sized dumpling and the other is a small bowl-headed friend whose hands are mostly made of matcha cream.

First they tried to lift the pole standing up, and it tipped, and Maru Matcha calmly walked three steps sideways to stay under it, and three steps the *other* way, and three steps back, until she was basically doing a slow, dignified little dance.

"This is the calmest panic I've ever seen," said Mame-bo, watching.

"It is not panic," said Maru Matcha serenely, jogging backward under the wobbling pole. "It is... enthusiastic balance."

Then they tried to tie the carp to the top, but the breeze kept gulping the fabric and filling it with air, so the red carp puffed up fat and round and bonked Maru Matcha softly on the head — *boomf* — like the world's gentlest pillow fight.

"He's eager," Maru Matcha observed, muffled inside the fish.

"He likes you!" said Mame-bo, and laughed properly now, both little dumpling-hands over its mouth.

A second gust came, and this time the carp swallowed so much air that it lifted Maru Matcha clean off her feet — just an inch, just for a heartbeat — and set her down again with a soft little *plop* in the clover, swirl slightly tilted.

"And now I have flown," she announced calmly, straightening her cream. "Briefly. As a hat."

Mame-bo laughed so hard the little dumpling tipped over sideways into the grass, which set them both giggling again, and somewhere in the middle of all that laughing the fear simply forgot to come back. That is the wonderful trick of laughter: it is very hard to be terribly afraid and terribly giggly at the very same time. Maru Matcha knew this. It was, perhaps, her cleverest cozy secret of all.

At last they got the carp tied on snug, its mouth open wide and round and ready, its long red body trailing down the pole. Now came the real part. The brave part.

"We lift him into the wind," said Maru Matcha. "Together. On three. And Mame-bo —" she caught the little one's worried eye — "he might wobble. He might dip. He might flap and flop and look like he's tumbling. That isn't failing. That is a carp *learning the river*. We don't pull him down when he wobbles. We hold steady and let him find his swim. Yes?"

Mame-bo took the deepest breath a teacup-sized dumpling has ever taken. "Yes."

"One. Two..." Maru Matcha's half-moon eyes crinkled. "**Ganbatte** (gahn-BAH-teh) — do your best, little carp." She winked. "Three!"

---

Up went the pole.

The red carp caught the spring wind with a great soft *FWUMP*, and — oh! — for one heart-skip it bucked and twisted and dove sideways, exactly the way Mame-bo had feared, and the little dumpling gasped and almost let go —

"Steady," said Maru Matcha, calm as a still pond. "Let him fight it. That's how he gets strong."

So Mame-bo held on. Held *steady*. And the most wonderful thing happened.

The carp stopped fighting the wind — and started *riding* it.

His round mouth gulped a great breath of breeze and his red body filled out long and proud and rippling, and his tail gave a flick, and then another, and then he was swimming. Truly swimming. Up and up the river of sky, shoulder to shoulder with all the other brave fish of spring, flipping and flashing in the sunshine like a wish that had finally, *finally* gotten permission to take flight.

Up above, the other carp seemed to notice the newcomer. The big blue rowboat-fish dipped a friendly tail. The wobbly green beginner spun an excited circle, as if to say *welcome, welcome, the river's lovely up here!* And the enormous round black carp — the one as big as a happy belly — turned slowly to make room, the way a kind grown-up makes room at a crowded table so the smallest guest can squeeze in.

Mame-bo stared up with shining eyes, both hands gripping the pole, cheeks pink with pride. "He's swimming," the little one whispered. "He's *really* swimming. And I'm — I'm flying him. Me. The tiny one."

"You are," said Maru Matcha softly. "And do you see? The strongest wind made the most beautiful swim." She gave her tiny whisk a gentle twirl. "The things that push against us aren't always trying to push us *down*, little friend. Sometimes the wind is just teaching us how to fly."

A breeze ruffled them both, sweet and warm with the smell of spring. High above, the red carp turned a slow, happy loop, as if waving down at the tiny dumpling who had been brave enough to let it go.

Mame-bo leaned its soft little head against Maru Matcha's arm. "Will you stay and watch him with me? Just a while?"

"Of course." Maru Matcha settled into the clover, the warm pole resting easy in her hands, the whole bright sky full of swimming fish above them. "We watch as long as you like. There is no rushing a carp." A pause, a tiny twinkle. "And no rushing a friend, either."

---

So they sat together on Whisk Hill all afternoon, the calm matcha friend and the no-longer-quite-so-scared little dumpling, watching the koinobori swim the spring wind until the sky turned soft and gold. And every time the wind blew hard, Mame-bo didn't flinch anymore. Mame-bo just smiled, and held steady, and watched the little red carp grow braver and stronger and more beautiful — swimming up, and up, and up.

When at last the sun grew sleepy and dipped low behind the hills, Maru Matcha helped Mame-bo lower the carp down ever so gently, folding him with great care for the night.

"He swam so well," Mame-bo said softly, hugging the folded fish — though this time the dumpling wasn't hiding behind it. This time it was a proud, happy hug. "Will he be brave again tomorrow?"

"He will," said Maru Matcha. "And so will you. That's the lovely thing about bravery, little one — you don't have to keep all of it ready at once. You only need a tiny sip, just enough for the next gust." She tucked her whisk away and gave the dumpling's soft head a gentle pat. "A little courage each morning. Whisk slow. Feel calm. And up your carp goes."

Mame-bo beamed. Then, very quietly, the little dumpling reached up and gave Maru Matcha the smallest, warmest hug a teacup-sized friend can give. "Thank you for not hurrying me," Mame-bo whispered.

"There is no rushing a carp," said Maru Matcha, smiling into the golden evening. "And no rushing the bravest little fish I've ever met."

## Goodnight Blessing

You are braver than you know, and you are loved more than the wind is wide. Goodnight.
