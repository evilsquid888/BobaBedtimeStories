# Story 79: Neko-Pan and the Lucky Beckoning Spirits

**Main Character:** Neko-Pan (Maneki Melon Cream)
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A tiny roadside shrine guarded by cheerful good-luck cat spirits
**Theme:** Look for the why before you blame — and mend the crack instead of pouring forever
**Japanese Spotlight:** **nazo** (NAH-zoh) — a mystery

---

On a sleepy bend in the road, just past the persimmon tree and exactly one hop from the puddle that always looked like a pancake, stood a shrine no taller than a teapot.

And that morning, the teapot was in an uproar.

"GONE!" wailed a tiny voice from inside. "Again! That's six mornings in a ROW!"

Neko-Pan, who happened to be bouncing past with one paw raised in his usual hello-to-everything wave, skidded to a stop so fast his gold-foil collar bell went *ti-i-i-ing* like a startled bicycle. He was a cream-filled bun shaped like a **maneki-neko** (mah-neh-kee NEH-koh) — the beckoning lucky cat — with a crackly golden dome and a custard heart that simply could not walk past a wail.

He peeked inside the shrine. On a moss cushion sat three small, pearly, see-through cat-spirits — Maneki, who was the size of a plum; Koneki, her son, the size of a grape; and Teeny, her daughter, the size of a worried raisin. All three were staring into an old clay jar.

The jar was empty.

"Every evening," Maneki explained, whiskers trembling, "we fill this jar with the day's luck — every sparkle of thanks from every traveler we wave at. We store it overnight, and in the morning we wave it back out to whoever needs it. Except for six mornings straight we've woken up to *nothing*. Someone," she said darkly, "is taking our luck in the night."

"It's the moth," said Koneki.

"It's the moon," whispered Teeny. "It's been looking very smug lately."

Neko-Pan's eyes went wide and sparkly. "Friends," he breathed, "what you have here is a **nazo** (NAH-zoh) — a mystery. And lucky for you, I have always, *always* wanted to be a **tantei** (tahn-TAY) — a detective."

"Have you ever actually been one?" asked Koneki.

"I have read two and a half detective comics," said Neko-Pan, with enormous dignity, "which is practically a badge."

---

The investigation began at once.

Neko-Pan circled the jar the way detectives circle things in comics — slowly, humming, squinting at it from angles that made absolutely no difference. Then he found his first real clue: a faint trail of golden glitter-dust, thin as a whisper, leading from the jar to the shrine doorway. Beside it, pressed into the moss, marched a line of tiny round footprints, each one the size of an apple seed.

"A-ha!" he announced. "Our luck-taker has feet!"

"Most takers do," said Koneki.

"Yes, but now we know it's not the moon," said Neko-Pan. "The moon famously has no feet. That's one suspect cleared already. We're very good at this."

He interviewed the moth, who turned out to have an airtight alibi: she had spent the entire night headbutting the lantern down the road, and the lantern, when consulted, wearily confirmed it. He interviewed the fat old toad who slept in the pancake-puddle, but the toad's footprints were the size of plums, not apple seeds, and besides, the toad snored so loudly that he was his own alibi for a mile in every direction. Neko-Pan even attempted to interview the wind, which declined to comment. He wrote that down as *suspicious, but typical*.

Then he noticed something odd. He sat back on his bun-bottom and frowned at his notes (the notes were scratched in the dirt and mostly drawings of himself in a detective hat, but still).

"These footprints are teeny-tiny," he said slowly. "Whoever made them couldn't carry more than a thimbleful. But the jar loses *everything*. A whole jar of luck doesn't fit in apple-seed feet." His bell gave one thoughtful *ting*. "Something about this **nazo** doesn't add up. Which means..."

The three spirits leaned in.

"...tonight, we stake out the shrine."

"What do we do in a stakeout?" asked Teeny.

"We stay very still, very quiet, and very awake," said Neko-Pan.

There was a pause.

"You fell asleep waving yesterday," Teeny pointed out. "Mid-wave. Standing up."

"That," said Neko-Pan, "was practice for being still."

---

Night settled over the sleepy bend in the road like a quilt being pulled up to its chin. The persimmon tree rustled. The stars came out, unhurried.

And beside the tiny shrine stood a brand-new lucky cat statue.

It was, to be honest, a terrible statue. It was bun-shaped instead of stone-shaped. It smelled of warm custard. Its bell tinkled faintly every time it breathed, and when a firefly drifted past, the statue's raised paw twitched and gave the firefly a tiny, friendly, completely-un-statue-like wave.

"Statues don't wave," hissed Koneki from the moss cushion.

"This one is very welcoming," whispered the statue.

The hours crept by. Neko-Pan's eyelids grew heavy as dumplings. He tried counting stars to stay awake, but counting is famously the sleepiest of all the maths, and by forty-two his head had begun to nod. He snored one small custard snore, woke himself up with his own bell, and stood extra still to make it look on purpose. The three spirits, who were watching, said nothing, which was the kindest thing they could possibly have done.

Then — at the deep, hushed middle of the night — *pitter pitter pitter*.

A shadow no bigger than a teacup tiptoed up the moss. A little gray field mouse, holding a thimble in both paws, crept to the jar, whispering to herself: "Just a pinch. Just a pinch. I'm sorry, I'm sorry, just a pinch."

She dipped the thimble in, scooped a single glowing pinch of luck, bowed to the jar three times — *bob, bob, bob* — and pattered away into the dark.

Neko-Pan nearly bounced up to follow. But then he stopped, because the jar was doing something strange. The mouse had taken one thimbleful — yet the golden glow inside kept sinking, slowly, steadily, all on its own, like bathwater with the plug half-out.

He crept close. He squinted. And there, low on the jar's round belly, hidden under a hundred years of moss-shadow, he found it: a hairline crack, fine as an eyelash. Through it, the night's whole harvest of luck was seeping out in a slow golden thread — down through the cushion, into the soil, into the roots of the earth below.

"Oh," whispered Neko-Pan, his custard heart going soft. "It wasn't a thief at all. Well — it was a *very small* thief and a *very big* crack."

---

At first light, Neko-Pan followed the apple-seed footprints to a snug burrow beneath the persimmon tree. Inside, the little gray mouse — her name was Chiyo — was tucking the glowing thimble under a leaf-blanket, where her small brother Mame lay sneezing the tiniest sneezes in Japan. *Choo. Choo. Choo.* His whiskers drooped like wet noodles.

"He's been poorly for weeks," Chiyo whispered, when she saw Neko-Pan in the doorway, and her ears went pink with shame. "I just wanted a pinch of luck under his pillow each night, to help him get better. I always bowed. I always said sorry. I know it wasn't mine to take."

Neko-Pan looked at the sneezing brother, and the worn-out thimble, and the sister's tired, hopeful face.

"Come with me," he said gently. "There's something the spirits need to hear. And nobody," he added, seeing her flinch, "is going to be cross. I'm the detective, and I officially won't allow it."

At the shrine, when the spirits saw the thimble, Koneki puffed up like a small offended cloud. "THE LUCK THIEF!"

"Before anyone points a paw," said Neko-Pan, stepping in between, "ask the *why*." And Chiyo, in a voice the size of a dewdrop, told them about Mame. By the end, Maneki's see-through eyes were shiny, and Teeny had quietly gone and stood next to Chiyo, holding her paw.

"Besides," said Neko-Pan, "she only ever took a pinch. *This* is the real culprit." He rolled the jar gently into the sunlight and showed them the eyelash-thin crack. "Your luck has been leaking into the ground every single night. That's where it all went." He glanced up at the persimmon tree, which — now that everyone looked — was the greenest, gladdest, most ridiculously leafy tree on the whole road, heavy with fruit in every direction. "And I think the tree says thank you, by the way. It's been drinking luck for a week. No wonder it looks so pleased with itself."

"But what do we do?" cried Maneki. "We can't get a new jar — this jar has held the shrine's luck for a hundred years!"

"We don't need a new jar," said Neko-Pan, and his bell rang out bright. "We mend the old one. And I know exactly how."

He peeled one shining fleck of gold foil from his own collar. He warmed a drop of sweet golden custard straight from his own heart — which is the sort of thing a bun can do, if the reason is good enough. He stirred them together into a glowing golden paste, and then, with the tip of Teeny's softest whisker for a brush, he painted it carefully, slowly, all along the crack.

The paste set like glaze. The crack became a thin, gleaming river of gold running down the jar's belly — sealed, solid, and somehow lovelier than the unbroken clay around it.

"There's a word for this," said Neko-Pan softly. "**Kintsugi** (keen-TSOO-ghee) — the art of mending broken things with gold. You don't hide the crack. You make it the most beautiful part."

---

The next morning, for the first time in a week, the jar was full.

Not just full — *brimming*, glowing like a sunrise in a pot, the golden seam shining proudly down its side. The three spirits danced in a circle on the moss cushion, paws raised, and their waves sent luck sailing up and down the road in long warm ribbons.

The very first ribbon went, by unanimous vote, to the burrow under the persimmon tree. After that, Chiyo didn't have to tiptoe anymore. Each evening she knocked, and each evening Teeny waved a proper, official pinch of luck into her thimble for Mame — whose sneezes grew smaller and smaller until one day they were just hiccups, and then just giggles.

That evening, as the sky turned peach-and-gold, everyone gathered at the tiny shrine: three glowing spirits, two field mice (one giggling), one extremely smug persimmon tree, and a round detective bun whose collar was missing exactly one fleck of gold and who had never felt richer.

"How did you know?" Maneki asked him. "We were so sure someone wicked was stealing."

"That's the thing about a **nazo**," said Neko-Pan, waving goodnight to a passing firefly, who looped politely in reply. "When something good keeps going missing, look for the *why* before you blame the *who*. The why is usually smaller, and sadder, and easier to fix than you think." He patted the jar's golden seam. "And when you find a crack — don't just keep pouring. Mend it. Mend it with something golden, and it'll hold forever."

The stars came out, unhurried. The jar glowed. The bell went *ting*, soft as a wish, and the whole sleepy bend in the road settled down for the night, every drop of its luck finally staying exactly where it belonged.

## Goodnight Blessing
May every puzzle in your day turn out to be smaller and kinder than it first appeared, little one. Tonight, a little jar on a sleepy road is full to the brim, a small mouse is giggling instead of sneezing, and three tiny spirits are waving a slow golden goodnight just for you. Let your eyes grow heavy as dumplings now — the mystery is solved, the luck is safe, and so are you. Goodnight.
