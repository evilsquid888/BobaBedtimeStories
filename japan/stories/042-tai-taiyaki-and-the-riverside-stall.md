# Story 42: Tai Taiyaki and the Riverside Stall

**Main Character:** Tai Taiyaki
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A canal-side taiyaki stall where the little fish cakes are made
**Theme:** Be brave enough to swim toward your dream
**Japanese Spotlight:** **taiyaki** (tie-yah-kee) — fish-shaped cake

---

The very best place in the whole evening was the little **taiyaki** (tie-yah-kee) — fish-shaped cake — stall that leaned right over the edge of the canal, close enough that the water giggled below it. And the very best fish at that stall — though he would never brag, he would only WIGGLE — was Tai Taiyaki.

Tai was a cheerful golden fish-shaped cake, crisp at the fins and warm in the middle, with a red-bean heart so cozy you could see it glow when he smiled wide. And oh, did he smile wide. He smiled while flipping. He smiled while cooling. He even smiled in his sleep, which made his fin-tail twitch.

But tonight Tai pressed his little nose to the stall's edge and watched the canal sparkle and sigh below him, and his smile went thoughtful.

"Someday," he whispered, "I'm going to swim in a REAL river."

The lantern overhead flickered, as if it had heard.

---

Now, you should know something about taiyaki, dear reader. They are baked in clinky iron molds shaped like fish, which means every taiyaki is BORN looking ready to swim — fins, tail, the works — but almost none of them ever touches water. They go straight from the warm mold into a paper sleeve, then into a happy mouth. A short and sweet life, but a very dry one.

Tai found this terribly unfair.

"Look at these fins!" he announced the next morning, wiggling them at the row of fresh taiyaki cooling on the rack. "Look at this tail! Friends, we are SHAPED for adventure. We are fish! And what do fish do?"

"Get eaten with great joy?" offered a small plain taiyaki named Pip, who was new and still a little crumbly at the edges.

"Well — yes. Eventually. Proudly!" said Tai. "But BEFORE that. What do fish do BEFORE that?"

The whole rack thought very hard.

"Sit on a rack?" said Pip.

"They SWIM!" cried Tai, and he flopped so enthusiastically that he nearly rolled off the counter, and had to grab the soy-sauce bottle to steady himself, which squirted a tidy little fish-shaped splat onto the wood. "See? Even my SHADOW wants to swim."

The other taiyaki laughed their warm crinkly laughs. They loved Tai. But they also thought he was a bit of a dreamer, which he was, and that's a wonderful thing to be, as long as you don't dream yourself right off a counter.

"You know," said a round taiyaki named Bun, who was stuffed extra full of red bean and therefore took everything very slowly, "I once met a fish who swam. A real one. Down in the canal."

The whole rack gasped.

"What was it like?" breathed Tai.

"Wet," said Bun, after a long, thoughtful pause. "Very wet. And it had... scales. And it blinked sideways. And do you know what it said to me?"

"WHAT?" cried everyone.

"Nothing," admitted Bun. "Fish don't talk much. But it WIGGLED at me. And I have never forgotten that wiggle." Bun sighed dreamily. "It was a magnificent wiggle."

Tai's eyes shone like two drops of warm syrup. A real fish. A real wiggle. A real river, right there, just past the edge of everything he had ever known.

"Then it's settled," he declared. "If a fish with scales can swim in that canal, then a fish with FINS and a HEART OF RED BEAN can certainly dip a tail."

"The canal is awfully far down," said Pip nervously, peering over the edge. "And awfully cold. And awfully... wet."

"That's rather the point of a swim," said Tai gently. But his fin-tail had stopped wiggling. Because — and here is the secret he hadn't told anyone — every time Tai looked all the way DOWN at the dark water lapping the stones, his warm red-bean heart did a little flip that wasn't quite excitement.

It was the other thing. The thing that lives right next door to excitement and borrows its socks.

It was being scared.

---

That night, when the stall had cooled and the lanterns dimmed to a sleepy amber, Tai made up his mind. Sort of. About forty percent of his mind, which is enough to get a fish to the edge of a counter but not quite over it.

He waddled to the lip of the stall. Below, the canal slid by, black and silver, whispering its watery secrets. A paper lantern drifted along the surface, glowing like a tiny floating moon. It was beautiful. It was also VERY far down.

"Right," said Tai to himself. "Swim toward the sweet stuff. That's my whole motto. So. Swim."

He leaned forward.

His heart did the flip.

He leaned back.

He took a deep breath that smelled of warm batter and night air and the green-river smell of the canal.

"Okay. This time for real."

He leaned forward.

His heart did the flip AND a somersault AND something his heart had never done before, which was knock politely on his ribs and ask whether they couldn't all just go back to bed.

He leaned back.

"This is ridiculous," Tai whispered to nobody. "I am SHAPED like a swim. I have practiced the wiggle. I have an excellent tail. And yet here I sit, dry as a cracker, scared of a little water." He drooped so low his fin-tail nearly folded. "Maybe Pip was right. Maybe some fish are just for racks."

"Hello, swimmer," said a soft voice.

Tai squeaked and nearly toppled the wrong way. Beside him sat the oldest taiyaki on the stall — a great-grandfish named Taisho, golden gone slightly toasty-brown with age, fin a little chipped, eyes warm as two drops of honey. Taisho had been baked so long ago that nobody could remember it, and somehow had never been eaten, which made Taisho the wisest fish Tai knew, and also possibly the only one with a story to finish.

"You want the water," said Taisho. It wasn't a question.

"More than anything," said Tai. "But every time I look down, my heart goes... flippy. Not the good flippy. The other flippy." He drooped. "Maybe I'm not really an adventure fish. Maybe I'm just an adventure-SHAPED fish."

Taisho chuckled, a sound like a kettle settling. "Let me tell you a thing about brave, little one. Brave isn't the fish who feels no fear. That fish has simply never looked down. Brave is the fish who looks down, feels the flip, and dips a fin anyway."

"Dips a fin," repeated Tai.

"Not the whole self. Not at first." Taisho's honey eyes crinkled. "Dreams are like hot tea, Tai. You don't pour the whole pot down your throat. You take a careful sip. Then another. The river will still be a river tomorrow. But tonight you can dip a fin."

Tai looked at the dark, beautiful, terrifying, wonderful water.

"Would you... come with me?" he asked. "Just to the edge?"

"I've been at the edge for sixty years, dear heart," said Taisho warmly. "I'd be honored to share it."

---

So here is how the bravest swim in the history of the riverside stall actually happened — and it is a great deal gentler than you might expect, which is exactly right, because the gentle things are usually the brave ones in disguise.

First, the whole rack of taiyaki woke up, because that is what friends do when one of them is about to do something enormous. Pip rolled to the front, crumbly and worried but TRYING, which is the best kind of friend there is.

"We've made you a ramp," Pip announced.

And they had! The little taiyaki had nudged a long wooden serving paddle out over the edge of the stall, sloping down, down, down to where a flat mossy stone sat just at the waterline — a perfect little fish-sized dock.

"And we tied a noodle to you," added Pip, "so if you get too scared, we pull you back. **Daijoubu** (dye-joh-boo) — it's okay. We've got you."

Tai felt something warm rise in his chest that had nothing to do with red beans. He looked at the ramp. He looked at the water. He looked at all his friends holding the other end of the noodle, and at old Taisho nodding by the rail.

His heart did the flip.

And this time, Tai dipped a fin anyway.

He slid down the paddle — wheeeee — fins out like a swimmer doing the most cautious belly-flop in history, and his crispy tail touched the canal with a sound that went *blip.*

It was cold. It was wet. It tickled.

It was the GREATEST FEELING IN THE WORLD.

A small silver fish — a REAL one, with scales and sideways-blinking eyes — drifted up out of the dark to see what all the *blip*-ing was about. It looked at Tai. Tai looked at it. For one breathless moment, fish met fish across the great divide of being-made-of-cake versus being-made-of-fish.

And then the real fish wiggled.

It was, Tai thought, an absolutely magnificent wiggle.

So Tai wiggled back, with everything he had, fins and tail and glowing heart, and the silver fish gave a flick that might have been a giggle, and slipped away into the dark water, and Tai understood at last exactly what Bun had meant. Some hellos don't need any words at all. They only need a wiggle, and the courage to be in the water to give one.

"It's WATER!" Tai shrieked, delighted. "Real, actual, wet, splashy, river-y WATER and it's tickling my TAIL!" He gave the smallest, most careful little kick, and a tiny ripple ran out across the canal, and the floating paper lantern bobbed as if to say hello, and a real moon and a paper moon and a fish-shaped cake all wobbled together on the dark glassy water.

"**Oyogu** (oh-yoh-goo) — to swim!" cried Taisho from the rail, and the whole stall cheered.

Tai paddled in a small proud circle, no bigger than a teacup, his red-bean heart glowing so warm it sent up the tiniest curl of steam where it met the cool air. He did not swim far. He did not swim long. The noodle stayed snug. But for one shimmering minute, beneath the lanterns, a taiyaki swam in a real river, fins flashing gold, exactly as he was always shaped to do.

"I'm doing it," he whispered. "I'm really, truly doing it."

---

When at last Tai's friends gently reeled him in — because cold water and warm cakes are not long-term friends, and a soggy taiyaki is a sad taiyaki — they wrapped him in a cozy paper sleeve and set him by the warm mold until he toasted right back to crisp.

He steamed happily, dripping and grinning, the proudest fish on the whole canal.

"You didn't swim very far," Pip observed kindly, snuggling beside him to share the warmth.

"No," agreed Tai, glowing. "I dipped a fin. And tomorrow, maybe I'll dip two. That's how you swim toward a dream, Pip. One brave little splash at a time."

Old Taisho smiled in the lantern-light and said nothing, because the best teachers know when a story has finished telling itself.

And do you know — the very next morning, a little girl with two crooked braids bought Tai for her breakfast, and bit into him with a gasp of pure delight, and discovered his warm red-bean heart, and declared him "the BRAVEST tasting taiyaki I've ever had." Which he was. You could taste it. Brave is sweet, it turns out, and just a little bit like the river.

Tai went out the way all the happiest taiyaki do — proudly, gloriously, like a firework that finally got to light the sky — knowing he had done the thing he'd always dreamed of doing.

He had been a fish.

He had touched the water.

And he had been brave enough to swim toward the sweet stuff after all.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little dreamer, your dreams are like Tai's river — wide and shining and waiting patiently for you. You don't have to leap in all at once. You can dip a fin tonight, and another tomorrow, and that is brave enough, more than enough, exactly enough. Curl up warm as a fresh-baked fish, with a heart as cozy and sweet as red bean, and let the gentle water of sleep carry you somewhere wonderful. We've got you, little one — we've always got you. Goodnight.
