# Story 78: Dora Dorayaki and the Lantern-Lighting Spirits

**Main Character:** Dora the Dorayaki
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A summer Obon evening with floating paper lanterns on a gentle river
**Theme:** Remembering someone keeps their warmth glowing
**Japanese Spotlight:** **chochin** (choh-cheen) — paper lantern

---

On the warmest, hushiest evening of summer, Dora the Dorayaki was sitting on the riverbank with his beret tipped just so, reading a story to absolutely nobody.

"And then," he announced, holding up a page to the frogs, "the brave little dumpling discovered the treasure was *friendship*."

The frogs blinked. One burped, which Dora chose to interpret as applause.

"You're too kind," he said, taking a tiny bow.

That was the thing about Dora. He was two fluffy honey-brown pancakes hugging a smile of sweet red-bean paste, soft and round as a sleepy moon, and he simply could not keep a story to himself. Stories were his favorite filling — even better than red bean, and he loved red bean *enormously*.

Tonight was **Obon** (oh-bohn) — the summer evening when warm memories come to visit. The whole town smelled of grilled corn and river-water and something gentle Dora couldn't name. And down by the reeds, very small and very worried, three tiny lights were bobbing in circles like fireflies who'd forgotten the way home.

Dora set down his book. "Now what," he murmured, "could those be?"

He waddled closer, beret bouncing, and gasped a soft red-bean gasp. The lights weren't fireflies at all. They were spirits — but the friendliest, roundest, most *adorable* little spirits you ever saw. Each one was no bigger than a rice grain, glowing a soft warm gold, with tiny cheeks and tiny noses and great big hopeful eyes. They wobbled and shimmered like steam over a warm bath.

"Oh, hello!" said the first, in a voice like a wind chime. "We are the lantern-lighting spirits! Tonight we float down the river. But—" The little spirit drooped. "We've lost our **chochin** (choh-cheen) — our paper lanterns. Without them, we can't find our way home along the water."

Dora's whole round body went warm with worry, which for a dorayaki means going *slightly toastier*. "You've lost your lanterns? On the most important night? That," he declared, "is a problem with a *story-sized* solution. And I happen to keep a great many stories. Come — a good story is the best filling, and the best filling starts with a plan."

The three spirits zoomed happy loops around his beret.

"First," said Dora, holding up one soft flap like a teacher, "we find lanterns. Or we make them. I once read a book about lantern-making. Or possibly it was a book about *dumplings*. I get those mixed up. Either way: let's begin!"

Down the bank they went, Dora leading, three golden glows trailing behind him like the tail of a very cozy comet.

The first thing they found was an old paper lantern snagged in the reeds, soggy and sad and folded shut like a closed fan.

"Aha!" cried Dora. "A chochin in distress!"

He tugged. He pulled. He braced his little pancake feet and *heaved* — and the lantern came loose all at once, sending Dora rolling backward down the bank like a runaway rice ball. He landed in the soft grass with a gentle *fwump*.

"That," he announced from upside down, beret over one eye, "was on purpose."

The spirits giggled like teaspoons clinking.

The lantern, sadly, was far too soggy to light. When the smallest spirit fluttered inside, it just made a damp little *fffp* sound and went dark.

"Hm," said Dora. He turned the lantern over. River water dribbled out of it, then a tiny startled snail, then *more* river water. "Well. That's a no from the snail too."

He propped the soggy lantern against a rock to dry, gave it an encouraging pat, and tried very hard not to feel discouraged. Story-keepers, he reminded himself, never abandon a tale halfway. The good part always came *after* the part where everything went a bit soggy.

"Hmm," said Dora, righting his beret. "A soggy lantern is like a story with no ending. We'll need something drier. And something to light it with — a warm sort of light, the kind that comes from inside."

The three spirits looked at each other. Then they looked at *Dora* — at his round, toasty, faintly-glowing self.

"You," whispered the first spirit, "feel warm. Like a memory."

Dora blushed all the way through to his red bean.

Now, here is a thing about lantern-lighting spirits that Dora was about to learn, so lean in close, because it is the cozy heart of this whole story. A chochin doesn't truly light up from a candle. It lights up from a *memory* — a warm, kind one, held gently and shared. The candle is just the candle. The memory is the *glow*.

But the little spirits had floated so long and so far that they had forgotten their warm memories entirely. That was why their lanterns had gone out, drifted off, and lost themselves in the reeds. The spirits were lost because they couldn't remember what kept them warm.

"Well," said Dora, sitting down very seriously in the grass, "*that* I can fix. I am a story-keeper. Memories are simply stories that already happened. Sit, little ones. Tell me — what is the warmest thing you remember?"

The first spirit thought hard, scrunching its tiny face. "There was... a grandmother. She made dumplings every summer. She let me lick the bowl."

And as the spirit spoke, the softest thing happened: it began to glow brighter. A warm, buttery, dumpling-bowl gold.

"More!" said Dora, delighted, leaning so far forward his beret nearly fell off. "Tell me MORE!"

"She hummed while she cooked," the spirit went on, glowing brighter still. "She always saved the biggest dumpling for me. She said—" The spirit's voice went soft and shining. "She said I was her sweetest snack."

The glow burst out like a tiny sunrise.

Dora clapped his flaps together. "There it is! *That's* the light! Quick — into the lantern!"

He found a second lantern wedged under a stone, dry and crinkly-new, and held it open. The first spirit zipped inside, glowing with grandmother-and-dumplings warmth, and — *foom!* — the whole chochin lit up a gorgeous golden gold, swaying softly on the night air like a little floating moon.

"WONDERFUL!" Dora cheered, and did a wobbly happy spin that nearly tipped him into the river. "Next!"

The second spirit went next, and it was so excited it nearly fizzled itself out. "Mine! Mine! I have one!" Its warmest memory was a cat named Mochi who slept on its feet every winter and purred like a tiny engine.

"A cat named *Mochi*," Dora repeated, pressing a flap to his cheek in delight. "Now THAT is a cat with excellent taste in names."

As the spirit remembered — the purring, the weight of the warm cat, the way Mochi blinked slow and trusting, the one whisker that always tickled its nose — it began to shine a soft rosy pink, brighter and brighter, until it lit its lantern bright as a sunset peach. The little chochin bobbed up so cheerfully it bonked Dora gently on the beret.

"Oof — and a *boop* from the lantern," he laughed. "I'll allow that too."

The third spirit was the shyest. It hid behind Dora's beret and wouldn't speak.

"I... don't have one," it whispered. "I don't remember anything warm. I think I've forgotten too much."

And oh, that made Dora's red bean heart go *squeeze*.

He sat down right beside the little spirit, all soft and toasty, and lowered his voice to his coziest storyteller hush. "Listen, friend. Forgetting isn't the end of a story. Sometimes a memory is just hiding, like the sweet filling tucked inside a pancake. You can't always see it. But it's there, keeping everything warm from the inside. Let's go looking for it together."

"How?" sniffled the spirit.

"The way I find anything," said Dora. "By asking gentle questions and listening with my whole self. Now — close your eyes. What do you smell, when you think of *home*?"

The little spirit closed its eyes. The river murmured. A breeze went by smelling of grilled corn.

"...Tea," the spirit said slowly. "Warm tea. And rain on a roof. And — and someone reading to me." Its eyes flew open, glowing. "Someone read me stories! Every single night! I'd fall asleep before the ending, and they'd always — they'd always be there in the morning to finish it!"

And the third spirit lit up the brightest of all — a deep, glowing, golden-honey warmth, the exact color of two pancakes hugging sweet red bean.

Dora's eyes went misty. "Why," he said, voice wobbling, "that sounds *just* like me."

"It's *you!*" the spirit laughed, looping around his head. "You're a warm memory too, Dora! You've been someone's bedtime story all along!"

Dora had to dab his eye with the corner of his beret. "Well," he said. "I'll allow it."

Into the last lantern the third spirit flew, and the chochin blazed up so warm and bright it made the reeds glow gold and the frogs sigh contentedly.

Three lanterns. Three warm spirits. One very proud, slightly teary dorayaki.

"You did it!" the spirits sang, swirling around him in a glowing ribbon of gold and pink and honey. "You found all our lights!"

"*You* found them," said Dora, dabbing his eye again. He was running out of dry beret. "I only asked the questions. The warmth was always yours. I just helped you remember where you'd tucked it." He patted his own round middle, right over the sweet red bean. "That's the trick with warm things. They don't go away when you forget them. They just go quiet and wait, snug as filling, until somebody asks the right gentle question."

Together they carried the lanterns to the water's edge. One by one, gentle as a goodnight kiss, Dora set them floating onto the dark glass of the river. The lanterns bobbed, found the current, and began to drift — soft golden lights gliding away beneath the summer stars, like wishes that knew exactly where they were going.

"Thank you, story-keeper," the spirits chimed, their voices going soft and faraway and happy. "We remember now. We'll remember all the way home. And as long as someone remembers us — we'll keep glowing."

"Always," Dora promised, waving both flaps. "I'll keep your story. Story-keepers always do."

He stood on the bank a long, warm while, watching the three little lanterns float down and down the gentle river until they were just three more stars among all the others. And do you know — Dora didn't feel sad even one bit. Because the lights weren't going *away*. They were going *home*, glowing the whole way, lit by love that had simply been waiting to be remembered.

Dora picked up his story book, settled his beret, and smiled his softest red-bean smile.

"And then," he whispered to the frogs, "the brave little dumpling learned the warmest truth of all: that remembering someone keeps their light glowing forever." He patted his round tummy. "*That's* a good filling."

The frogs, this time, said nothing at all. They were already half asleep — and so, dear one, are you.

## Goodnight Blessing
May your warm memories float gently beside you like little golden lanterns on a quiet summer river. May everyone you love stay glowing softly in your heart, lighting the kindest path through your dreams. Remember the people who read you stories, who saved you the biggest dumpling, who hummed while they cooked — and feel how warm that makes you, even now. You are someone's warm memory too, and that is a glow that never goes out. Snuggle down, breathe slow, and let the lanterns carry you off. Goodnight.
