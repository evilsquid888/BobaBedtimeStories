# Story 21: Kuma Kuri and the Mountain Hot-Spring Eggs

**Main Character:** Kuma the Kuri (Chestnut)
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A misty volcanic valley where eggs cook gently in the steamy hot-spring water
**Theme:** Quiet helpers make the warmest difference
**Japanese Spotlight:** たまご (tamago) = egg

---

High up where the mountain wore a hat of cloud, there lived a small roasted chestnut named Kuma the Kuri, who was so shy that he sometimes said "good morning" to his own scarf instead of to anybody else.

Kuma was glossy and mahogany-brown, round and sturdy as a tiny brave acorn-knight, with a fuzzy little cap and an autumn-leaf scarf wrapped twice around his neck for courage. He glowed warm, the way a chestnut does after the fire — toasty on the outside, gentle on the inside.

This morning, Kuma was doing his very favorite thing: tiptoeing through the steam where nobody could see him be nervous. The volcanic valley puffed and burbled and whispered, *pssshhhh*, and Kuma whispered *pssshhh* right back, because it felt rude not to answer a mountain.

Then his foot bumped something round and smooth and white in the rocks.

"Oh!" said Kuma, very quietly, to the steam. "Hello there. Are you a pebble?"

It was not a pebble. It was an egg.

It was, in fact, a whole little nest of them, tucked between two warm stones beside the steamiest, bubbliest pool in the whole valley — a pool so toasty that the water hummed like a kettle that had forgotten to stop.

"A たまご (tamago) = egg," Kuma breathed, because he had read the word once in a cozy book and had been saving it for a special occasion. "A nest of tamago, all cold and lonely. That won't do. That won't do at all."

Now, Kuma was shy, but Kuma was the sort of brave that nobody notices — the quiet kind, the kind that just *does the thing*. He gathered the little eggs into his autumn-leaf scarf, careful as carrying clouds, and waddled to the edge of the kettle-warm pool.

"Small and roasty," he murmured to himself, which was his private courage-chant, "ready and toasty."

And — *plop, plop, plop* — he lowered the eggs into the steaming water on a flat stone, like putting babies down for a warm nap.

He counted. He had read about this too. "One mountain minute. Two mountain minutes. Three..."

The steam curled. The water hummed. And after seven and a half mountain minutes (Kuma counted carefully, the way you count when you truly care), the eggs came out warm and gleaming, their shells gone the softest, dreamiest shade of brown.

Kuma peeled one with his tiny careful fingers. Inside, the white was tender and the yolk was a sunset — warm and golden and just-set, the perfect mountain snack for anybody whose toes had gone cold from climbing.

"Oh my," whispered Kuma. "Oh my, oh my."

He had discovered something wonderful. The only trouble was that Kuma was much too shy to know what to *do* with something wonderful.

He sat down on a warm rock and thought about it, scarf pulled up over his nose.

What does a shy chestnut do with a kettle-warm pool and a nest of perfect eggs? He could keep them all to himself, he supposed. But that didn't feel toasty at all — that felt cold and pebble-lonely, the same way the eggs had felt before he warmed them. No, no. Kuma had spent his whole quiet life watching other friends from far away — the bold ones, the loud ones, the ones who waved and laughed and got noticed. He'd always wished he could give the way they gave. The trouble was, every time he opened his mouth to be helpful, the words got shy and hid behind his scarf.

"But a tamago," Kuma whispered to himself, an idea glowing up warm inside him like a little ember, "a tamago doesn't need me to *talk*. A tamago just needs me to *care*."

And that, it turned out, was something Kuma could do better than anyone in the whole valley.

That was when the first traveler came.

It was a weary little snow-monkey-manju named Yuki, hiking down from the high ridge with rosy onsen-warmed cheeks and very, very tired feet. She didn't see Kuma at all — he had tucked himself behind a steam-cloud out of pure bashfulness — but she did see something marvelous waiting on a clean flat stone right in the middle of the path:

One warm egg. Peeled. Gleaming. Steaming gently. And beside it, written in pebbles, the wobbly little word: *for you*.

"For... me?" Yuki blinked. She looked left. She looked right. She saw nobody (because Kuma had ducked completely behind his own scarf). "Warm cheeks, warm heart, warm friends," she sighed happily, and ate the egg in two delighted bites. "Mmmm. Whoever you are, you are *the kindest*. Thank you, mystery friend!"

And off she went, warm right down to her toes.

Behind the steam, Kuma turned the exact color of a ripe persimmon. Someone had said *thank you*. To him! Well — to his egg. But still!

His little chestnut heart went *toasty-warm*.

So Kuma did the only thing a quiet helper knows how to do. He made another egg. And another. And he set them out, gleaming and peeled, each one with a little pebble-word beside it: *for you.*

All that long misty day, weary mountain travelers came down the path, and all that long misty day, a shy chestnut hid in the steam and warmed their tummies one tamago at a time.

A bowl of ramen named Rin trudged by, his golden broth gone lukewarm and his noodle-curls drooping with tiredness. He found an egg waiting and gasped, "Everything's better warm and bubbly! Oh, this is *exactly* what I needed!" And he steamed right back up to a happy bubble.

A sleepy melon-bun named Mero wandered through, half-dreaming as always, and found his egg and murmured, "Crackle on the outside... dreams on the inside... and tamago in the *middle*." He didn't even wonder who'd left it. He just felt cozy, the way you feel when somebody tucks the blanket up while you sleep. Then he lay right down on the warm path and took a little nap, an egg-shell hat balanced on his head, snoring soft as a teakettle. Kuma very gently tiptoed out, slid a folded leaf under his sleepy head for a pillow, and tiptoed straight back into the steam before Mero could so much as blink.

A pair of giggly takoyaki balls came bouncing through, too — Tako and Yaki, finishing each other's sentences as always. "Look, an egg!" "Two eggs!" "One each!" "Roll us once —" "— we come as two!" They bonked happily together, peeled their eggs side by side, and bounced off down the mountain leaving a trail of sauce-zig-zag giggles. Kuma had to press his scarf over his mouth so they wouldn't hear *him* giggling back.

Each one said thank you to the steam. Each one walked away warmer. And each time, Kuma glowed a little brighter behind his cloud, hugging his scarf, never once stepping out to be seen.

But here is the thing about doing kind things in secret: sometimes the kindness comes looking *for you*.

Late in the afternoon, the steam thickened into a proper mountain fog — thick as miso soup — and from somewhere up the ridge came a worried little voice.

"Hello? Hellooo? Is anybody — *oof* — is anybody there?"

Kuma peeked out. A tiny butter-yellow kei-truck named Kei had taken a wrong turn in the fog. His big headlight-eyes were wide and wobbly, his springs were shivering, and his little knitted seat-cover sweater had gone all damp and droopy. He was very lost, and very cold, and very near to crying small oily tears.

"Putt... putt..." he hiccupped. "I can't see the road. I can't see *anything*."

And every shy bone in Kuma's roasty little body said: *hide. Stay in the steam. Somebody braver will help.*

But there was nobody braver. There was only Kuma.

So Kuma took the deepest breath a chestnut has ever taken, wrapped his courage-scarf tight, and stepped — for the very first time all day — right out of the steam where everyone could see him.

"H-hello," he said, in a voice as small as a chestnut. Then, a little louder, because Kei needed it louder: "Hello! You're not lost. You're *found*. Follow my glow."

And he glowed — warm and toasty-brown, like a little roasted lantern — and he waddled ahead through the fog, and Kei putt-putted gratefully behind, headlights fixed on that cozy roasty light.

"Fall down seven, bounce up eight," Kuma whispered to himself (he had borrowed that chant from a daruma he'd once admired from very far away). His feet were nervous. His cheeks were hot. But he did not stop.

He led Kei right down to the warm bubbling pool, sat him beside the steam to dry his sweater, and — of course — made him an egg.

"For me?" sniffled Kei, headlight-eyes brimming.

"For you," said Kuma. And this time, he did not duck behind a cloud. This time, he stayed.

Kei ate his warm tamago, and his shivers stopped, and his springs went *boing* with happiness, and he looked at the little glowing chestnut and gasped:

"It was YOU! The eggs all over the mountain — the *for you* eggs — that was all *you*?? Everybody's been talking about the Mystery Mountain Helper! There are songs! Little songs! And it was a chestnut the whole time!"

Kuma turned persimmon-red again. "I... I didn't want a fuss," he mumbled into his scarf. "I just wanted everyone to be warm."

"But that's the WARMEST thing of all!" beeped Kei. "Putt-putt — you have to let people *thank* you!"

And right then, as if the mountain itself agreed, the fog lifted — and down the path came every traveler Kuma had quietly fed that day. Yuki and Rin and dreamy Mero and a dozen more, all of them following the rumor of the Mystery Mountain Helper, all of them holding their bellies that were still cozy-warm from his eggs.

When they saw it was little Kuma — small and shy and glowing — they did not make a big loud fuss (because they were kind, and they could tell that a big loud fuss would make him want to crawl back into the steam).

Instead, very gently, they gathered close. And one by one, soft as snowfall, they whispered:

"Thank you."
"Thank you, Kuma."
"Thank you, quiet friend."

Yuki snuggled up beside him. "Warm cheeks, warm heart, warm friends," she said. "You gave us all three, and you didn't even want a thank-you. That's the best kind of warm there is."

Kuma's eyes went a little misty — happy-misty, like the valley. He had spent his whole life being the chestnut nobody noticed. And it turned out that quiet helpers don't make a *loud* difference.

They make a *warm* one.

So they all stayed, that evening, by the kettle-warm pool. Kuma showed them his secret — the perfect seven-and-a-half mountain minutes — and they made eggs together until the stars came out, steam curling up to meet them. And nobody was cold, and nobody was lost, and nobody, ever again, was alone in the fog.

And little Kuma the Kuri, the shy hero who glowed, learned that the bravest thing in the whole wide valley is to step out of the steam — just once — and let yourself be thanked.

## Goodnight Blessing

May your night be warm as an egg cooked in mountain steam, and may someone, somewhere, do you a small quiet kindness you'll never even know about. May you remember that you don't have to be loud to be wonderful — that the gentlest helpers light the warmest fires. Snuggle down now, little one, toasty as a chestnut by the embers. The steam is curling, the stars are out, and every cold toe in the valley is finally, perfectly warm. Goodnight.
