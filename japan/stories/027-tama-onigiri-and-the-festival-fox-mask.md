# Story 27: Tama Onigiri and the Festival Fox Mask

**Main Character:** Tama Onigiri
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A summer night matsuri with food stalls and lantern stands
**Theme:** Sharing a snack turns strangers into friends
**Japanese Spotlight:** **matsuri** (mah-tsoo-ree) — festival

---

On the warmest, most twinkly night of the whole summer, a plump little triangle of warm white rice came bouncing down the hill toward the river, and that triangle was Tama Onigiri. She had a crisp black band of seaweed wrapped around her middle like a sash, a tiny pickled-plum blush on one round cheek, and a backpack so stuffed it jingled. The backpack was not full of toys. Oh no. It was full of *snacks*.

"Extra rice balls, extra napkins, extra everything," Tama hummed, patting the bag. "I packed a little extra, just in case!"

Because tonight was the **matsuri** (mah-tsoo-ree) — festival — the big summer one, with paper lanterns strung overhead like a hundred glowing tangerines, and goldfish-scooping stalls, and the boomy, sleepy *don-don-don* of a faraway drum.

Tama took one step onto the festival path and a firefly bonked her right on the nose.

"Oh! Hello," said Tama. "Are you lost too?"

The firefly blinked twice, which Tama decided meant *yes, but happily so*, and off they both went into the glow. Tama was the sort who collected friends the way other people collected pebbles — happily, and far too many of them, and she never minded if they were small or wobbly or made entirely of light.

---

The matsuri was the busiest, most delicious-smelling place Tama had ever waddled into.

There were stalls of golden takoyaki sizzling in their little round pans, going *pop, pop, pop*. There were candy apples redder than a sunset and shaved ice towers taller than Tama's whole self. A stall man was flipping yakisoba noodles so high they nearly tickled a lantern. Somewhere a bell went *chirin-chirin*, and somewhere else, somebody was laughing so hard they got the hiccups.

There were stalls selling little spinning tops, and stalls where you could win a balloon shaped like a panda, and one stall that sold absolutely nothing but pickles, run by a very serious old radish who took his pickles *extremely* seriously. There were **chouchin** (choh-cheen) — paper lanterns — hung in long swaying rows, each one painted with a friend's name, bobbing in the warm river breeze like they were nodding hello. Tama waddled along with her mouth gently open in wonder, accidentally bumping a candy-apple cart, apologizing to it, and then apologizing again to the candy apple itself, just to be safe.

Tama loved it. Tama loved all of it. But Tama also noticed something most busy festival-goers would have rushed right past.

In the shadow behind the goldfish stall, where the lantern-light went soft and blue, somebody small was sitting all alone on a rock. They were wearing a **kitsune** (kee-tsoo-neh) mask — kitsune means fox — a darling little white fox face with curly red swirls on the cheeks and two pointy painted ears. The mask was cute as a button.

But the somebody behind it had their knees pulled up tight, and their toes were tapping a worried little tippy-tap, and they were watching all the fun the way you watch a parade through a fence: wanting in, and not quite knowing how.

"Hmm," said Tama, in her gentle, snacky way. "That looks like a tummy-rumble feeling. And I happen to be an *expert* at tummy-rumble feelings."

She waddled right over.

---

"Good evening!" said Tama warmly, plopping down on the rock beside the fox mask. "I'm Tama. I'm a rice ball. The pickled plum is real, the worry is optional."

The fox mask turned toward her very slowly. From behind it came the tiniest voice in the world.

"...H-hello."

"Are you enjoying the matsuri?" Tama asked.

The little fox-masked friend looked down at their tapping toes. "I *want* to," they whispered. "But there are so many people. And everybody already has a friend. And what if I do the games wrong? What if I scoop the goldfish and it goes *sploosh* and everyone laughs?"

Tama nodded the slow, understanding nod of someone who has personally worried about a great many goldfish.

"That," she said kindly, "is a very big feeling for such a little rock. You know what I do when I have a big feeling?"

"What?"

Tama swung her backpack around with a *thump* and unzipped it. A puff of warm rice-steam drifted out, smelling of comfort itself.

"I eat a snack," she said. "Big feelings get a lot smaller once your tummy is happy. It's science. Probably."

She pulled out a perfect little rice ball wrapped in a square of seaweed, still cozy-warm from her backpack, and held it out.

The fox mask hesitated. "...For me?"

"I packed a little extra, just in case," said Tama, with a wink. "And tonight, 'just in case' turned out to be *exactly* in case."

---

The little fox-masked friend took the rice ball in two careful paws. Then they tipped the mask up — just a smidge, just enough — and took a nibble.

There was a tiny pause.

"...It's *salty-soft*," they whispered, amazed. "And warm. And there's a surprise plum in the middle!"

"That's the best part," Tama agreed proudly. "Everybody likes the surprise plum. Even folks who say they don't. I once met a grumpy old radish at the pickle stall who *swore* he hated surprise plums, and then he ate seven and asked for the recipe. He pretended he was just checking it for quality. I let him keep his dignity."

The fox-masked stranger giggled — a small, muffled sound from behind the mask, but a giggle all the same.

And there, on a blue-lit rock behind the goldfish stall, the two of them munched together. It is a curious and wonderful fact about the world that it is very, very hard to feel lonely while you are sharing a snack with somebody. The fox-masked friend's worried toe-tapping slowed... and slowed... and stopped.

"My name's Konko," they said at last, a little louder now. "I always wear the fox mask at matsuri. When I have it on, nobody can see me being shy."

"It's a wonderful mask," said Tama honestly. "Very brave-looking. Very swirly. But you know what I think, Konko? I think the bravest fox is the one who's *also* a little nervous and plays anyway." She held up a second rice ball. "Want to test that theory? With snacks for backup?"

Konko's painted fox-eyes seemed to twinkle.

"...Okay," whispered Konko. "But you go first."

"Deal," said Tama. "Squish close, we'll go together."

---

The first stop was the goldfish-scooping pool, exactly the thing Konko had been dreading. The little paper paddles were so flimsy a sneeze could break them.

Tama went first, just as promised. She dipped her paddle in with great confidence — and the paper went *fwip*, dissolving instantly, and her goldfish swam off looking smug.

"Oops," said Tama cheerfully. "Well. The goldfish wins that round. I packed extra dignity, just in case."

Konko *giggled* — a real, bright, un-shy giggle that surprised even Konko.

"Your turn," said Tama. "And remember, the goldfish is already laughing at *me*, so you've got nothing to worry about."

Konko leaned over the pool. Tippy-tap went the toes — but only once. Then, very gently, Konko swooped the paddle low and slow, the way you'd scoop a sleepy kitten, and lifted out one shimmering orange goldfish into the little bowl.

"I DID IT!" Konko shouted, loud enough that the takoyaki stall went *pop* in surprise. "TAMA, I DID IT!"

"You absolutely did it!" cheered Tama, bouncing on her round little bottom. "And nobody laughed except us, and we were laughing *with* the goldfish, which is allowed."

---

After that, the night unrolled like a ribbon of glowing tangerine lanterns.

They tried the **wata-ame** (wah-tah-ah-meh) — cotton candy — stall, where the spun-sugar cloud was bigger than both of them combined. Tama took one bite and got a sticky pink beard, which she wore proudly for the rest of the night, occasionally stroking it like a wise old grandfather. "It vanishes the moment it touches your tongue," Konko marveled, watching a puff disappear. "Like a tiny sweet ghost," Tama agreed solemnly, "but the friendly, snackable kind." A real ghost would never have been so delicious, and they both decided that was for the best.

They tried the ring toss, where Konko hooked a tiny wind-up frog on the very first throw, and Tama hooked nothing at all but somehow knocked her own seaweed sash crooked, which made Konko laugh so hard the fox mask slipped right off.

And here is the thing: Konko didn't grab for it.

Underneath the mask was a small, round, perfectly ordinary, perfectly lovely face — a little dumpling-soft friend with cheeks pink as the festival lanterns and a smile that had been hiding far, far too long.

"Oops," said Konko, reaching for the mask. Then they stopped. "...Actually. Maybe I'll just hold it for a while."

"I like both your faces," said Tama. "But this one shares snacks better."

They bought a paper cone of shaved ice and split it down the middle, blue syrup on one side and strawberry on the other, and swapped bites until both of them had matching rainbow tongues. They stuck out their tongues at each other to compare, and laughed so hard a passing goldfish-seller asked if they were all right, and they had to nod *yes* very seriously while still laughing, which is one of the hardest things a person can do.

They watched the big drum boom *don-don-don* under the lanterns, and Konko — who only an hour ago had been hiding behind a rock — actually clapped along, and even did a tiny shy hop that was nearly a dance. Tama did a much less tiny, much less shy hop, lost her balance because she is shaped like a triangle, and rolled gently onto her back like an overturned dumpling. "I meant to do that," she announced to the sky. She had not meant to do that. Konko helped her up, and that felt nice too — the helping, and the being helped.

They fed three crumbs to the firefly, who had stuck around the whole time and who Tama was now fairly sure had adopted them. The firefly accepted the crumbs with great dignity and blinked twice, which Tama had decided some time ago was firefly for *thank you, you are my family now*.

"Tama?" said Konko, as they sat with the goldfish bowl glowing between them. "Thank you for the rice ball. I think... I think it wasn't really about the rice ball."

"No?" said Tama, with a knowing little smile, because she had packed *exactly* the right amount of just-in-case.

"It was about somebody sitting down next to me," said Konko softly. "When I was sure nobody would."

Tama's pickled-plum cheek went the warmest, happiest pink.

"Konko," she said, "that's the whole reason I pack extra. A snack is never *just* a snack. It's a way to say: *here, there's room for you too.*"

---

When the very last lantern was lit and the drum slowed to a sleepy heartbeat, Tama and Konko walked back up the hill together, goldfish bowl swinging, fox mask tucked safe in the backpack right next to one final emergency rice ball.

"Will you come to the next matsuri?" Konko asked.

"I'll pack extra," promised Tama. "I *always* pack extra. That's how you make sure there's always enough room for one more friend."

And the firefly, blinking sleepily, agreed that this was the very best kind of math.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, may your pockets always hold a little extra to share, and may someone always sit beside you when you need it most. May you be brave enough to play even when your toes are tapping, and kind enough to scoot over and make room for somebody new. The lanterns are dim now, the drum is hushed, and the fireflies are tucking themselves into the grass. Snuggle down warm, like a rice ball wrapped cozy in its seaweed sash. Goodnight.
