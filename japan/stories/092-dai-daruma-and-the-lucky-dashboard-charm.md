# Story 92: Dai Daruma and the Lucky Dashboard Charm

**Main Character:** Dai Daruma (daruma doll)
**Guest Characters:** The whole car crew — Kei the kei-truck, Pock Ramune, Tako & Yaki, Maru Matcha, Coro the Korokke
**Setting:** A cozy garage workshop restoring a beloved classic car
**Theme:** The luck you feel is the love you put in
**Japanese Spotlight:** **omamori** (oh-mah-MOH-ree) — lucky charm

---

Dai Daruma had been sitting on a dusty shelf in the back of the garage for so long that a spider had tried to use his single painted eye as a doorbell.

"Excuse me," Dai had said politely, "I am not a button."

The spider apologized and moved out.

Now, you should know something about Dai Daruma right away, before anything exciting happens — and exciting things were about to happen, so listen quick. He was a round, roly-poly crimson bun shaped like a little dome, with the biggest, most determined eyebrows you ever saw. Eyebrows like two brave caterpillars who had decided, together, to never give up. He had ONE painted eye. The other eye was blank and waiting — waiting for a wish to come true so someone could paint it in.

He wobbled. He always wobbled. But here is the wonderful thing about Dai Daruma: no matter how far he tipped, he always, always bounced back up.

"Fall down seven," he announced to the empty garage that morning, tipping left, then right, then upright again, "BOUNCE UP EIGHT!"

That was when the big doors rumbled open, and the whole crew rolled in.

The first one through was **Kei** the kei-truck — a teeny, round, butter-yellow mini-truck with big headlight eyes and a knitted seat-cover worn like a cozy sweater. "Putt-putt, let's GO!" Kei beeped, bouncing happily on her springs.

Behind Kei came Pock Ramune, a frosty pale-blue soda bottle who zipped in leaving a trail of happy bubbles — *pop, pop, pop!* Then Tako & Yaki, the giggly golden takoyaki twins, bounced in finishing each other's sentences ("We heard—" "—there's a SURPRISE in here!"). Maru Matcha glided in last, calm and jade-green, her little bamboo whisk tucked behind her ear like a pencil, with Coro the Korokke trotting beside her, golden and crispy and already a little flustered.

"There it is," whispered Coro, and a tiny puff of steam escaped him the way it always did when he felt big feelings. "Oh, look at HER."

In the center of the garage, under a sheet gone gray with dust, slept a car.

Not a new car. An old one. A beloved one. When Maru Matcha gently lifted the sheet, everyone went quiet — even the twins.

She was a classic. Round fenders like a friendly turtle's shell, a long curving hood, headlights like two surprised eyes, and a coat of paint that had once been the deep red of a sunset but was now faded, scratched, and freckled with rust. One mirror dangled by a thread. A door hung crooked. Her tires were as flat as forgotten pancakes.

"She used to belong to somebody's grandfather," Maru Matcha said softly. "He drove her up every mountain road in the prefecture. And now..." She ran her whisk along the dusty hood, drawing a small clean line. "Now it's our turn to wake her up."

"WAKE HER UP!" cheered Tako & Yaki, high-fiving so hard they both fell over and rolled in a circle.

From his shelf, Dai Daruma's brave eyebrows lifted higher than they had in years.

A challenge. A great big never-give-up, fall-down-seven, bounce-up-eight kind of challenge. His painted heart (he was sure he had one, somewhere under all that sweet bean paste) began to thump.

"Friends!" he boomed in his coach-voice. "I will help! I will ENCOURAGE!"

Nobody heard him. He was very small and very far back on the shelf.

"Ahem. I will encourage from... here, then."

So the work began, and oh, what work it was.

Kei the kei-truck became the hauler. All day she putt-putted in and out of the garage, her little flatbed loaded with new tires, cans of paint, and shiny silver bolts. "Putt-PUTT!" she beeped each time, springs bouncing, very pleased to be useful. Once she hauled in a part so big it stuck out both sides, and she wobbled down the road like a turtle wearing a surfboard, and everyone laughed, and Kei laughed too — *beep beep beep* — which only made her wobble more.

Pock Ramune was the polisher. He zipped across the old red paint at top speed, fizzing and buffing, *pop-pop-pop*, leaving the metal gleaming behind him. "Watch THIS!" he cried, and zoomed across the hood so fast his bubbles spelled out a tiny *WOW* in the air before they popped.

Tako & Yaki were... well. They were the *enthusiasm*. They were not, strictly speaking, the most helpful. They tried to tighten one bolt and somehow tightened each other together instead, and rolled around stuck like that for ten whole minutes, giggling. Then they discovered the car's horn, and pressed it forty-one times in a row — *beep beep beep BEEP* — until Maru Matcha gently set a quiet bamboo whisk between them and the button. But they fetched things fast, and they cheered louder than anyone, and a job goes quicker with cheering. That is just a fact.

"Hand me the wrench!" Coro would call, and *zoom* — a takoyaki twin would deliver it before the sentence finished.

"Now the OTHER wrench!"

"There's only one wrench, Coro," Maru Matcha would murmur.

"...Then hand me the same wrench again, very nicely!" And they would, and they'd take a bow, and Coro would laugh so hard a little steam puffed right out of his crispy ears.

Coro the Korokke did the careful work — the patient sanding, the gentle wiping, the soft polishing of the little chrome bits. Whenever something went wrong, Coro puffed steam and fretted, "Oh no, oh no, did I do it wrong?" And whenever it went right, he glowed golden and warm and whispered, "Oh. Oh, that's nice."

And Maru Matcha? She kept everyone calm.

"Whisk slow," she said, when the twins got too giddy. "Feel calm," she said, when Coro got too steamy. She moved through the noisy garage like a cool green breeze, and somehow, wherever she went, the panic settled and the work got smoother.

Days went by. The car began to change.

The flat tires became round and bouncy. The crooked door swung straight and shut with a satisfying *thunk*. The faded paint, layer by careful layer, became deep glowing red again — the red of a sunset, the red of a ripe plum, the red (Dai Daruma noticed proudly) of a certain roly-poly daruma he knew very well.

Each evening, when the work was done, the crew flopped down together in a tired, happy heap on the garage floor and shared a snack — usually whatever Coro had nervously over-prepared, which was always far too much, which meant there was always enough. Pock fizzed soda for everyone. Maru Matcha whisked a little pot of warm matcha. And from his shelf, Dai Daruma watched the friends giggle and yawn and lean on each other, and he thought to himself that this — *this* — looked an awful lot like luck. The good kind. The kind that doesn't come from a charm at all, but from people who keep showing up for the same dusty old car, day after day, just because they love her.

But something was wrong.

One evening, when the car was nearly finished, the crew gathered around her and frowned. She *looked* perfect. She gleamed. Her chrome sparkled. Her headlights shone.

But she wouldn't start.

Kei nudged her bumper gently. Pock fizzed encouragingly into her engine. Coro checked every bolt twice. Tako & Yaki even sang to her. Nothing. The engine just sighed — *vrrm... vrm... vmm* — and went quiet, like a sleepy creature that didn't quite want to wake.

"We did everything right," said Coro, steaming with worry. "Why won't she go?"

Maru Matcha sat down on the cold garage floor and was quiet for a long, long moment.

"Maybe," she said at last, "she's missing something. Not a part. Something else."

And that is when Dai Daruma decided he had encouraged from the shelf long enough.

He took a deep breath. He looked down — it was a *very* high shelf — and his single painted eye went a little wide. But his eyebrows, those brave brave caterpillars, did not even tremble.

"FALL DOWN SEVEN," he hollered, and he leaned forward, and he *rolled.*

He bounced off the shelf — *boing!* — off a toolbox — *boing!* — off Kei's springy little roof — *BOING!* — and tumbled, head over round red bottom, right into the middle of the startled crew.

"BOUNCE UP EIGHT!" he finished, popping upright, wobbling, steadying, grinning his painted grin.

"It's the daruma!" gasped Tako & Yaki together.

"You've been here the whole time?" said Coro.

"The WHOLE time," said Dai Daruma. "And I have been thinking. You have given this car new tires, new paint, new bolts. You have given her your hands and your days and your whole hearts. But you have not given her one thing." He puffed up his crimson chest. "An **omamori** (oh-mah-MOH-ree) — a lucky charm. Every beloved car needs one swinging from her mirror — a little guardian, full of all the wishes and love that went into her."

"Then it should be YOU!" cried Tako & Yaki together.

Dai's eyebrows wiggled, very flattered. "Tempting! Extremely tempting. But no." He tapped the smooth blank spot where his second eye waited. "A daruma's eyes belong to his own wish — and mine waits for the biggest wish of all. This car needs a little daruma of her very own. And as it happens..." He leaned in, lowering his coach-voice to a coach-whisper. "A daruma who sits on a shelf for years sees *everything*. Bottom drawer of the big toolbox. Behind the spare spark plugs."

Coro hurried over, tugged the drawer open with a squeak, and let out a tiny gasp of steam. There, wrapped in a soft cloth, lay a teeny daruma charm no bigger than a cherry — round and crimson on a faded red cord, both eyes blank and patiently waiting. Grandfather must have bought it at a shrine long ago, meaning to hang it the very day the car was finished. He had simply never gotten the chance.

"Then we'll finish it for him," said Maru Matcha, smiling her slow, calm smile. "A daruma is for wishes. And there's an old way to wake a wish. When the work begins, you paint in one eye. And when the wish comes true..."

"...you paint in the other," whispered Coro, glowing.

So they did. Maru Matcha hung the tiny charm from the rearview mirror on its soft red cord, where it swung gently over the whole front seat — and she set Dai Daruma on the dashboard right beneath it, because every brand-new charm deserves a coach. "Sit tall, little one," Dai instructed tenderly. "Eyebrows determined. Wobble proud. You hold the wishes — we'll do the work." Then Coro, with the steadiest crumb-steady grip he could manage, dipped a tiny brush and painted the little charm's first eye fresh and bright — the wish begun at last — and they all wished together — *please, dear car, wake up* — and held their breath.

But first, Maru Matcha did one more quiet thing. She slipped beneath the hood and gave the last stubborn bolt on the fuel line — the very one Kei had been beeping about all afternoon — a careful final quarter-turn. "Luck works best," she murmured, "when the bolts are snug."

Kei put a hopeful little tire on the pedal.

The engine coughed. It sputtered. It went *vrm... vrm...*

And then — as the tiny charm swung from the mirror, and Dai Daruma beamed up at it with every ounce of stubborn never-give-up courage in his round red body — the engine roared to life. *VROOM!* The headlights blazed. The whole car shivered awake and gave a happy little honk, *BEEP*, as if she'd only been waiting for someone to believe in her enough — and, as Maru pointed out gently, for that very last snug bolt. The charm was the blessing. The work was the waking. A beloved car needs both.

The garage exploded with cheers. Pock fizzed bubbles everywhere. Tako & Yaki rolled in joyful circles. Coro cried happy golden tears (and a little steam). And there, in the middle of it all, Maru Matcha lifted the brush one more time and painted in the little charm's *second* eye — because the wish had come true.

Two bright eyes on the tiny guardian. A wish fulfilled. A car alive. And Dai Daruma's own second eye? Still blank, still patient, still waiting — exactly the way he wanted it.

That night, they took her for her first drive — down the quiet mountain road under a sky full of stars, Kei putt-putting proudly behind, everyone laughing, the engine humming a happy song.

And Dai Daruma rode proudly on the dashboard, his one painted eye shining, the little two-eyed charm swinging above him — both of them watching over the friends who had loved this old car back to life. He understood something now, deep in his sweet-bean heart.

The luck wasn't *in* the charm. The luck was all of them — every careful bolt, every silly cheer, every patient day. The charm was just the little round red place that got to hold all that love.

"The luck you feel," he said softly to his tiny new student, "is the love you put in."

And the car beeped, very gently, as if to say: *exactly.*

## Goodnight Blessing
May you be like a beloved old car, woken up gently by friends who care. May the people who love you polish your worried days until they shine, and may you always bounce back up, no matter how many times you wobble. Keep a little lucky charm in your heart tonight — not because the world is scary, but because love is real, and it's swinging right there above you, watching over your dreams. Whisk slow. Feel calm. Close your two bright eyes. Goodnight.
