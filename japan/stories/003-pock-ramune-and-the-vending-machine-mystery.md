# Story 3: Pock Ramune and the Vending Machine Mystery

**Main Character:** Pock Ramune
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A glowing row of Tokyo street vending machines after dark
**Theme:** Curiosity is wonderful when you share what you find
**Japanese Spotlight:** **Sugoi** (soo-goy) — amazing, wow

---

Pock Ramune could not sit still. He had tried — once, for almost four whole seconds — and it had nearly fizzed his cap off.

So instead he zipped down the little Tokyo side street where the vending machines glowed like a row of friendly lanterns, his frosty pale-blue glass body bouncing, his bubble-freckles popping happy little *pop! pop! pops!* into the cool night air. The marble in his neck rattled with excitement.

"Bubbles out, world!" he giggled, leaving a trail of tiny shining fizz-dots behind him like a comet made of soda.

The vending machines hummed. They were his favorite thing in the whole city — tall, warm-lit, full of mysteries behind glass. Orange juice here. Cold barley tea there. A machine that sold tiny umbrellas, which Pock found *deeply* hilarious for reasons he could not explain.

But tonight, at the very end of the row, one machine glowed a soft, sleepy gold he had never noticed before.

And it was doing something extremely strange.

---

*Ka-chunk.*

A can dropped into the tray with a gentle thump. All by itself. Nobody had pushed a single button. Nobody was even *there.*

Pock skidded to a stop so fast his bubbles caught up and bonked into the back of his head. *Pop-pop-pop!*

"Whoa-whoa-whoa," he whispered. He rolled closer, marble clinking. The can sat in the tray, steaming gently, a little curl of warmth rising into the dark. He poked it with one fizzy finger.

"Warm!" he gasped. "A *warm* can! At *midnight!* That came out by ITSELF!"

He pressed his cool glass nose to the machine's window. Inside, the rows were full of cans, every single one of them the same plain silver, with no label, no name, no picture. Just one little hand-painted star on each lid, gold as candlelight.

Pock fizzed so hard with wonder that he floated three inches off the ground.

"**Sugoi** (soo-goy) — amazing," he breathed, because some things are so wonderful that the only word that fits is a Japanese one. "*Sugoi!*"

He had to know. He *had* to. Who filled this machine? Where did the warm cans come from? Why a star? His curiosity bubbled up so big and bright that for one teeny tiny second, Pock thought about keeping it all to himself — a secret machine, just for him, the most marvelous discovery in all of Tokyo.

But a secret you keep alone, Pock realized, fizzes flat awfully fast.

A wonder you *share* — now THAT keeps its bubbles.

"Friends!" he shouted, and rocketed off into the night. "I need my FRIENDS!"

---

He found Momo Mochi first, of course, because Momo was always somewhere soft and snuggly. She was curled on a warm sidewalk grate, and the moment Pock told her about the mystery machine, her rosy peach cheeks went round with delight and her taffy arms stretched all the way out for a hug.

"A warm can, all by itself?" Momo squished him gently. "Oh, that's the squishiest mystery I ever heard! Squish together, stay together — let's go solve it!"

Next they bounced past Maru Matcha, who was sitting very calmly on a bench, twirling her little bamboo whisk and watching the stars do nothing in particular.

"A machine that gives a gift every midnight, to no one, asking nothing back," Maru said slowly, her half-moon eyes crinkling. She tapped the whisk against her jade-green cheek. "Hmm. That is either a ghost… or a very kind someone." She stood up. "Whisk slow. Feel calm. We will find out gently — mysteries do not like to be chased."

"Right! Gently!" Pock zoomed in two full circles around her head. "Gentle-gentle-gentle-POP!"

Maru sighed the patient sigh of a calm friend with a fizzy one.

The last to join was Tama Onigiri, the plump little rice-ball triangle with the crisp nori sash and a tiny plum blush on one cheek. She came waddling up at once, a small cloth bundle bouncing on her back.

"I heard there's an adventure," Tama said warmly, "so I packed a little extra, just in case. Rice crackers. A blanket. Three pairs of mittens. You never know!" She blinked. "Why would we need three pairs of mittens?"

"You never know!" everyone agreed.

---

They tiptoed back to the glowing machine. It was almost midnight. The street was hushed and blue, the way Tokyo gets late at night when even the traffic lights blink slow and dreamy. Their warm-window glows reflected in the puddles like little floating moons.

"Okay," whispered Pock, vibrating. "Plan. We watch. We wait. We catch the mystery in the actual ACT."

"And we do not pounce," added Maru.

"And we do not pounce," Pock repeated solemnly. Then, two seconds later: "But what if I *want* to pou—"

"No pouncing," said all three friends at once.

So they hid behind a stack of crates — Momo squishing flat as a pancake to fit, Tama sharing out rice crackers, Maru sitting perfectly still, and Pock holding his bubbles in so hard that little *fzzt, fzzt* squeaks kept slipping out anyway.

The streetlight clicked. A clock somewhere far away began to bong.

*Bong. Bong. Bong…*

And then, soft as snowfall, something tiny came around the corner.

It wasn't a ghost. It wasn't a giant. It was the smallest, roundest, most cinnamon-colored little creature any of them had ever seen — a sleepy mound of fur about the size of an orange, with two round ears and a tail like a fluffy comma. It carried, balanced on its head, a single warm silver can with a gold star painted on the lid.

It was a **tanuki** (tah-noo-kee) — a raccoon-dog. A baby one. And it was on its tippy-toes.

The friends held their breath.

The little tanuki padded up to the machine, opened a tiny door in the back that none of them had noticed, and *very* carefully placed the warm can inside. It dabbed a fresh gold star onto the lid with the tip of its tail, which was apparently also a paintbrush. Then it patted the can goodnight, the way you'd pat a sleeping puppy.

*Ka-chunk.* The machine warmed it. The can rolled into the tray, ready for whoever came along.

And the baby tanuki turned to scamper away — and saw four glowing dessert friends staring at it with their mouths wide open.

It froze. Its little ears went flat. It looked like it might cry.

---

"Oh, sweetie, no, no, no!" Momo unsquished herself at once and reached out gently. "We're friends! We're not scary, we're just very, *very* nosy."

"Catastrophically nosy," agreed Pock, drifting down slow and soft so as not to fizz too loud. "I'm Pock. Are *you* the midnight mystery? Because — *sugoi.* You're the best mystery I've ever met."

The little tanuki sniffled. In a teeny voice, smaller than a teacup, it explained.

Its grandmother had run the warm-can machine for *years* — one free can every midnight, for anyone walking home cold and tired and far from anyone who loved them. A late-shift worker. A lonely traveler. A friend who'd had a hard day. "Grandma said," the tanuki whispered, "a warm thing in your hands at midnight reminds you that *somebody* thought of you." But Grandma was getting old now and slept early. So every night, the little tanuki crept out alone to keep the warm-can magic going. All by itself. In secret. Because it was shy, and a tiny bit scared of being seen.

The friends were quiet for a moment. Even Pock stopped fizzing.

"You've been doing the kindest thing in all of Tokyo," said Maru softly, "and carrying it alone." She knelt down. "But kindness is like tea, little one. It's good warm. It's *better* shared."

Tama was already untying her bundle. "I knew there was a reason I packed three pairs of mittens!" she beamed, and slipped one tiny pair onto the tanuki's cold little paws. They fit perfectly. "Now your hands won't get chilly on the midnight rounds."

"And you don't have to do it alone anymore," said Momo, gathering everyone in. "Squish together, stay together!"

---

So that became the new way of things.

Every midnight, the little tanuki — whose name, it turned out, was Pon — met four glowing friends at the end of the vending-machine row. Tama brought the warm cans, baked fresh and snug. Momo carried Pon on her squishy shoulders so its paws could rest. Maru painted the gold stars slow and steady, one careful stroke each. And Pock?

Pock told *everyone.* Not the location — that stayed a soft secret, the way the best kindnesses do. But he zipped all over the city leaving bubble-trails and whispering to tired travelers, "If you walk home cold tonight… check the warm machines. Somebody thought of you. *Pop!*"

And little by little, people started leaving things back. A folded paper crane. A drawing. A note that just said *thank you, whoever you are.* Pon kept every single one in a shoebox, and on hard nights, looked through them, and felt — not alone at all.

"You know what's the most *sugoi* part?" Pock said one night, floating happily over the glowing machine, his freckles fizzing gold in its warm light. "I found a secret. And the second I gave it away…" He did a little loop in the air. "…it got a hundred times bigger and *way* more bubbly."

"That," said Maru, painting one last perfect star, "is how the best mysteries work."

And far down the quiet Tokyo street, a cold and tired someone reached into a tray, lifted out a warm can with a gold star on the lid, and smiled in the dark — feeling, for one cozy midnight moment, completely and wonderfully *thought of.*
