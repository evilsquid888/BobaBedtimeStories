# Story 65: Coro Korokke and the Hokkaido Potato Fields

**Main Character:** Coro the Korokke
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** Rolling golden potato fields under a wide Hokkaido sky
**Theme:** Knowing where you come from helps you grow
**Japanese Spotlight:** jagaimo (jah-gah-ee-moh) — potato

---

Coro the Korokke had a small, crispy worry, and it wobbled around inside him like a loose crumb.

It happened at the night market, right in the middle of a perfectly ordinary Tuesday. A glittery cupcake had floated past on tiny sugar wings, a towering parfait had told a story about the time it met a movie star, and a very fancy éclair had introduced itself as "Lord Crackleworth the Third, of the Famous Pastry Cracklewroths."

And little Coro — crispy golden outside, soft mashed-potato fluff inside — had felt a nervous puff of steam escape his middle. *Pfffft.*

"And what are *you*?" Lord Crackleworth had asked, peering down his sugary nose.

"I'm... I'm just a korokke," Coro had said. "Crispy outside, soft heart inside. I'm mostly... potato."

"*Just* potato?" the éclair had sniffed. "How charmingly *humble*."

And that little word — *just* — had been bouncing around inside Coro ever since.

So that very night, Coro decided to do something brave. He would go and find out where potatoes came from. He would find out where *he* came from.

He packed a tiny scarf, a map, and a snack (for emergencies), and he set off north — all the way to the wide green-and-gold island of Hokkaido.

The train ride took forever, which was wonderful, because Coro was a little nervous and forever gave him plenty of time to nibble his emergency snack. By the time he stepped off, the air had changed. It smelled clean and cool and faintly sweet, like a kitchen just before something delicious comes out of the oven.

And then Coro saw them.

Fields. *Endless* fields, rolling up and over the hills in long golden-green stripes, all the way to a sky so wide and so blue that Coro had to tip his whole crispy self backward just to see the top of it.

"Oh my," he whispered, and a happy little puff of steam slipped out. *Pfft.*

"Bless you," said a voice from the dirt.

Coro nearly jumped out of his breadcrumbs. He looked down. There, poking up between two leafy green plants, was a round, lumpy, earthy little face with two dark eyes and a smudge of soil on its cheek.

"I'm a **jagaimo** (jah-gah-ee-moh) — potato," said the little face cheerfully. "Most folks call me Spud. And you, my crispy friend, look an *awful* lot like family."

"Family?" said Coro.

"Sure!" Spud wriggled the rest of the way out of the soil with a soft *fwump* and stood there, round and proud and covered in dirt. "Every korokke starts as a jagaimo. We're the soft fluffy middle. The heart of the matter, you might say. So — welcome home!"

Coro's crispy outside felt suddenly warm all over. *Home.* Nobody had ever said that word to him before.

"Come on," said Spud, brushing soil off his lumpy shoulders. "Let me show you around. There's more to being a potato than people think."

---

Spud led Coro down a path between the rows, and oh, the *jagaimo* were everywhere — peeking out of the earth, snoozing in the cool dark soil, stretching their little leafy arms up toward the sun. They waved as Coro passed. Far off over the hills, a gray smudge of cloud sat low against the bright blue sky, and Spud eyed it warily for a moment, the way farmers do, before walking on.

"Welcome home, cousin!" they called. "We've been waiting!"

Coro had never been waved at by so many vegetables in his life. It made his soft middle feel fluffier than ever.

"You see," said Spud, "we potatoes are simple. We're not fancy. We don't sparkle or float or wear capes." He patted his own lumpy tummy. "But here's the secret nobody tells you: simple is *strong*."

"Strong?" said Coro.

"Watch this." Spud led him to the edge of a field where the wind came whistling cold off the far mountains. The leafy potato plants didn't sparkle, and they didn't show off — but they didn't blow over, either. They just held on, roots deep in the good dark earth, swaying and steady.

"A jagaimo can grow almost anywhere," said Spud. "Cold soil, hard soil, lonely hillsides where nothing fancy would dare. We grow when the weather is gentle and we grow when the weather is grumpy. We feed whole families through whole winters. That's not *just* anything, friend. That's the most important job there is."

A nearby potato — a plump grandmotherly one with three soil-smudges and a leaf tucked behind where her ear might be — nodded so hard she nearly tipped over. "When the big cold storms come," she said, "the fancy crops all faint and the sweet little berries shiver right off their vines. But us? We just dig in deeper and keep on going. *Somebody's* got to be supper."

"And we never complain about it," added a small round potato proudly. "Well. *Almost* never. I once complained that a worm tickled my left side, but that's different."

"That is *entirely* different," agreed Spud solemnly, and the whole row of potatoes giggled their soft hiccuping giggles. *Hic-ha, hic-ha!*

Coro thought about Lord Crackleworth, who would surely melt into a puddle the moment a single cold breeze touched him.

"What's so funny?" asked Spud, because Coro had started to giggle.

"Oh — nothing," said Coro. "I just met an éclair who would not last five seconds out here."

Spud roared with laughter, which for a potato sounds a bit like a happy little hiccup. "*Hic-HA!* No, no, the fancy ones never do. But you know who does? *You.* You're made of us. Crispy on the outside, sure — but soft, warm, and steady all the way through."

They walked on, and Spud showed Coro the whole secret world of the fields. He showed him the **daichi** (dah-ee-chee) — great earth, the deep cool soil where every jagaimo gets its start, dark and quiet and full of good things. He showed him the morning **kiri** (kee-ree) — mist that rolled in soft and silver and tucked the whole valley under a blanket. And he showed him the **shukaku** (shoo-kah-koo) — harvest baskets, woven and round, waiting at the edge of the field for the day the potatoes were ready.

Coro liked the harvest baskets best of all. They were warm and round and smelled like good clean earth, and the morning sun made them glow like little nests woven out of gold.

"When a jagaimo is grown and ready and proud," said Spud softly, "it gets gently lifted up, washed warm, and turned into something that makes a whole table happy. Stew. Soup. Croquettes." He winked. "Like you."

Coro thought about all the times someone had walked up to his little stall on a cold dark evening, cupped him in two chilly hands, and gone *mmmm* at the first warm crunchy bite. He thought about the way their shoulders unbunched and their faces softened, the way even the grumpiest customer turned gentle once they were full and warm.

"You know," Coro said slowly, "I think I've been doing the most important job in the world this whole time. And I didn't even know it."

"That," said Spud, "is the wonderful thing about roots. They're working hard down in the dark long before you ever notice them up in the light."

Coro felt the warm feeling swell up bigger than ever. "Spud," he said slowly, "when you put it that way... becoming a korokke sounds like the *best* thing a potato could ever do."

"It is!" cried Spud, and several nearby potatoes cheered. "It's our shining moment! We spend all spring and summer growing soft and golden and full of good fluffy goodness — and then we get to be the cozy crispy thing that makes someone smile on a cold night. That's not a sad ending. That's a *celebration!* It's like a firework that grows underground all year just to finally get to light up the sky."

Coro had never thought of it that way. He'd always felt small. Plain. *Just* potato.

But standing here, in the wide gold fields under the enormous Hokkaido sky, surrounded by hundreds of cheerful cousins who thought he was the most exciting thing to happen all season — Coro suddenly didn't feel small at all.

He felt *rooted.*

---

But just then, the cool wind picked up, and a great gray cloud came tumbling over the mountains. Fat raindrops began to plop down — *plip, plop, plip* — and all the little jagaimo squealed.

"The baskets!" cried Spud. "The harvest baskets are out in the open! If they get soaked, the whole day's work is ruined!"

The baskets sat at the far edge of the field, too heavy for any little potato to drag. The fancy desserts back home would have wrung their sugary hands and fainted dramatically into a puddle.

But Coro the Korokke did not faint.

Coro the Korokke was crispy on the outside — and *crispy doesn't mind a little weather.*

"Roll!" he shouted. "Everybody, climb into the baskets — I'll roll them to the barn!"

The potatoes tumbled in by the dozen, giggling, while Coro got behind the first heavy basket and *shoved.* His crispy crust gave him grip; his round shape gave him roll; and his warm steady heart gave him the puff of steam he needed to push. *Pfft-pfft-PFFT!*

One basket, two baskets, three — Coro rolled them all the way to the warm dry barn, his golden breadcrumbs glistening with rain, never once complaining. By the time the last basket was safe, Coro was a little damp and a lot proud, and every single jagaimo in Hokkaido was cheering his name.

"CO-RO! CO-RO! CO-RO!"

Spud climbed up beside him, dripping and beaming. "See?" he said. "A fancy pastry would've melted. But a korokke? A korokke shows up, stays warm, and gets the job done." He bumped Coro gently, the way good cousins do. "*That's* who you come from. And knowing it — well. It helps you stand a little taller, doesn't it?"

It did. Coro felt it right down to his fluffy middle.

---

That night, the rain cleared, and the whole valley came out to watch the stars wheel over the wide dark fields. The jagaimo snuggled down into their warm soil, and Spud and Coro sat together at the edge of the world, sharing the last of Coro's emergency snack.

"You don't have to stay simple to be small," Spud said sleepily. "You can be simple and be *everything.* The whole island runs on us humble little potatoes."

Coro looked up at the enormous sky and the endless gold fields glowing faintly under the moon, and he finally understood. He wasn't *just* a potato. He was made of the great earth and the silver mist and the steady wind and a whole family of brave little jagaimo who fed the world and never once needed a cape to do it.

When Coro rode the long train home, he wasn't nervous anymore. And the next time Lord Crackleworth peered down his sugary nose and asked, "And what are *you?*" — Coro stood up straight, crispy and warm and proud as a harvest moon.

"I'm Coro the Korokke," he said. "Crispy outside, soft heart inside. I come from the wide gold fields of Hokkaido, where the strongest, warmest, coziest things grow." He smiled. "I'm a jagaimo. And there is *nothing* in the world more wonderful than that."

And somewhere far to the north, under a sky full of stars, a whole field of little potatoes felt their cousin's pride, and snuggled a little deeper into the good dark earth, smiling in their sleep.
