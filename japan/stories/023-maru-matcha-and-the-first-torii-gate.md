# Story 23: Maru Matcha and the First Torii Gate

**Main Character:** Maru Matcha
**Guest Characters:** Niko Nigiri, Rin Ramen, Kori Kakigori, Dai Daruma, Pock Ramune, and a small white shrine fox
**Setting:** A quiet forest shrine with a single vermilion torii gate
**Theme:** Respect isn't rules — it's how you fix what your noise knocked over
**Japanese Spotlight:** **torii** (toh-ree) — the shrine gate

---

It took the gang exactly eleven seconds to ruin the quietest place in the whole forest.

Maru Matcha counted.

It began at the top of the mossy stone stair, where a single vermilion gate stood among the cedars — a **torii** (toh-ree) — the shrine gate, glowing like warm jam in the green shade. On one side of it lay the busy world. On the other side lay a little shrine, and a hush so soft you could have tucked it in.

Maru Matcha had planned to explain all of that. She did not get the chance.

"LAST ONE THROUGH IS A SOGGY NOODLE!" hollered Pock Ramune, and he rocketed up the stairs, bounced off one tree, bounced off a second tree, and somehow arrived at the gate first, second, AND third.

"NOBODY calls me soggy!" boomed Rin Ramen, thundering after him, broth sloshing like a tiny stormy sea.

"COMING THROUGH!" bellowed Dai Daruma, who could not run, exactly, but could roll like a small crimson boulder with eyebrows. Tucked under his arm was his most precious thing in the world: an **ema** (EH-mah) — a small wooden wish tablet, his biggest wish painted on it in wobbly brushstrokes. Dai had carried that tablet for three days. He slept hugging it. He had, reportedly, given it a bedtime story.

He had come all this way to hang it at this very shrine.

The gang hit the torii like a parade falling down a staircase. Pock fizzed through sideways. Rin sloshed through backwards. Kori Kakigori sashayed through announcing, "The forest may now ADMIRE me," and Dai Daruma tripped on the last stone step, went airborne, and landed flat on his round red tummy with an *OOF* that scattered birds for half a mile.

The ema popped out from under his arm. It skittered across the stones like a flat little frog — *clack, clack, clack* — and stopped at the base of the offering box.

Where, it turned out, someone had been sleeping.

A small white fox — a real one, no bigger than a teapot, with a tail like a paintbrush dipped in snow — shot straight up out of its nap, fur standing out in all directions like it had been struck by polite lightning. It took one look at the invading dessert stampede, did the only sensible thing a startled fox can do, and grabbed the nearest object to protect.

The nearest object was Dai's ema.

"Wait—" said Dai.

The fox bolted. White tail, wooden tablet, gone — flick — into the deep ferns at the back of the shrine grounds.

For a moment nobody spoke.

"We have been here," said Maru Matcha, arriving last and calmest, "for eleven seconds. And we already owe this forest an apology."

---

"MY WISH!" wailed Dai Daruma, rolling upright. "Three days! I carried it through rain! I carried it up a mountain! I carried it past a bakery WITHOUT STOPPING, which was the hardest part — and a fluffy bandit stole it in eleven seconds!"

"To be fair," said Kori, inspecting his syrup, "the fluffy bandit was here first. We're the bandits. Loud, delicious bandits."

"Then we chase it!" Pock fizzed in a circle. "Fox hunt! Pop! I'm fast! I once outran a vending machine!"

"Vending machines don't run," said Niko Nigiri.

"Exactly how I outran it."

"No chasing," said Maru, and although her voice was as soft as steam off a teacup, everyone stopped. She pointed her tiny bamboo whisk at the trembling ferns. "That little fox lives here, and we burst into its home like a thunderstorm with sprinkles. Scared things do not come back for louder things. They come back for gentler things."

"So what do we do?" asked Rin.

Maru turned and looked back at the bright red gate they had crashed through.

"We go back out," she said, "and we come in again. Properly, this time. There is a right way to enter a shrine — a kind way — and every step of it is exactly what that fox needs to see." She tucked her whisk under her arm like a tiny general. "We broke the quiet, friends. Now we are going to fix it."

Niko Nigiri stood up so straight his nori belt creaked. "A correct procedure? With *steps*? In *order*?" He was deeply moved. "This is the greatest emergency of my life."

---

So the whole gang trooped back out under the torii and gathered at the bottom of the stone stair, like actors starting the scene over.

"Step one," said Maru. "At a torii, you stop. You stand still for one whole breath. Then you bow — one small, slow bow — to say: *I know this isn't my house. Thank you for letting me in.*"

"And the fox sees that?" asked Dai doubtfully.

"The fox sees everything," said Maru. "Foxes are basically tiny librarians with tails."

They lined up. They stood still — actually still, which for this group was roughly as natural as a kettle whispering. Pock held his fizz in until his cheeks bulged. Rin's broth settled, ripple by ripple, until it lay flat as a mirror. And together, on Maru's nod, six dessert friends bowed slowly to a wooden gate.

And here is the secret nobody tells you about standing perfectly still: you start to *notice* things.

Mid-bow, sunglasses sliding down his nose, Kori whispered, "Don't anyone move. Behind the stone lantern. There's a tail."

There it was: one snow-white tail tip poking out from behind the old stone lantern near the wash basin, twitching like a question mark.

"That's the trick of a bow," murmured Maru, rising. "It looks like you're lowering your head. Really, you're opening your eyes."

They stepped through the gate slowly, in single file — and the forest did not scatter. One bird even came back.

---

"Step two," whispered Maru, leading them to a small stone basin where cool clear water trickled from a bamboo spout. "Before we go closer, we rinse our hands. Quietly. We arrived dusty and loud. We wash off the dust *and* the loud."

It worked — for surprisingly practical reasons.

When Pock Ramune ladled the cool water over his hands, his fizz — which had been crackling like tiny firecrackers, making the lantern-tail flinch every few seconds — went soft and silent.

He stared at himself in horror and delight. "I'm DECAF," he whispered. "Pop... but inside my heart only."

Kori, meanwhile, looked back the way they'd come and gasped. Dotted across the clean stones was a trail of tiny ruby drips. "I've been leaking strawberry syrup since the stairs," he said, scandalized. "I've turned a sacred forest into a crime scene. A DELICIOUS crime scene, but still." He rinsed until the drips stopped, then dabbed the stones clean behind him with a fern frond and tremendous drama.

And then the loveliest thing happened. The basin's gentle trickle — *plip, plip, plip* — drifted across the courtyard, and the white tail behind the lantern went still. A small white face peeked out. Ears up. Listening.

"It likes that sound," whispered Dai.

"Of course it does," whispered Maru. "That little spout has been this fox's lullaby every night of its life. We just stopped being louder than its favorite song."

---

"Step three," breathed Maru. "Soft steps. The path to the offering box is gravel, and gravel is the world's crunchiest alarm system. Heel first, then toes. Like walking on a sleeping grandpa."

They crept forward in a line — Niko placing each foot with surveyor's precision, Rin holding his broth dead level like the world's most serious soup waiter, Kori tiptoeing with arms flung out in silent drama, and Pock drifting along on his own held breath.

Step by soft step, the fox let them come. Five paces. Four. It stood in the open now beside the offering box, Dai's ema still held gently in its mouth, watching them with eyes like two drops of black tea. Three paces. Its tail gave one slow, hopeful wag.

And Dai Daruma — whose wish was *right there*, who had been so good for so many minutes — felt all his bottled-up pep reach the very top of the bottle.

"FALL DOWN SEVEN—" he announced, at full coach-volume.

"*DAI!*" hissed five desserts at once.

The fox shot three feet straight up, came down running, and vanished behind the shrine with the ema — flick — gone again.

In the ringing silence, Dai cleared his throat. "...Bounce up eight," he finished, very, very quietly. "I un-bounce my words. I'm sorry. It's just — encouragement *builds up* in me. I'm like a teapot, except instead of whistling I do motivational shouting."

"Then it's time for step four," said Maru, taking his hand. "Because step four is the gentlest noise in the whole world — and this time, Dai, *you're* going to make it."

---

By the offering box hung a thick braided rope, and at its top, a round brass bell.

"At a shrine," whispered Maru, "you may ring the bell — once, softly. It isn't a doorbell. It's a hello. The shrine keeper rang it every evening at dusk; if our little friend has a favorite sound in all the world, it's this one." She placed the rope in Dai's hands. "Loud friends make the best gentle sounds, when they finally get the chance. Slow as honey, now."

Dai Daruma took the rope. He took the deepest breath of his round red life. And the most thunderous, bounciest, shoutiest dessert in all Japan swung the rope as gently as tucking in a baby.

*Bonnnng.*

One single round note rolled out across the shrine — soft and warm and golden — and settled over the courtyard like a quilt.

The ferns parted.

Out stepped the white fox, ears up, tail high, Dai's ema held carefully in its mouth like something precious — because, of course, it was. It trotted across the gravel, straight up to Dai, and set the wish tablet down at his feet. Then it sat back and looked up at him, head tilted.

Dai bowed. Slowly. Properly. He did not tip over, which by his own count made it the first time ever.

The fox bowed back — front paws stretched, tail in the air — which is how foxes bow, and also how they ask to play, and perhaps it meant a little of both.

"Thank you," whispered Dai. "Sorry we stampeded your house."

Then Dai himself hung the ema on the wooden rack beside the offering box, where it swung gently among the other wishes, *clack, clack*, like it was greeting its new neighbors. Rin left a respectful golden corn kernel by the offering box. The fox sniffed it, decided it was acceptable rent, and curled up beneath the rack — right under Dai's wish — with its paintbrush tail over its nose.

---

Dusk came down through the cedars, slow and violet. Along the path, the old stone lanterns held the last warm light like cupped hands, and the vermilion torii glowed softly at the edge of the dark, keeping watch.

The friends sat together on the bottom step, quiet at last — not because anyone had shushed them, but because quiet, it turns out, is something you can learn to like.

"Maru," said Niko softly, "I memorized every step tonight — the bow, the rinsing, the soft walking, the bell. Is that what respect is? Knowing all the rules?"

Maru Matcha looked at the little white fox asleep beneath Dai's wish, in the home they had frightened and then made peaceful again.

"No," she said. "Respect isn't rules. It's how you fix what your noise knocked over."

"Then we fixed it," yawned Pock, fizzing one slow, sleepy bubble. "Pop. Inside my heart only."

When it was time to go, they rose, walked under the torii, turned around together, and bowed one last small bow to the lantern-lit hush — and Dai's bow was the slowest and gentlest of all.

The bell, though no one touched it, seemed to hum a tiny note goodbye.

And the gang walked home down the darkening path, soft-stepped and warm-hearted, so quietly that the forest never even noticed they'd gone.

## Goodnight Blessing
May you pass through gentle doorways tonight, little one, and may you always know how to mend a quiet you've accidentally broken — with a bow, with soft steps, with the gentlest hello. If your day was loud, let it hush now, the way a bell-note settles over a sleepy shrine. Somewhere a small white fox is curled beneath a hanging wish, and everything that was startled has come safely home. Heel first, then toes, into your dreams. Goodnight.
