# Story 23: Maru Matcha and the First Torii Gate

**Main Character:** Maru Matcha
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A quiet forest shrine with a single vermilion torii gate
**Theme:** Some doorways are for slowing down and saying thanks
**Japanese Spotlight:** とりい (torii) = shrine gate

---

It is a curious fact, dear reader, that the noisiest day a group of dessert friends ever has is usually the day they decide to go somewhere quiet.

This particular day began with Pock Ramune fizzing in seven directions at once, Kori Kakigori shouting that the morning sun was "SO last season," and Tako & Yaki rolling downhill in a giggling blur while finishing each other's sentences mid-tumble.

"There!" Maru Matcha said, pointing her tiny bamboo whisk at a leafy path. "A forest. The map says there is something special at the end of it."

"Special like SNACKS?" puffed Rin Ramen, already steaming with excitement.

"Special like quiet," said Maru, her frothy matcha-cream swirl wobbling gently.

The whole gang stopped. Quiet was, frankly, the one flavor none of them had ever tried. And so, grumbling and giggling and tripping over their own crumbs, they followed the calm little green friend into the trees — straight toward the most special doorway any dessert had ever seen.

The forest path was soft with old leaves, and the light came down in slow golden ribbons. Mero Melonpan immediately found a sunbeam and tried to nap standing up. Dai Daruma kept rolling ahead and rolling back, shouting, "Faster, team! Adventure waits for nobody! Fall down seven, bounce up eight!"

"Daruma," called Tama Onigiri kindly, "you've rolled past us four times now. You're doing more walking than all of us put together."

"I'm SCOUTING," puffed Dai Daruma, who was a little out of breath. "Somebody has to make sure the adventure is adventurous!"

"It's a forest," said Kori. "It's mostly trees being trees."

But it was, in fact, a lovely forest. Ferns curled like little green question marks. A stream somewhere nearby hummed a tune with no words. And the deeper they went, the quieter it became — not an empty quiet, but a full one, the kind that seems to be holding something gently in its hands.

But Maru Matcha did not hurry. She never did. She walked the way warm tea pours — smooth, unbothered, taking exactly as long as it needed to take.

"Maru," whined Kori, melting just slightly with impatience, "what IS this special thing? A treasure? A spa? Please tell me it's a spa."

"You'll know it," said Maru, "when you feel like being a little bit smaller."

"Smaller?" gasped Tako.
"We're already pretty small!" finished Yaki.

Maru only smiled her calm half-moon smile.

Then the trees opened up — and there it stood.

At the top of a gentle stone stair sat a single gate. Two tall vermilion posts, bright as red-bean jam, holding up two graceful curved beams across the top. It was not a door. It had no walls. You could simply walk around it if you wanted to. And yet it felt, somehow, like the most important thing in the whole forest.

"It's a **とりい** (torii) = shrine gate," said Maru softly. "It marks the doorway to a quiet, special place. On one side, the busy world. On the other side... a little hush."

Pock Ramune zipped right up to it. "Oh! A gate with no door — pop! — that means we can just GO, go, go!" And he fizzed toward the opening at top speed.

"Wait," said Maru — gently, but the word had a soft weight to it, like the lid on a warm pot.

Pock skidded. "Wait? But it's open!"

"It is open," agreed Maru. "Which is exactly why we slow down." She tucked her little whisk under one arm. "At a torii, friends, we don't rush through. We pause. We stand still for one breath. And we give a small bow — like saying thank you to the quiet for letting us in."

The friends blinked at her.

"A bow?" said Rin. "Like... before we eat?"

"A little like that," said Maru. "When you bow before you eat, you say thank you for the meal. Here, you say thank you for the calm. Watch."

And the calmest dessert in all the land stepped to the front of the group. She straightened her small round body. She breathed in slow — Maru always breathed like the kettle was just about to whistle but politely waiting its turn — and then she folded forward into a gentle, graceful bow.

For one whole heartbeat, the forest went perfectly still.

Even the birds seemed to wait.

Then Maru rose, gave a tiny nod, and stepped under the torii. And — well. It's hard to explain unless you've felt it. But the moment she passed beneath that bright red gate, the whole shrine seemed to glow just a little softer, like someone had turned the world's volume down to its coziest setting.

"Ohhh," whispered Mero Melonpan, suddenly very awake. "Crackle on the outside, dreams on the inside. This place feels like the inside of a dream."

"My turn! My turn!" said Dai Daruma, rolling forward. But because he was Dai Daruma, and Dai Daruma does everything with enormous energy, his bow was so deep and so proud and so VIGOROUS that he tipped right over and rolled in a little circle. "FALL DOWN SEVEN—" he announced from the ground, "—BOUNCE UP EIGHT!" And he righted himself, beaming.

The friends burst out laughing.

Maru pressed her whisk gently to her cheek, hiding the tiniest deadpan smile. "A bow with extra rolling," she said. "Very respectful. The gate is impressed."

One by one, the friends came to the torii — and one by one, the magic of slowing down caught them.

Niko Nigiri stood very straight, adjusted his neat little nori belt, and gave a bow so tidy and precise that you could have measured it with a ruler. "Let's do this neatly," he said, "...or deliciously." Then he paused. "Actually — neatly. Some places are for neatly."

Tama Onigiri bowed and, of course, pulled three extra rice crumbs from somewhere about his person. "I packed a little extra thank-you," he whispered to the gate, "just in case."

Rin Ramen bowed so warmly that a happy puff of steam rose off his broth and curled up into the trees like a little cloud saying hello. "Everything's better warm and bubbly," he sighed, "even saying thanks."

But the two who surprised everyone most were Tako & Yaki.

The giggly takoyaki twins were not famous for slowing down. They bounced. They tumbled. They high-fived in mid-air. Stillness was, for them, basically a foreign language.

They rolled up to the gate together — and stopped.

They looked at the bright red posts.
They looked at each other.
And then, very seriously, for the first time in their entire bouncy lives, they bowed at the exact same moment, perfectly together, without a single giggle.

"...Whoa," said Tako.
"...Yeah," said Yaki.
"It feels—" started Tako.
"—really nice," finished Yaki.

And then, because they were still Tako & Yaki, they added together: "Roll us once — we come as two — but we'll BOW as two as well!"

Only Kori Kakigori remained.

The shaved-ice diva stood at the foot of the torii with her sunglasses on, tapping her syrupy foot. "Okay, okay," she said. "I'll do the little bow thing. But everyone watch, because when I do something, I do it FABULOUSLY—"

"Kori," said Maru softly.

"What?"

"A torii bow isn't about being fabulous." Maru's voice was as gentle as cream melting into tea. "It's the one place where you get to stop performing. Where nobody's watching. Where you can just... be a little quiet, and a little grateful, and that's enough."

Kori paused. She reached up — and for the first time anyone could remember — she took off her sunglasses.

Without them, her eyes were soft, and a tiny bit shiny.

She breathed in. She breathed out. And she gave a slow, simple, un-fabulous, completely sincere bow.

When she rose, a single ruby drip of strawberry syrup rolled down her cheek like a happy little tear. "Huh," she said quietly. "That actually felt... the coolest. Not loud-cool. Just... cool like a quiet afternoon." She blinked. "Don't tell anyone I said something nice."

"Wouldn't dream of it," said Maru, deadpan, while everyone giggled.

Now the whole gang stood together on the quiet side of the torii, in the soft hush of the little shrine. The air smelled of cedar and old rain. The vermilion gate glowed warm behind them. And something had changed — not in the shrine, but in the friends themselves. They felt gathered. Settled. Like a cup of tea that had finally stopped swirling.

"Maru," said Niko, after a long, comfortable silence, "I think I understand now. Some doorways are for racing through. But this one... this one is for slowing down. For saying thank you."

"Yes," said Maru. "Some doors are for going somewhere. And some doors are just for being grateful you arrived."

They explored the little shrine together — quietly now, the way you tiptoe in a room where someone is having a lovely dream. They found a small stone basin of cool water, a tiny bell with a soft braided rope, and a wooden box where, Maru explained, visitors leave small wishes.

At the water basin, Maru showed them how to dip a little wooden ladle and rinse their hands — a small washing-up before stepping closer to the calm. "We tidy ourselves a little," she said, "the way you smooth a blanket before you climb in."

Niko Nigiri loved this part enormously. "Washing up properly before entering? This is my favorite custom in the whole world," he said, rinsing his hands with great care. "It's so... orderly. So thank-you-ish."

Then Maru showed them the bell. "You can give the rope a gentle shake," she said. "Soft, now. It's not a doorbell — it's more like a hello." She swung it, and a single round note rolled out across the shrine — *bonnng* — so soft and warm that it seemed to settle over them like a quilt. Pock Ramune, who normally found it physically impossible to be still, hovered with his fizz held in, just listening.

"That's the quietest sound I've ever loved," Pock whispered. "Pop... but softly."

At the wishing box, each friend left a small wish in their own way. Dai Daruma wished to bounce even higher. Mero Melonpan wished for a longer nap, and very nearly took one on the spot. Sora the Soft-Cream, calm as a snowfield, wished simply for "more slow days like this one." Tama Onigiri wished for snacks for everyone, which, frankly, was very on-brand. And Maru Matcha closed her half-moon eyes and made the smallest wish of all — that her friends would remember, on their loud and busy days, how good it felt to slow down just once.

And when at last it was time to leave, Maru gathered them at the torii once more.

"There's one more part of the custom," she said. "When you go back out — when you return to the busy world — you turn around at the gate, and you bow once more. To say thank you for the visit."

So they did. All of them, together, turned at the bright red torii and bowed — Niko neat, Daruma rolling, Tako & Yaki in perfect twin-time, Kori sincere with her sunglasses still off, Rin steaming softly, and little Maru Matcha in the middle, calm as a still green pond.

And the shrine, behind them, seemed to glow one last gentle glow — like a friend waving goodbye from a warm window.

"Whisk slow," said Maru as they walked back into the golden trees. "Feel calm."

"Whisk slow," the friends repeated softly. "Feel calm."

And do you know — they walked the whole way home quietly, and not one of them minded a bit.

## Goodnight Blessing
May your day have one quiet doorway in it, little one — a soft place where you stop, and breathe, and remember to say thank you. May your busy thoughts settle like leaves drifting down a forest path, slow and golden and unhurried. May you feel gathered and warm, the way friends feel when they stand together in a hush. Whisk slow, sweet one. Feel calm. Goodnight.
