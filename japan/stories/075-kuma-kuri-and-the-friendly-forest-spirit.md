# Story 75: Kuma Kuri and the Friendly Forest Spirit

**Main Character:** Kuma the Kuri
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A mossy forest shrine where a small, cuddly forest spirit lives
**Theme:** Kindness makes even shy spirits smile
**Japanese Spotlight:** **mori** (moh-ree) — forest

---

Now, between you and me, Kuma the Kuri was the shyest chestnut in the whole wide world — and possibly several worlds beyond that, because shyness like Kuma's tends to travel.

He was a glossy little chestnut, smooth and warm and the color of polished mahogany, with a fuzzy cap perched on his head like a tiny brown beret that had wandered off and refused to come home. Around his neck he wore an autumn-leaf scarf, crinkly and red-gold, and when he got nervous — which was roughly always — he tugged on it and went *very* quiet.

This particular evening, Kuma had wandered deep, deep into the **mori** (moh-ree) — the forest, following a trail of fallen acorns because he liked counting them. (He was up to two hundred and eleven.) The trees grew tall and mossy, the light went soft and green, and somewhere ahead, a tiny bell went *ting*.

"Oh dear," whispered Kuma. He had never been this deep before. But the bell went *ting* again — and it sounded, well... lonely.

---

Kuma did not like lonely sounds. They made his little roasted heart squeeze.

So, tugging his scarf for courage, he waddled toward the *ting*. Round and sturdy as he was, Kuma waddled the way a brave acorn-knight might march into battle — if that knight had to stop every few steps to make sure nobody was watching.

The trail opened into a clearing, and there sat the smallest shrine Kuma had ever seen. It was made of weathered grey wood, no taller than Kuma himself, with a mossy little roof and a bell that hung from a faded red cord. Green velvet moss carpeted everything, soft as a sleeping cat. Fireflies blinked lazily in the dusk.

And there, half-hidden behind the bell cord, was something *fluffy*.

It was round and white and impossibly soft-looking, like a dandelion puff that had decided to grow ears. It had two enormous dark eyes — gentle and round and a tiny bit watery — and two stubby leaf-shaped arms. The moment it saw Kuma, it gave a startled squeak and dove behind the shrine, leaving only one wobbly ear poking out.

"H-hello?" said Kuma. Then he panicked at his own boldness and ducked behind a mushroom.

For a long moment, the forest was very quiet.

Then, slowly, the fluffy thing peeked out from behind the shrine. And slowly, slowly, Kuma peeked out from behind his mushroom. They looked at each other. They both immediately hid again.

This happened four more times.

"This," thought Kuma, "is the most exhausting conversation I have ever almost had." His little roasted heart was thumping like a woodpecker. But underneath the thumping was something else — a warm, fizzy hope, the kind you feel when you suspect the scary thing in the dark might actually be friendly. So he counted to ten (twice, to be safe), tightened his scarf, and made up his mind not to run.

Finally, the fluffy thing peeped in the tiniest voice you can imagine — about the size of a dewdrop falling on a leaf: "Are... are you here to make a *wish*?"

Kuma poked his fuzzy cap out from behind the mushroom. "A wish?"

"I'm the shrine spirit," whispered the fluffy thing, twiddling its leaf-hands. "I'm a **kodama** (koh-dah-mah) — a tree spirit. I'm supposed to grant wishes for travelers who ring the bell. Only..." Its big eyes welled up. "Only nobody ever comes. They think the forest is spooky. They run away before they even reach the clearing."

"Oh," said Kuma softly. "That sounds lonely."

"It IS," sniffled the kodama. "I've been practicing my wish-granting for *ages*. Watch — I can make a flower bloom!" It scrunched up its little face, concentrating hard, and waved a leaf-arm with great ceremony.

A single mushroom nearby sneezed and fell over. A nearby acorn rolled three inches to the left. And a beetle, who had been minding his own business, suddenly found himself wearing a tiny crown of dew — which was actually quite stylish, though nobody had asked for it.

"...That wasn't supposed to happen," said the kodama miserably. "None of that was supposed to happen. The flower was supposed to *bloom.* That's the whole trick. Bloom, sparkle, traveler says *ooh.* That's how it's meant to go." It flopped down on the moss with a soft *whump.* "I've been practicing for a hundred years and I still can't get the *ooh.*"

Kuma, who knew exactly what it felt like to try your hardest and have a mushroom sneeze on your big moment, waddled out from behind his hiding spot entirely. "I thought it was lovely," he said. "I've never seen a mushroom do that before."

The kodama blinked. "...You did?"

"Mm-hm." Kuma sat down on the soft moss, a careful chestnut-length away — close enough to be friendly, far enough not to be frightening. "I'm shy too, you know. I'm so shy I once hid inside my own scarf for an entire afternoon."

The kodama gasped, delighted. "An entire AFTERNOON?"

"There was a picnic," Kuma admitted. "And music. It was very loud and very happy and I just... couldn't." He tugged his scarf. "So I understand about being too bashful to come out."

And here is the funny thing about two shy creatures meeting in the woods: each one was so busy worrying about being shy that neither had time left over to be afraid of the other. It is rather like two umbrellas bumping in a doorway — they both apologize so much that they end up the best of friends.

"My name is Kuma," said Kuma. "Kuma the Kuri."

"I'm... I don't have a name," said the kodama, scuffing one leaf-foot. "Nobody's stayed long enough to give me one."

This was, frankly, the saddest thing Kuma had heard all week, and he decided right then and there to fix it.

"Hmm," he said. "You're round, and soft, and you live in the trees, and you glow a little when the fireflies come close..." He thought hard. "What about **Mori**? Like the forest. Because you're the heart of it."

The kodama — *Mori* — went absolutely pink with joy. Which is a very strange thing for a white fluffy creature to do, but there it is. "Mori," it whispered, trying the name on like a cozy hat. "*I have a name.* Kuma, I have a NAME!"

And Mori was so excited that it bounced — a great fluffy bounce straight up into the air — and accidentally rang the bell with its head. *TING!*

Both of them froze.

"...Did you just grant a wish?" asked Kuma.

"I think *I* was the wish," giggled Mori, and that giggle was the first laugh that little clearing had heard in a hundred years. The moss seemed to perk up. The fireflies glowed brighter. Even the sneezing mushroom righted itself, as if to say, *well, finally.*

---

Now, the two of them might have spent the whole night being shy at each other from opposite ends of the moss — but Kuma had a better idea, and brave ideas, he was learning, get easier the more friends you have.

"Mori," he said, "I don't think you grant wishes wrong. I think you've just been practicing the *hard* magic. Let's try the easy kind."

"There's an *easy* kind?"

"The easiest magic in the mori there is," said Kuma. "Being a good friend to whoever comes by. That's a wish-come-true all on its own."

Mori looked doubtful. So Kuma showed it. He gathered a little pile of the acorns he'd been counting and arranged them into a cozy ring, like seats around a campfire. He unwound his autumn-leaf scarf and laid it over the cold stone of the shrine step so Mori would have somewhere soft to perch. Then he gathered a handful of glowing **hotaru** (hoh-tah-roo) — fireflies in his cupped paws — gently, gently — and let them drift up like tiny lanterns, until the whole clearing twinkled.

"Oh," breathed Mori. "*Oh.* It's so cozy."

"That's the magic," said Kuma, glowing his warm roasted-chestnut glow. "You make a place where someone feels safe. That's the best wish you can grant anybody."

Mori looked around the twinkling clearing — at the acorn campfire-ring, the soft scarf, the floating lanterns of firefly light — and something seemed to unknot inside its fluffy chest. "All this time," it whispered, "I thought wish-granting meant doing something *big* and *shiny.* I thought that was why everyone ran away — because I wasn't shiny enough yet."

"Nobody ran away because of you," said Kuma gently. "They ran away because they were a little scared of the dark, and they never gave the dark a chance to show them how cozy it can be." He patted the moss beside him. "Same way I almost ran from you. And look what I'd have missed."

Mori's big eyes shimmered. And then it tried its OWN bit of magic — not the showy mushroom kind, but the quiet kind. It waved both leaf-arms softly, and the moss beneath them grew warm, the way moss does when the sun has been sitting on it all day. A gentle, drowsy heat, perfect for two sleepy friends.

"I did it," whispered Mori. "I made it *warm.*"

"Small and roasty," said Kuma, beaming. "Ready and toasty."

They sat together in the firefly light — one fuzzy chestnut, one fluffy spirit — and they didn't say much, because shy friends don't need to. The not-talking was warm and full, like a quilt. Every now and then Mori would bounce just a little and ring the bell, *ting*, and they'd both laugh, and it didn't startle them anymore.

"You know what's funny," said Mori after a while, in its dewdrop voice. "I spent a whole hundred years waiting for somebody brave to come along. And then the bravest thing that ever happened was the *shy* fellow who decided not to run." It leaned its fluffy head against Kuma's fuzzy cap. "I think shy and brave might be the same thing, sometimes. Just brave that takes its time."

Kuma considered this. It was, he thought, the wisest thing a sneezing-mushroom spirit had ever said. "I think you might be right," he agreed. "And I think you grant excellent wishes after all."

"Will you visit again?" asked Mori, when the moon climbed high.

"Every autumn," promised Kuma. "And I'll tell the others the forest isn't spooky at all. I'll tell them there's the kindest spirit in the world here, and its name is Mori, and it gives the very best wish of all — the wish of *come sit, you're welcome here.*"

Mori hugged its leaf-arms around itself, too happy to speak. So instead it rang the bell one more time — *ting!* — a little bell of pure joy ringing out across the sleeping mori, telling every tree and every firefly that, at long last, the shrine spirit had a friend.

And two shy creatures, who had each been certain they were too bashful for friendship, discovered the loveliest secret in the woods: that being bashful *together* is not lonely at all. It is simply the cozy, quiet beginning of something sweet.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, somewhere in a soft green mori, a fuzzy chestnut and a fluffy spirit are curled up on warm moss, watching the fireflies blink themselves to sleep. May your heart be brave enough to say one shy hello, and may that hello bloom into a friend who feels like home. You don't have to be loud to be loved — the quiet kindnesses count the very most. Close your eyes now, snug as a chestnut in its cap. The forest is humming you a lullaby. Goodnight.
