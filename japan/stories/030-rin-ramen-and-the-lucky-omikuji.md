# Story 30: Rin Ramen and the Lucky Omikuji

**Main Character:** Rin Ramen
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A hilltop shrine where you draw paper fortunes
**Theme:** You make your own good fortune by being kind
**Japanese Spotlight:** おみくじ (omikuji) = paper fortune

---

Rin Ramen was so excited that he was steaming. Literally. Little puffs of golden mist curled off his broth as he wobbled up the long stone steps toward the hilltop shrine, his stubby legs going *putt-putt-putt* and his noodle-curl hair bouncing with every hop.

"Today," Rin announced to absolutely everyone, "I am going to draw the LUCKIEST fortune anyone has EVER drawn!"

His soft-boiled-egg cheek jiggled. His single corn freckle gleamed. A drop of broth sloshed over his ceramic rim and went *sploosh* on the step, and Rin gasped as if it were a tiny tragedy.

"Careful, Rin," giggled Maru Matcha, drifting up the stairs beside him with her little bamboo whisk. "Whisk slow. Feel calm. The shrine isn't going anywhere."

"But my LUCK might!" Rin slurped dramatically. "Everything's better warm and bubbly — and a great fortune is the warmest, bubbliest thing of ALL!"

---

The shrine sat at the very top, tucked under a roof of curling tiles and a forest of swishy pine trees. A red **鳥居** (torii) = shrine gate stood at the entrance, tall and proud, and the wind through the pines sounded exactly like someone whispering, *welcome, welcome, welcome.*

Rin's whole bowl of friends had come along. Momo Mochi bounced through the gate first, arms already stretching out for hugs. Tama Onigiri waddled in behind, his nori sash neat, his round shoulders carrying a little cloth bundle. The Dango Three pivoted up the steps like a tiny three-piece carousel — Pinku in front, Shiro fretting, Cha thinking.

"Okay, listen up, listen up," said Pinku, the bold pink dumpling. "How does this fortune thing even work?"

"You shake a box," said Cha, the clever green one, "until a numbered stick falls out. Then you find the drawer with your number, and inside is your **おみくじ** (omikuji) = paper fortune."

"And what if it's a BAD one?" worried Shiro, the shy white dumpling, wobbling so hard he nearly tipped his whole skewer.

"Three on a stick, stuck together for good," the trio chanted, steadying themselves. "We'll be fine!"

At the shrine, a kind old shrine-keeper — a wrinkly, smiling umeboshi plum named Granny Ume — sat behind a wooden counter stacked with fortune boxes. She had a face like a friendly raisin and a voice like warm tea, and she had clearly been smiling so long that the wrinkles had simply decided to stay that way.

"Welcome, little ones," she said. "First, you must do everything in the proper order. Wash your hands at the fountain. Ring the bell. Give a little bow. Make a wish. *Then* — and only then — you may shake for your fortune!"

"In the PROPER order?" gasped Tako. "What if we do the bow before the bell?"

"What if we ring the bell with our FEET?" added Yaki, who only had round batter-ball feet and was very curious about this.

Granny Ume chuckled, a sound like a kettle just before it whistles. "Then you would simply be a silly takoyaki. Which, I suspect, you already are."

Tako and Yaki high-fived. They considered "silly takoyaki" the highest possible compliment.

So they did. They cupped cool water from the stone fountain. They rang the great rope-bell — *ka-LONG!* — which made Momo squeal and Tama jump. They bowed, eyes squeezed shut, wishing their secret wishes.

And then it was time.

---

"Me first! ME FIRST!" Rin steamed, hopping in place. He grabbed a fortune box nearly as big as he was and shook it with his whole heart.

*Rattle-rattle-rattle-CLACK!*

A numbered stick tumbled out: forty-two. Granny Ume pointed a wrinkly finger toward a little wooden drawer. Rin slid it open, plucked out his folded paper omikuji, and unfolded it so fast he almost slurped it by accident.

His broth-eyes went wide. His friends crowded close, holding their breath.

"What's it say, what's it say?" bounced Tako and Yaki, who had rolled in late and were, as always, talking on top of each other.

Rin read aloud, slow and careful.

"It says... **小吉** (shōkichi) = small blessing."

There was a pause.

"...Small?" said Rin, his steam fizzling down to a sad little wisp. "Not great blessing? Not big blessing? Just... *small?*"

Behind him, Maru Matcha read over his shoulder. "It says, 'A so-so day. Be patient. Good things come slowly, like soup on a low flame.'"

"SOUP ON A LOW FLAME?!" Rin's egg-cheek drooped. "I am a ramen bowl! I do NOT simmer slowly, I BUBBLE! This is the worst, most lukewarm fortune in the entire history of fortunes!"

It was not, of course. A small blessing is a perfectly lovely thing. But Rin had wanted a *spectacular* day, and the little paper had handed him an *ordinary* one, and Rin felt all his bubbles go flat at once — *fwump.*

Then everyone else opened theirs.

"GREAT BLESSING!" shrieked Pinku, waving her omikuji like a flag.

"Great blessing too!" beamed Tama.

"Mine says 'wonderful blessing'!" bounced Momo, squishing her own paper with joy.

"Whisk slow," murmured Maru, peeking at hers. "'Calm blessing.' That suits me."

Even Tako and Yaki got "double blessing — comes as two!" which made them high-five so hard they wobbled into a pine tree.

Rin looked down at his small, so-so, low-flame fortune. And for the first time all day, the steam stopped curling off him entirely.

"It's not fair," he mumbled. "Everybody got a wonderful day but me."

---

Now, you and I both know that a paper fortune doesn't actually *decide* your day — it just makes a polite little guess, the way the sky makes a guess about rain. Sometimes it's right. Often it isn't. And it has absolutely no idea what's about to happen, because it is, after all, only a piece of paper. But try telling that to a sad bowl of soup.

Rin trudged over to the **絵馬** (ema) = wishing-plaque rack, where visitors hung little wooden tablets with their hopes painted on them. There were wishes for sunny weather and good grades and one, in very wobbly handwriting, that just said *more snacks please.* Rin read every single one and somehow felt even gloopier. He plopped down beneath the rack with a heavy *gloop,* his broth gone still and quiet.

"What's the point of even *making* a wish," he sighed, "if the paper already says your day is going to be so-so?"

His friends gathered close, and that's when Maru Matcha said the wisest thing of the whole day.

"Rin," she said gently, "a fortune isn't a finished cake. It's just the flour. *You* bake the rest."

Rin blinked. "I... don't follow."

"Then let's *show* him," said Tama Onigiri, and he opened his little cloth bundle. Inside were small bowls — empty ones, the kind you bring on a picnic. "I packed a little extra, just in case. I always do."

"Extra what?" sniffed Rin.

"Extra *room,*" said Tama, smiling. "For your soup."

And here's where the day turned warm again.

Because just then, up the long stone steps, came other shrine visitors — and they all looked tired, and chilly, and a little sad. There was a shivery sparrow with frost on his wings. A pair of lost dumpling-shaped tourists who'd missed their bus. A tiny weeping mochi-baby who had dropped his candy on the steps. And Granny Ume herself, rubbing her achy old plum-knees, who had been standing behind that counter since before sunrise without a single sip of anything warm.

Maru Matcha tilted her head at Rin. "You said it yourself this morning, didn't you?"

Rin sniffled. "...Said what?"

"*Everything's better warm and bubbly.*"

For a long second, Rin stared at his cooling broth. Then — *foomp!* — a single brave bubble rose up through his golden soup and popped at the surface.

And Rin Ramen began, at last, to steam again.

---

"Tama!" he cried, springing up so fast he sloshed. "Bring those bowls! Maru, can your whisk warm things? Dango, you three are my serving carousel! We have a SHRINE to feed!"

What happened next was the coziest commotion the hilltop had ever seen.

Rin ladled steaming golden broth into Tama's picnic bowls. Maru whisked each one until it frothed and glowed, murmuring "warm slow, feel calm" over every cup. The Dango Three spun and delivered — Pinku zooming, Cha steering, Shiro carefully, carefully not spilling a drop. Momo Mochi stretched her taffy arms to wrap each shivering guest in a snug, squishy hug while they sipped. Tako and Yaki bounced around handing out corn freckles like confetti.

The frosty sparrow took one slurp and his frost melted clean away, every feather puffing out warm and fluffy. "Cheep!" he sang, doing a little hop. "That's the best soup on the whole mountain! No — the best soup on the whole *sky!*"

The lost tourists wrapped their hands around their bowls. "We missed our bus," one said, "but honestly? We'd rather be here."

The weeping mochi-baby took a tiny sip, hiccuped, and broke into a gummy, gleaming smile that lit up the whole shrine.

And Granny Ume — dear achy Granny Ume — cradled her bowl like treasure, took one long, slow, grateful sip, and let out the biggest, happiest sigh you ever heard.

"Oh," she breathed, eyes shining. "Oh, little ramen. In sixty years on this hill, no one has ever climbed all this way... just to warm an old plum's heart." She patted his ceramic rim with her wrinkly hand. "*This* is a great blessing. Not on paper. In a bowl."

---

Rin Ramen looked around at the whole steaming, slurping, hugging, giggling hilltop — every guest warm, every face glowing, his friends laughing in the pine-scented evening light — and his broth bubbled up so happy it nearly overflowed.

"I get it now," he said softly. He pulled his little so-so omikuji from where he'd tucked it. "The paper said *small blessing.* But the paper didn't know I had friends. The paper didn't know I had soup." He grinned his big slurpy grin. "A fortune just tells you the weather. *Kindness* is the umbrella, the picnic, AND the sunshine!"

Maru Matcha bumped him gently with her whisk. "You made your own great blessing, Rin. You always could."

Then Rin reached up to the wishing-plaque rack and — with a little help from Cha — tied his small fortune there, right beside all the hopeful ema. Not because it had failed him. But because it had, in its own low-flame way, been exactly right.

Good things really do come slowly... like soup on a warm, low flame, shared with everyone you love.

The friends sat together on the shrine steps as the lanterns flickered on, sipping the last of the broth, watching the first sleepy stars blink awake over the pines.

"Everything's better warm and bubbly," Rin sighed contentedly.

And tonight — finally, truly — it was.

## Goodnight Blessing
May your day be like a warm bowl shared with friends — small or great, it is yours to fill with kindness. May any so-so moment turn cozy the second you reach out a hand. Remember, little one: you are the steam in your own soup, the sunshine behind any cloudy fortune. Snuggle down now, soft and warm, and let your good wishes simmer gently into dreams. Goodnight.
