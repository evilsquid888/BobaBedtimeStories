# Story 30: Rin Ramen and the Lucky Omikuji

**Main Character:** Rin Ramen
**Guest Characters:** Granny Ume, the umeboshi-plum shrine-keeper; Maru Matcha; Tama Onigiri
**Setting:** A hilltop shrine where you draw paper fortunes
**Theme:** You make your own good fortune by being kind
**Japanese Spotlight:** **omikuji** (oh-mee-koo-jee) — paper fortune

---

Rin Ramen was so excited he was steaming. Little puffs of golden mist curled off his broth.

Up the long stone steps he hopped, putt-putt-putt, noodle-hair bouncing, broth sloshing over his rim.

"Today," he announced to the whole mountain, "I draw the LUCKIEST fortune anyone has EVER drawn!"

"Whisk slow. Feel calm," said Maru Matcha, drifting up beside him. "The shrine isn't going anywhere."

"But my LUCK might!" Rin slurped. A splash of broth landed on Maru's head. She blinked.

Behind them waddled Tama Onigiri with a cloth bundle. "I packed a little extra, just in case!"

---

At the top, under the red gate, sat Granny Ume, a wrinkly pickled plum behind boxes of fortunes.

"The proper order, little ones," she said. "Wash. Ring the bell. Bow. Wish. THEN shake."

Rin rang the bell so hard, ka-LONG, that he bounced backward into the fountain. Splash.

"Me first! ME FIRST!" He grabbed a fortune box and shook it with his whole heart.

Rattle-rattle-CLACK. Out flew a stick. Then forty more. Sticks everywhere. One in Maru's whisk.

Granny Ume opened a tiny drawer and unfolded his **omikuji** (oh-mee-koo-jee) — paper fortune. One tiny doodled sprout.

"Small blessing," she said. "A so-so day. Good things come slowly, like soup on a low flame."

"LOW FLAME?" Rin's steam fizzled to a wisp. "I'm a ramen bowl! I do not simmer. I BUBBLE!"

---

"Great blessing!" beamed Tama, waving hers.

Rin plopped down under the rack of wishing plaques with a heavy gloop. His bubbles went flat.

"What's the point of wishing," he sighed, "if the paper already says so-so?"

"A fortune isn't a finished cake," said Maru. "It's just the flour. You bake the rest."

---

Then a cold grey fog came rolling up the mountain and swallowed the steps whole.

From far below came shivery little voices. Lost treat-folk, halfway up, and the lanterns not lit yet.

Granny Ume rubbed her achy plum-knees. "Oh dear. Nobody can see the steps."

Maru whisked at the fog. Fwip. The fog got thicker. "Hm," said Maru. "Not that."

Tama opened her bundle. Little empty picnic bowls. "Extra room," she said. "For soup."

Rin stared at his cooling broth. One brave bubble rose up. Foomp.

"Everything's better warm and bubbly," he said, and began, at last, to steam.

---

Rin steamed so hard the fog around him glowed gold, like a lighthouse made of soup.

Down the steps he hopped, bowl by bowl, sloshing, never once spilling. Nearly. Whoops.

He tipped on the wet edge, teetered over the drop, and Tama's sash yanked him back.

"A little extra," panted Tama, "just in case."

A shivery little bun took one slurp, puffed up warm and fluffy, and its frost melted clean off.

Maru whisked each bowl to a froth. The steam rose up the steps in a glowing golden line.

One by one, the lost treat-folk followed the steam up through the fog, bowls warm in their arms.

---

The fog thinned. The lanterns lit. The whole hilltop was slurping.

Granny Ume cradled the very last bowl like treasure, took one long sip, and sighed the biggest sigh.

"Sixty years on this hill," she said, "and nobody ever climbed it just to warm an old plum."

"THIS is a great blessing," said Granny Ume. "Not on paper. In a bowl."

---

Rin pulled out his small so-so fortune. "The paper didn't know I had soup," he said.

"A fortune just tells you the weather," said Rin. "Kindness is the umbrella AND the sunshine."

With Maru's help he tied the little paper to the rack beside all the hopeful plaques.

The friends sat on the steps as the first sleepy stars blinked awake over the pines.

"Everything's better warm and bubbly," sighed Rin. And tonight, finally, truly, it was.

---
