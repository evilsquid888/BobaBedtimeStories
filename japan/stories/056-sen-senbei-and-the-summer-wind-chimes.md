# Story 56: Sen Senbei and the Summer Wind-Chimes

**Main Character:** Sen the Senbei (a flat golden rice cracker wrapped in seaweed)
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A summer wind-chime (fūrin) festival in the leafy courtyard of a Kyoto temple, hundreds of glass bells hanging from the trees
**Theme:** The small things you make and give away can comfort a stranger you will never meet
**Japanese Spotlight:** fūrin (foo-reen) — glass wind-chime

---

Sen the Senbei was, by his own honest measurement, exactly the size and shape of a coaster. A flat, round, golden rice cracker, toasted crisp at the edges, with a tidy black sash of seaweed wrapped snug around his middle like the world's smallest belt. When the wind blew he did not sway grandly like a willow. He went *flump*, sideways, into a bush.

"You see?" he announced, climbing out of the bush and spitting out a leaf. "This is precisely the problem with being small AND flat. The wind just files you away like a forgotten letter."

It was the first warm evening of summer, and Sen had wibbled and wobbled his way all the way up the mossy stone steps to a famous old temple in Kyoto, because he had heard there was a festival there — and Sen did love a festival, even if he was usually too short to see the good parts.

He squeezed under the great wooden gate, stood on his crispy tiptoes, and gasped so hard he nearly cracked.

---

The whole leafy courtyard was *singing*.

Hung from every branch of every tree were hundreds — no, thousands — of little glass bells, each one trembling in the breeze. They were **fūrin** (foo-reen) — glass wind-chimes — and each had a slip of paper tied beneath it, a little paper tail that caught the wind and made the glass bell go *tin... tin... tinnnn*. The sound was everywhere and nowhere, soft as falling stars, cool as a sip of cold barley tea on a hot day.

"Ohhhh," breathed Sen, and for one whole minute he forgot he was small.

Then he remembered, because a fūrin directly above his head went *TING* very loudly, as if to say, *and what exactly do YOU make, little cracker?*

Sen's crispy shoulders drooped. "Nothing, that's what," he muttered. "Everybody here makes something. The tofu shop makes tofu. The temple makes peace and quiet. These trees make a thousand singing bells." He scuffed a sesame seed with his foot. "I'm just a snack. A flat one. The kind people forget at the bottom of a tin until I go soft and sad."

---

"You are not soft," said a voice like a windowpane clearing its throat. "You look beautifully crisp to me. And crisp things keep their shape, you know. That is not nothing."

Sen spun around — *flump* — and found himself looking at the strangest, loveliest person he had ever seen. She was tall and slim and entirely **see-through**, like a vase full of cool water, with little ribbons of color drifting inside her the way ink drifts in a glass. Where the lantern light touched her she glowed faintly blue, faintly rose, faintly gold.

"I'm Garan," she said, and her voice tinkled. "I'm the glassblower. I made every fūrin in this courtyard." She knelt down — which took a while, being mostly made of glass — until she was eye to eye with Sen. "And I have far too many bells and not nearly enough hands. Would you help me paint some?"

Sen blinked. "*Me?* Paint? On glass? I'd crack the brush. I'd crack *myself*. I'm a rice cracker, I crack at the slightest — "

"I think," said Garan gently, "you are exactly the right size to hold a very small brush very carefully." And she pressed one into his crispy little hand before he could escape into a bush.

---

The brush was tiny. The pot of paint was tinier. And the bare little glass bell that Garan set in front of him was the most fragile, frightening thing Sen had ever met.

"What if I paint it wrong?" he whispered.

"There is no wrong," said Garan, dipping color into the air. "There is only *yours*."

So Sen, holding his breath until his edges nearly toasted, painted one tiny blue dot on the glass. Then another. Then a curving line. A little wobbly, a little crooked — but when the wind nudged the bell and it went *tin*, Sen's three small dots spun around and around and looked, for all the world, like three tiny fish chasing each other in a circle.

"Oh," said Sen, very quietly.

"Now the tail," said Garan, handing him a slip of crisp white paper, the **tanzaku** (tahn-zah-koo) — a little paper strip for writing on, the kind you hang beneath the bell. "Write something on it. A wish, a hello, a kindness. The breeze will read it all summer long."

Sen chewed the end of his brush. "Who's it *for*, though? Who gets this one?"

Garan smiled, and the light moved through her like a smile through water. "That is the secret of these bells, little Sen. At the end of the festival, strangers come and choose one to take home. You will never know who. They will never know you. Someone you will never meet will hang your bell in their window, and on some hot, hard, tired day, your little voice will sing to them — and they will feel, just for a moment, a bit less alone."

---

Sen sat very still, holding the empty paper.

He thought about all the hot, hard, tired days he had ever had. The days he felt too small. The days the wind filed him into a bush. And he thought about what, on a day like that, he would most have wanted somebody — anybody, even a stranger — to lean down and whisper to him.

Slowly, carefully, in his neatest little letters, Sen wrote:

***You are exactly the right size for the things only you can do.***

Then he tied the tail beneath the bell, and the breeze caught it, and the three painted fish spun, and the glass went *tinnnn* — and Sen felt something warm bloom right in the middle of his crispy chest.

"Garan," he said. "Can I make another one?"

Garan's whole glass face lit up like sunrise through a window. "Oh, *please*."

---

So Sen made another. And another. And another after that.

He painted bells with tiny golden stars for someone who might be missing the sky. He painted a fat round moon for someone who couldn't sleep. He painted a single sesame seed, very proudly, because sesame seeds are small and lovely and nobody ever notices them, and he thought *somebody* out there might need to feel small and lovely too.

And on each paper tail, he wrote the comfort he himself had most needed.

***Tomorrow is allowed to be easier than today.***

***Someone, somewhere, is glad you are here.***

***Rest now. The world will keep without you for one night.***

He worked so long and so happily that his seaweed sash came a little unstuck and a little starlight got toasted onto his edges, and he did not even notice, because for the first time in his flat little life Sen was not thinking about his own size at all. He was thinking about a stranger on a hot day, leaning toward a window, hearing a bell.

By the time the moon climbed over the temple roof, Sen had made dozens of fūrin, and the lowest branch of the courtyard's biggest tree sang entirely in *his* voice.

---

And then the strangers came.

They drifted in through the great gate in the cool of the night — all sorts of weary travelers, come to choose one bell each to carry home. Sen, suddenly shy, ducked behind a tree root and *watched*.

He watched a tired old radish choose the moon bell and hold it to his heart. He watched a pair of giggling strawberries pick a fish bell and argue happily over who'd hang it. And then — last of all — he saw a very small, very droopy gyoza dumpling-child come shuffling in, the kind of tired where even your pleats sag.

The little dumpling looked too worn out to choose anything at all. But she wandered beneath the lowest branch, and a breeze came through, and one bell went *tin... tin... tinnnn* — Sen's bell, the one with the single proud sesame seed, with the tail that read *Someone, somewhere, is glad you are here.*

The dumpling stopped. She read the tiny paper. And slowly — so slowly Sen held his breath — a smile spread across her tired little face like warmth across cold dough.

She reached up on her tiptoes, lifted the bell down with both hands as if it were made of moonlight, and hugged it to her chest. Then she shuffled out the gate and into the dark, gone forever, off to a window Sen would never, ever see.

"She'll never know it was me," Sen whispered.

"No," said Garan softly, behind him. "She never will."

"And I'll never know if she's all right." Sen's voice wobbled. "On the hot days. The hard ones."

"No," said Garan again. "You won't." She rested one cool glass hand on his crispy shoulder. "That is the bigness of it, little one. You gave something away with no name on it and no thank-you coming back. Just a kindness, set loose in the wind, to find a stranger and sit with them awhile." The colors drifted gently inside her. "Did you feel too small for that?"

Sen thought about it. He thought about the dumpling's smile, lighting up the dark.

"No," he said, surprised. "No. I felt exactly the right size."

---

High in the trees, a hundred glass bells sang the kindness of a small flat cracker out into the summer night — out toward windows and strangers and tired hearts he would never meet — and Sen the Senbei, crisp and golden and not one bit forgettable, listened to his own little voice slip into the great big song and understood, at last, that it had mattered all along.

The wind blew. Sen did not go *flump* into a bush.

He stood, small and golden and proud, and let it carry his bells away.

## Goodnight Blessing
Hush now, little one, and listen — can you hear it? Somewhere a glass bell is swinging in a dark window, singing softly to someone you will never meet, just because a small flat cracker once decided his kindness was worth giving away. You are that size too, you know — exactly the right size for the small, true things only you can make and give: a drawing, a note, a held hand, a quiet kindness sent off into the world with no name on it. They are never too little. They are some of the biggest things there are. Let the breeze cool your pillow and the faraway bells ring you down, soft and slow, into sleep. *Tin... tin... tinnnn.* Rest now, sweet dreamer. The world will keep without you for one night. Goodnight.
