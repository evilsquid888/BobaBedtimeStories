# Story 59: Dai Daruma and the Calligraphy Brush

**Main Character:** Dai Daruma
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A traditional calligraphy classroom in Kyoto, with ink stones, soft brushes, and crisp white rice paper
**Theme:** Your first try doesn't have to be perfect to be proud
**Japanese Spotlight:** kaku (kah-koo) — to write / draw

---

Dai Daruma rolled into the calligraphy classroom like a little crimson bowling ball wearing a kimono, bounced off a cushion, wobbled left, wobbled right, and finally tipped upright with his big determined eyebrows pointed at the sky.

"Today," he announced to the empty room, "is the day I learn to **kaku** (kah-koo) — to write!"

His voice echoed grandly. Then a paintbrush, startled by all the wobbling, slid off the low wooden table and bonked him gently on the dome.

"Ow! Hello to you too," said Dai, rubbing his round head. The brush was as long as he was, with a swoosh of soft white hair at the tip, and it lay across his lap like a sleepy noodle. "We're going to be best friends, you and I. We are going to write the boldest, neatest, most spectacular character anyone in all of Kyoto has ever seen!"

The brush did not answer. Brushes rarely do. But Dai Daruma did not need answers. He had something better: pep.

The classroom was the coziest room in all of Kyoto, if you asked Dai, and Dai was the only one in it, so you could only ask Dai. It had a floor of soft golden **tatami** (tah-tah-mee) — woven straw mats — low wooden tables polished smooth by a hundred years of careful elbows, and a wall hung with scrolls so perfect they looked like they had been written by the wind itself. Pots of brushes stood in tidy rows like little forests. The whole place smelled of pine smoke and cool stone and something quiet and old, the way a grandmother's hands smell of tea.

"I shall fit right in," said Dai, who came up to about the height of an ink stone and had, at that exact moment, a smear of starch on his nose. "A natural. A prodigy. A master in the making."

---

He set himself up the proper way, because Dai liked doing things proper. He dipped the brush into the ink stone, where a puddle of glossy black **sumi** (soo-mee) — ink — shone like a tiny midnight pond. He smoothed out a fresh sheet of rice paper. He puffed out his crimson cheeks and squared his already-square eyebrows.

"The character I will kaku today," he declared, "is **Chikara** (chee-kah-rah) — strength. Three strokes. Simple. Brave. Just like me!"

He lifted the brush.

He took a steadying breath.

He leaned forward to begin the very first stroke —

— and being round, Dai Daruma did the one thing round things do best.

He rolled.

"Whoa-OH-oh!" The brush flew sideways. A long black streak shot across the paper like a comet with a very bad attitude. Dai tumbled over his own bottom, spun in a full circle, and landed plop on his back, four little limbs in the air, the brush balanced on his nose.

For a long moment there was silence in the classroom.

Then Dai Daruma laughed — a big, round, rumbly laugh. "Well! That was certainly *a* stroke. Just not the one I planned." He rocked back and forth until his weighted bottom swung him upright again. That was the wonderful thing about being a daruma. You could fall down, but you always, always bounced back up.

"Fall down seven, bounce up eight!" he cheered. "Try number two!"

---

Try number two had splotches.

Try number three had a blob so big it looked like a sleepy black cat had curled up in the middle of the page.

Try number four was going beautifully — truly, genuinely beautifully — until Dai got so excited about how beautiful it was that he wobbled with joy and dragged a long smear right through the lovely part.

"Hmph," said Dai. He lined the papers up on the floor and frowned his most determined frown at them. Smear. Blob. Comet. Sleepy cat. Not a single proper chikara among them.

He tried again. And again. He tried holding the brush higher, the way the scrolls on the wall seemed to suggest, and the ink dripped down his arm. He tried holding it lower, and the bristles bent flat and made a sound like a tiny duck. He tried closing his eyes for focus, which is a famously bad idea, and wrote what appeared to be a long surprised eyebrow across two pages at once.

"This brush," Dai informed the empty room, "is being very difficult on purpose."

The brush, balanced patiently across an ink stone, did not look the least bit difficult. It looked, if anything, like it was waiting for Dai to figure something out.

By now the afternoon light had gone golden and slanty through the paper screens, and somewhere a wind-chime went *ting*. A real calligraphy master, Dai knew, would have a wall of perfect characters by now, each one crisp and black and balanced as a folded crane. A real master would not have ink on his eyebrows.

Dai had ink on his eyebrows.

He also, he realized, had ink on his belly, on the cushion, on one mud-flap-shaped foot, and somehow on the *ceiling*, which he did not even want to think about.

"Maybe," said Dai quietly, in a smaller voice than usual, "maybe some of us are just better at falling down than standing out."

---

Now, that is exactly the kind of sentence a story cannot allow to sit there, so the story did something about it.

The breeze nudged the screen door. It swung open just a crack — enough for the last warm sunbeam of the day to slide across the floor and land, of all places, on try number one. The comet streak. The very first, wobbliest, most accidental mark he had made.

And in that golden light, Dai Daruma tilted his head.

"...Huh," he said.

The streak wasn't a failed chikara. Of course it wasn't. But it wasn't *nothing*, either. It was bold. It was brave. It went swooping clear across the page with the confidence of someone who had absolutely no idea what they were doing and did it anyway, full speed, full heart. It looked, Dai thought, exactly the way it had *felt* — like a little round friend rolling joyfully out of control on his very first day.

It looked like trying.

"That," said Dai Daruma slowly, "is the proudest mistake I have ever made."

He rolled closer to the line of papers and looked at them again — really looked, the way you look at the stars when you finally stop wishing they were streetlights. The blob wasn't a blob. It was *enthusiasm*. The sleepy-cat smear wasn't ruined. It was *brave*. Every messy, splotchy, wobbly mark was the shape of a daruma who had not given up.

A master's wall is full of perfect characters.

But a beginner's floor, Dai realized, is full of courage.

He thought about the scrolls hanging so proudly above him — each one balanced, each one calm. And for the first time it occurred to Dai that every single master who had ever written one of those scrolls had *also* started exactly here: on a floor, with a too-big brush, making a comet and a blob and a sleepy ink cat. Nobody was born knowing how to kaku. Every steady hand had once been a wobbly one. Every perfect line had a whole secret pile of splotchy first tries hidden behind it, somewhere, that nobody ever hung on a wall.

"Maybe that's the part they don't show you," Dai said softly. "The bouncing part comes before the standing part. It always does."

---

He looked down at his own painted face — the one big eye he'd had since the day he was made, and the other eye still blank and waiting. Everyone knew the daruma way: you paint in one eye when you make a wish, and the second eye only when the wish comes true. Dai had been saving his second eye for the day he wrote something *perfect*.

"But that's silly," he said aloud, and grinned. "Perfect is too small a wish for a whole eye. No — that eye is saving itself for something *bigger*. Something grand, somewhere down the road." He puffed up his chest. "Today I don't need a wish at all. Today I only need to be *brave enough to try* — and I already DID that. Seven times!"

He picked the brush back up. It still felt enormous in his stubby arms. His ink was getting low. His paper was getting crumply. His eyebrows were, frankly, a disaster.

He didn't care one bit.

"One more," he said. "Not for perfect. For proud."

---

Dai Daruma planted himself like a little crimson mountain. He thought about all the wobbling that had brought him here. He thought about how the bravest thing in the world isn't never falling — it's the bounce.

He lifted the brush.

He breathed in.

And this time, when he felt himself start to tip, he didn't fight it. He went *with* it. He let his round body sway into the stroke, swooping the brush down in one big confident swoosh — *shhhk* — then across — *shhk* — then a final flick that sent a tiny happy spray of droplets into the air like fireworks.

He rocked back on his weighted bottom and looked.

It was not perfect.

The first stroke leaned. The second was a little fat. There was a droplet freckle where no droplet was supposed to be. But it was, unmistakably, a real character. Chikara. Strength. Written by a daruma who had fallen down seven times and stood up eight, ink-eyebrowed and beaming.

It was the best thing he had ever made, precisely *because* you could see the wobble in it.

"Hello, strength," whispered Dai. "There you are. You were inside the wobble the whole time."

---

That evening, when the calligraphy master came to tidy the classroom, she found a little crimson daruma fast asleep beside a long row of inky papers, the brush tucked under one arm like a teddy bear.

She picked up the last sheet — the leaning, freckled, gloriously imperfect chikara — and a slow smile spread across her face.

"Now *that*," she murmured, "is what writing looks like when somebody means it."

She woke him gently, and together they chose the very center of the classroom wall — and it was Dai himself, wobbling proudly on the master's little stepping stool, who hung his splotchy, brave, beautiful first character right where every beginner who came after him would see it first.

For a long happy moment, Dai looked at his wobbly chikara in its place of honor. Then he caught his reflection in the darkening window — one bold painted eye, and one still blank and waiting. He could paint the second one right now, he supposed. It had certainly been a proud day.

"Not yet," he decided, patting the blank eye fondly. "You're not for today, friend. You're waiting for the great wish — the big one, at the far end of this whole grand journey. And oh, won't THAT be a day worth two whole eyes."

One eye open, one eye dreaming. Not *finished*. **Proud.**

"Fall down seven," he said softly to the quiet golden room, "bounce up eight. And kaku with your whole brave heart."

Then he yawned an enormous round yawn, rolled gently onto his side, and decided that tomorrow he would try something *really* hard.

But that — that was a story for tomorrow.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, your first try does not have to be perfect to be wonderful — it only has to be brave. Tomorrow you may wobble, and smudge, and roll right off the cushion, and that is exactly how brave beginnings look. So rest now, soft and round as a sleepy daruma, knowing that every splotch you make is the shape of a heart that tried. Bounce up gently in the morning, and kaku your own brave mark upon the day. You are already proud. You are already enough. Goodnight.
