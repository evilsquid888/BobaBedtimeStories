# Story 24: Niko Nigiri and the Thousand Red Gates

**Main Character:** Niko Nigiri
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** The endless tunnel of vermilion torii gates at Fushimi Inari, Kyoto
**Theme:** You don't need to see the end to enjoy the path
**Japanese Spotlight:** あかい (akai) = red

---

Niko Nigiri arrived at the foot of the mountain exactly on time, which was, in his opinion, the only acceptable way to arrive anywhere. He stood very straight — a tidy oval pillow of pearly rice wearing his glossy coral salmon cape, his neat little nori belt cinched just so — and he held up a tiny clipboard.

"Today," Niko announced to nobody in particular, because nobody in particular was the most reliable audience, "I shall count every single gate. All one thousand of them. Neatly."

Above him stretched the most marvelous sight in all of Kyoto: a tunnel of **torii** (toh-ree) = shrine gates, glowing **あかい** (akai) = red, marching up the mountainside like a great wooden caterpillar made of doorways. Sunlight slipped between them in warm orange ribbons.

"One," said Niko, ticking his clipboard. "Two. Three." He walked faster. "Four-five-six-seveneightnine—"

He tripped over his own salmon cape and sat down with a soft *floomp*.

"...Tenth gate," he sighed, "achieved by falling into it. Not neat at all."

---

Niko stood, brushed a grain of rice back into place, and frowned up the endless red tunnel. He could not see the top. He could not even see *around the bend.* And for a fellow who liked to do things "the right way," not seeing the end of something felt like wearing his nori belt backward — itchy and wrong.

"If I don't know how many gates are left," he reasoned, "how will I know if I'm making good progress? I must see the whole path at once. Then I can plan it. Properly."

So Niko tried.

First he stood on his very tippy-rice-toes and stretched his neck up high. He saw exactly four more gates and one extremely unbothered cat sitting in the path, washing its paw.

"Excuse me," Niko said politely. "Do you know how many gates there are?"

The cat looked at him, blinked slowly, and went back to washing its paw — which Niko took to mean *that is a you problem, rice friend.*

Next he tried climbing the **やま** (yama) = mountain off to the side, to get a better view. But the mountain was steep and mossy, and Niko was shaped like a small pillow, and pillows are famously bad at climbing. He slid right back down with a *swooooosh* and landed — *floomp* — exactly where he'd started.

"Fourteen gates counted," he reported to the cat. "And one whole mountain *un*-climbed. Marvelously efficient."

The cat yawned.

Then Niko had what he was *certain* was a brilliant idea. "A tower!" he declared. "I shall build a counting tower and see over the bend!" He gathered three smooth pebbles, a curled-up leaf, and an acorn, and he stacked them very carefully, very neatly, tallest things on the bottom. He climbed on top. The tower wobbled. The acorn shot out sideways. And Niko did a slow, dignified somersault into a soft bush, where he lay upside down with his salmon cape over his face, looking at the world the wrong way up.

From down there, with his feet in the air, he could see exactly *zero* gates — but he could see one ladybug walking calmly along a twig, minding its own small business, not worrying one bit about the top of the mountain.

"You," Niko told the ladybug, "have absolutely no idea how to make a counting tower. And honestly? Right now I envy you."

---

Niko sat down properly this time and pulled out a fresh sheet on his clipboard. He drew a very neat chart with very neat boxes. He labeled it **PLAN FOR COUNTING ALL THE GATES AT ONCE (NEATLY).** He stared at it. The boxes stared back. They were extremely neat and completely useless, because you cannot count a thousand things you cannot see, no matter how lovely your boxes are.

"Let's do this neatly," Niko murmured, his catchphrase coming out a little wobbly. "...or—"

He stopped. He'd been about to say *or deliciously*, which was the other half of his catchphrase, the half he said when plans went sideways and a fellow simply had to roll with surprises. But rolling with surprises was precisely the thing Niko found hardest. Surprises did not fit in neat boxes. Surprises did not announce themselves on time.

A small breeze wandered down through the tunnel just then. It made every red gate hum a low, gentle note — *hmmmmmm* — like the whole mountain was a giant wooden harmonica played by the sky. The warm light shifted, and for one cozy second the tunnel ahead glowed so brightly **あかい** that Niko forgot all about his clipboard.

"Oh," he said softly. "That's rather... nice."

Then he caught himself, sat up straight, and said sternly, "Focus, Niko. We have *counting* to do."

---

He marched on. Gate fifteen. Gate sixteen. Each one was painted that deep, warm, lantern-glow **あかい**, with black characters down the wooden posts. Niko told himself he didn't have time to read them; he only had time to count them. So he counted, faster and faster, until the gates began to blur into one long red smear and his little legs ached and his salmon cape drooped and he hadn't actually *seen* a single gate in ages — he'd only *numbered* them.

At gate forty-something (he'd lost the exact figure during the slide down the mountain, which still bothered him deeply), Niko reached a quiet bend where the path split in two. Two red tunnels, side by side, both vanishing around their own curves.

Niko froze.

"Two paths," he whispered. "Which one leads to the top? Which one is *correct?* If I pick wrong, I'll have to come back, and then my count will be all muddled, and—" His rice grains practically rattled with worry.

It was here, at the splitting of the path, that Niko met the fox.

---

She was a small stone **きつね** (kitsune) = fox, the kind that guards shrines, sitting tidily on a little pedestal beside the gates. She had a chip on one ear and a face worn smooth and kind by a hundred years of rain. And — this is the marvelous part, the part you'd only believe at bedtime — when Niko looked at her, she gave the tiniest stone wink.

"You're in an awful hurry," said the fox, in a voice like dry leaves and warm tea, "for someone walking through somewhere so lovely."

"I have to count all the gates," Niko explained. "All one thousand. Neatly. But I can't *see* them all at once, so I don't know how I'm doing, and now there are two paths, and—" He held up his clipboard helplessly. The boxes were still empty.

The stone fox considered this. "May I ask you something, rice cake? The gate you are standing under, right now. What color is it?"

Niko blinked. "**Akai.** Red. Obviously."

"Is it? Look properly. Not the whole thousand. Just this one."

So Niko looked. *Properly.* For the first time all day.

---

And oh — the gate above him was not just "red." It was a thousand reds at once. It was sunset-red where the light caught the wood, and shadow-red in the deep grain, and warm-cherry-red where someone long ago had painted it with care, and there was even a little patch of soft moss-green where the rain liked to gather, and a tiny golden spider's web strung between two beams, sparkling like it had been hung up specially for a festival.

It was, Niko realized, the most beautiful gate he had ever seen.

It was *also* the most beautiful gate he had ever seen at gate fifteen, and gate forty, and gate ten-which-he-fell-into. He'd just been going too fast to notice.

"I didn't see any of them," Niko said quietly. "I was so busy counting the path that I forgot to *walk* it."

The stone fox's eyes crinkled. "That is the secret of the mountain, little one. You cannot see the top. Nobody can. The gates go up and up and around and around, and even the people who built them never saw the whole thing at once. But that was never the point." She nodded at the glowing tunnel. "The point is *this* gate. And then the next one. One cozy step at a time."

---

Niko looked down at his clipboard. At the very neat, very empty boxes. At **PLAN FOR COUNTING ALL THE GATES AT ONCE (NEATLY).**

Slowly — and it really was the bravest thing he did all day, braver than the mountain, braver than the cat — Niko turned to a fresh page. And instead of boxes, he wrote, in his tidiest hand:

***Gate 1: the warm red one with the golden spiderweb. Lovely.***

Then he tucked the clipboard away and simply *walked.*

He walked under the next gate and noticed the way the light fell in soft orange stripes across his salmon cape. He walked under the one after that and heard the mountain hum its harmonica note again — *hmmmmmm* — and this time he hummed back, a little wobbly nigiri harmony that made a passing dragonfly stop and listen.

He found a tiny stream chattering down beside the path, so cold and clear it made his rice grains shiver happily, and he dipped his cape-edge in and watched the ripples carry a single red maple leaf away around the bend — off to see gates Niko would never count, and somehow that thought didn't worry him anymore. He found a mossy old gate so soft and green at the bottom that he gave it a gentle pat, the way you'd pat a sleepy old dog. At a tiny bend he found a row of teeny fox statues no bigger than acorns, each wearing a red bib, and he laughed out loud, a real laugh, the first one all day.

"Hello, little ones," he said, bowing politely to each acorn-fox. "Let's do this neatly... or *deliciously.*" And this time, the *deliciously* came out just right — easy and warm, the sound of a fellow finally rolling with the surprises.

---

He never did count to a thousand. Somewhere up near the top, where the trees opened and all of Kyoto lay below him like a bowl of twinkling lights, Niko realized he'd stopped counting entirely a long, long time ago — and that nothing bad had happened at all. The gates had not run away. The path had not broken. The world had not become un-neat. It had simply become *lovely*, one gate at a time.

He sat at the very top as the sky turned the same deep **あかい** as the gates, until the mountain and the sunset were one single warm red glow, and he could not tell where the doorways ended and the evening began.

"You know," Niko told the first evening star, very straight and very proud, "I think the path enjoyed me right back."

And far below, on her little pedestal, the small stone fox gave one more tiny wink — and kept the secret of the thousand red gates safe for the next hurried little traveler who needed to learn how to slow down.

## Goodnight Blessing

May you walk your own path one cozy gate at a time, dear one, never minding how far the top might be. May you notice the small lovely things — the golden spiderweb, the warm red light, the gentle hum of the wind — that you only ever see when you slow right down. You don't have to know how the day will end to enjoy the step you're standing in. Tuck in now, soft as pearly rice, and let tomorrow's gates wait quietly until morning. They'll still be there, glowing **akai** and patient, when you're ready. Goodnight.
