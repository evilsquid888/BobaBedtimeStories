# Story 71: Tai Taiyaki and the Children's Day Carp

**Main Character:** Tai Taiyaki
**Guest Characters:** Tama Onigiri
**Setting:** A breezy hillside on Children's Day with giant carp streamers
**Theme:** The smallest flag matters most to the smallest watchers
**Japanese Spotlight:** **kodomo no hi** (koh-doh-moh noh hee) — Children's Day

---

On the morning of **kodomo no hi** (koh-doh-moh noh hee) — Children's Day — Tai Taiyaki waddled up the hillside before the dew was even done, because he wanted to be the very first fish to see the sky go fishing.

And oh, it had! Strung from tall bamboo poles all up the hill, giant carp streamers gulped the breeze and swam through the air — scarlet ones, midnight-blue ones, a gold one the size of a canoe. Tai, a cheerful golden fish-shaped cake with a crispy fin-tail and a warm red-bean heart you could see when he grinned, grinned so wide that a passing sparrow mistook him for sunrise.

"Swim toward the sweet stuff!" he whooped, flapping up the path.

Then he stopped. He counted the tallest pole's streamers the way you count your own toes — papa carp, mama carp, big-kid carp, and...

Nothing. At the bottom of the line, where the smallest carp always flew — the minnow-sized silver one, the one that swam for the littlest children of the village — there was only a snapped string, flicking in the wind like a sad noodle.

Old Mr. Hata, the festival caretaker, stood beneath the pole holding his hat. "The storm last night," he sighed. "It tore the little one right off and carried it away. The ceremony is at noon, and I've no spare. Unless..." He looked at his own sock, then shook his head. "No. The children would notice."

The littlest kids would come at noon — and find no carp flying for them at all.

Tai's red-bean heart went *thump*. He was a small fish himself. He knew exactly how big a missing small thing could feel.

"Mr. Hata," he announced, fins on hips, "this fish is going fishing."

---

Tai followed the storm's trail down the hill the way only a fish-cake can: nose first, tail wagging. The wind had blown from the hilltop toward the village gardens, so that's where he waddle-zoomed, checking everything. He checked a hedge (one umbrella, very bent). He checked the pond (three ducks, very unhelpful). He checked under a bench (one pickle, mysteriously).

Then he heard the crows laughing.

Three of them sat on a fence around Mr. Hata's vegetable patch, cackling at the scarecrow — because the scarecrow appeared, from every angle, to be getting swallowed headfirst by a silver fish.

There it was! The smallest carp streamer had blown clean across the gardens and wrapped itself snugly over the scarecrow's head and shoulders, tail flapping where a face should be, so the poor fellow looked like the first scarecrow in history to lose an argument with lunch.

"Hang on, friend! I'll save you!" Tai called — to the scarecrow or the streamer, even he wasn't sure — and he scrambled up the post, gripped the fabric with his fins, and pulled. The streamer came loose with a *fwip*, the scarecrow's button eyes reappeared looking deeply embarrassed, and the crows applauded, which from crows sounds exactly like more laughing.

But when Tai spread the little carp out on the grass, his fins drooped. The storm had not been gentle. There was a long ragged tear right through the tail — a hole big enough to put his whole head through, which he did, briefly, just to check, and then wished he hadn't, because now he was wearing it.

"A carp with a torn tail can't hold the wind," he said, wriggling free. "It'll just flop." He looked at the sun. Halfway to noon already.

A small fish cannot sew. But a small fish knows who packs *everything*.

---

Tama Onigiri was laying out a picnic blanket at the bottom of the hill — a plump, cozy triangle of warm rice with a crisp seaweed sash and a little plum blush on one cheek. When Tai came flapping up with a wounded carp streamer and a speech he'd been practicing the whole way, she held up one hand before he got past "EMERGENCY—"

"Needle and thread?" she said, already digging in her pack. "I packed a little extra, just in case."

Out came a sewing kit. Also two spoons, a whistle, a folding fan, a backup whistle, and a pickled plum labeled *DO NOT PANIC*.

"You're magic," said Tai.

"I'm *prepared*," said Tama Onigiri. "It looks the same from far away."

She threaded the needle and frowned at the tear. "I can stitch the edges, but there's fabric missing — see? The hole needs a patch. Something light. Something strong. Something that won't mind the wind." She glanced around the picnic. "And we don't have anything like—"

Tai was already unwrapping himself.

Because Tai Taiyaki, like every taiyaki from the riverside stall, had come wrapped in a square of crisp wax paper — *his* paper, printed with tiny blue waves, the one he kept folded under his fin like a cape on cold mornings. He smoothed it flat on the blanket. It was exactly the size of the hole, and it crinkled like it had been waiting its whole life for this.

"He's a water fish," Tai said. "He should have a little water on him."

So Tama stitched, fast and neat, while Tai held the fabric taut — and only sewed Tai's own fin to the carp once, which they agreed was a very good average. By the time the patch was on, the little silver carp had a square of tiny blue waves shining in his tail like a piece of the sea.

"Now," said Tama, biting the thread, "how exactly do we get him *up*?"

Tai looked at the tallest pole on the hill, far away and very, very tall.

"Ah," he said. "Yes. Up."

---

Here was the trouble, and it was a tall one. The streamers on the great pole hung from a rope that ran through a pulley at the very top, and the storm had jammed the pulley with a stubborn knot of tangled line. Mr. Hata couldn't lower the rope to clip the little carp on. Which meant somebody small, somebody light, somebody with absolutely no sense when it came to heights, had to climb the pole and clip it on by hand.

"I'm a fish," said Tai. "Climbing is just swimming with extra holding on."

Tama tied the rolled-up streamer to his tail with her spare sash, so his fins were free. "Ganbatte," she said — "**ganbatte** (gahn-baht-teh) — do your best!" — and stuffed the *DO NOT PANIC* plum in his mouth for luck.

Up Tai went. The bamboo was still rain-slick, so he climbed the way a taiyaki must: hug, scoot, hug, scoot, with his flat batter body pressed to the pole and his crispy tail-fin catching each gust like a little sail, boosting him up in hops. Below, the first families were arriving on the hill, spreading blankets. A cluster of the littlest children gathered at the bottom of the pole, heads back, mouths open.

"A fish is climbing the pole," said one toddler, in the tone of someone reporting completely normal weather.

Hug, scoot. Hug, scoot. Tai's fins burned. The wind got bigger the higher he went, the way wind does, and the pole swayed in slow, stomach-tilting circles. At last — at LAST — he reached the top, where the jammed pulley waited, and the empty clip dangled from the line, and the whole spring valley spread out below him like a picnic for giants.

He untied the streamer from his tail. He reached for the clip.

And the wind — the big, rude, unsentimental noon wind — snatched the little carp right out of his fins.

"NO!" Tai watched it tumble away from the pole, down and down, silver and waves and all that stitching — and he did not think, because thinking takes time. He let go.

A taiyaki is a flat fish. A flat fish in a dive is *fast*. Tai dropped like a golden arrow, fins back, eyes streaming, gaining on the fluttering streamer — closer — closer — and he hooked it with his tail-fin at the very last moment, spread himself wide and flat against the air, and glided — *whoooosh* — in one long bumpy swoop, right into the middle of Tama Onigiri's picnic blanket, where he bounced twice and came to rest in the potato salad.

The littlest children cheered wildly. Tai lay there a moment, hugging the streamer, breathing hard.

"The noon bell rings in a few minutes," said Mr. Hata gently. "Nobody would blame you, little one. It's a long climb twice."

Tai looked at the children. The children looked at the empty spot at the bottom of the line.

"That's their carp," he said, and stood up, dripping potato salad. "Little fish stick together."

The second climb was slower. His fins shook. Halfway up he stopped, forehead against the bamboo, and very nearly couldn't. But from below came small voices, chanting the way only small voices can — *"go, fish! go, fish!"* — and his red-bean heart filled up warmer than any wind. Hug, scoot. Hug, scoot. This time, at the top, he hooked his tail-fin around the pole, held the streamer in his teeth, and clipped it on with both fins just as the noon bell sang out across the valley.

---

The wind found the little carp at once.

It poured in through his round mouth, and the patched tail held — *held!* — and the smallest streamer on the whole hillside filled out silver and proud and swam, flicking and rippling at the bottom of the great line, with a square of tiny blue waves flashing in his tail like the sea saying hello.

Down below, the littlest children went absolutely wild.

"The baby carp! The baby carp flies for US!" they shouted, jumping with their arms up, and one toddler bowed to the pole, and another tried to bow and sat down instead, and nobody minded one bit.

Tai slid down the bamboo — fwip, fwip, fwip — into a hero's welcome of sticky hands and flower crowns and someone's half-eaten rice cracker, gravely offered. Mr. Hata shook his fin up and down for a long time and didn't say anything, which is how some grown-ups say the most.

"You know," said Tama Onigiri, dusting potato salad off him, "the big carp up there are beautiful. But not one of those little ones is looking at the big carp."

It was true. Every small face on the hillside was turned to the smallest fish in the sky — the one their size, the one with the patched tail, the one that flew for *them*.

"The littlest watchers need a fish their own size," Tai said softly, watching it swim. "That's the one that tells them the sky has room for small fish too."

---

The afternoon mellowed into evening the way festivals do — slowly, and full of crumbs. The families packed their blankets. The flower crowns wilted happily. And the wind grew soft and sleepy, so that all up the hillside the carp streamers stopped their daytime dashing and began to swim slow, the way fish do in a quiet pond at dusk: a long, lazy ripple, nose to tail, over and over, like breathing.

Tai Taiyaki and Tama Onigiri lay back in the warm clover and watched them. Papa carp, mama carp, big-kid carp — and there at the bottom, smallest and silver, the little one, riding the evening breeze with his patch of blue waves glowing faintly gold in the sunset.

"He swims fine with a patched tail," murmured Tama, eyes half closed.

"Better than fine," yawned Tai. "He swims *loved*."

And the smallest carp dipped his nose, just once, as if he agreed.

## Goodnight Blessing
Sleepy one, the festival is folded up and the wind has gone soft and slow. High on the hill the carp are swimming their dozy evening swim, and the smallest one is flying right where he belongs — because somebody small refused to give up on him. Remember this as you drift off: you never know who is watching the sky hoping to see something just their size. Showing up for the littlest ones is the biggest kind of brave. So rest your fins, little helper. Climb down from the day. Let the breeze rock you slow, nose to tail, like a carp in a quiet evening sky. Goodnight.
