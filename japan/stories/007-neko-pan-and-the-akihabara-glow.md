# Story 7: Neko-Pan and the Akihabara Glow

**Main Character:** Neko-Pan (Maneki Melon Cream)
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** The bright electric streets of Akihabara, Tokyo
**Theme:** Sharing your luck multiplies it
**Japanese Spotlight:** **Maneki-neko** (mah-neh-kee neh-koh) — beckoning lucky cat

---

Now, if you have never seen a cream-filled bun shaped like a beckoning cat go *wheee* down a Tokyo sidewalk at dusk, then you have not yet lived your most delicious life. But tonight you will, because tonight Neko-Pan was bouncing through **Akihabara** (ah-kee-hah-bah-rah) — the electric town — with one little paw raised and a gold-foil collar bell going *ting-a-ling* on every hop.

Neko-Pan was a soft custard-hearted bun shaped like a **maneki-neko** (mah-neh-kee neh-koh) — a beckoning lucky cat — you know, those little statues in shop windows with one paw waving hello. Except Neko-Pan was warm, and crackly-domed, and absolutely, positively *alive*.

"Wave for luck, share the luck!" Neko-Pan sang, waving that raised paw at a lamppost.

The lamppost did not wave back. Lampposts rarely do. But Neko-Pan didn't mind one bit, because just then — *blink-blink-FLASH* — the whole street woke up.

---

Akihabara at night is a billion glowing signs stacked on top of each other like a wedding cake made of lightning. Pink signs. Blue signs. Signs shaped like robots, rocket ships, ramen bowls, and one enormous cat that meowed every nine seconds whether you wanted it to or not.

Neko-Pan stood in the middle of it all, custard heart going *thump*, and gasped the gasp of a bun who has found heaven.

"Oh," whispered Neko-Pan. "Oh, this is the *luckiest* street in the whole wide world. There's so much to wave at, I don't know where to start!"

So Neko-Pan started everywhere at once, which is the very best way to start.

The first stop was a tiny noodle stand squeezed between two skyscrapers, where an old radish-shaped fellow named Mister Daikon was stirring a pot and looking glummer than a Monday morning. His **chochin** (choh-cheen) — paper lantern — had gone dark, its little candle flickering out, and nobody was stopping to eat.

"My lantern's broken," Mister Daikon sighed, "and a noodle stand with no glow is a noodle stand with no friends."

Neko-Pan toddled right up, raised that lucky paw, and gave it a great big enthusiastic *wave*.

"Hello-hello! Wave for luck, share the luck!"

And here's the funny thing about luck — it's a bit like a sneeze. The moment one happens, another one's usually right behind it. Just as Neko-Pan waved, a gust of warm noodle-steam puffed up, caught the lantern's wick, and — *fwoomp!* — relit it. The lantern glowed a buttery gold.

"My lantern!" Mister Daikon cried. "It's brighter than ever! How did you—"

"I didn't!" Neko-Pan giggled, paws over a custard-soft grin. "I just waved. The luck did the rest. It's very good at its job. Honestly, it does most of the work. I mostly just point it in the right direction and try not to get in its way."

A passing pigeon, three tourists, and a small confused beetle all noticed the glowing lantern at the same moment and decided that any stand *that* cozy must have wonderful noodles. They lined right up. The beetle, who could not actually eat noodles, ordered some anyway, just to be part of things. Mister Daikon laughed so hard his radish-cheeks turned pink and a single noodle flew out of the pot and landed on his hat, where it sat like a very floppy feather.

"You know," Mister Daikon chuckled, straightening the noodle-feather, "I was about to close up forever. I'd packed away my best bowls. Now look — I'll be ladling 'til midnight!"

"Take some luck for the road!" Neko-Pan called, already bouncing onward, paw waving.

"But wait — don't you want to *keep* some?" Mister Daikon shouted after the little bun.

Neko-Pan only bounced higher. Because Neko-Pan had a secret, and the secret was this: the more luck you give away, the more you seem to find lying around. It's the silliest math in the world. It should not work. It works anyway.

---

Down the next alley, Neko-Pan found a claw machine.

Now, you and I both know claw machines are the trickiest, slipperiest, most ticklish contraptions ever invented. This one was packed with tiny stuffed mochi-toys, all squished up against the glass with hopeful little faces. And in front of it stood a small girl — well, a small *gyoza* dumpling-girl, with crimped pleats around her edges like a fancy collar — who had spent her very last coin and won absolutely nothing.

"Nine tries," the gyoza-girl sniffled, her crimped pleats drooping. "Nine! The claw always lets go at the last second. It scoops up the prize, it lifts it up high, my heart goes *whee* — and then *ploop*, it drops it. Every single time. I think it *hates* me. I think this claw was built specifically to break dumpling hearts."

"The claw doesn't hate anybody," Neko-Pan said gently. "It's just shy. It needs a little luck to be brave." And Neko-Pan raised that paw and waved at the claw machine with all the warmth of a fresh-baked bun.

"Wave for luck, share the luck!"

The claw machine's lights did a happy little shimmy. Somewhere deep inside its gears, something went *clunk* — a friendly clunk, the clunk of a machine that has decided to stop being shy.

"Give it one more try," said Neko-Pan.

"But I'm out of coins!"

Neko-Pan reached into the soft custard fold where lucky things tend to gather, and — *would you believe it* — there sat one shiny coin that absolutely had not been there a moment ago. (Luck does this. Luck is a terrible show-off.)

The gyoza-girl dropped the coin in with a trembling pleat. The claw descended, *hummmm*, swung left, swung right, hovered, hesitated, and — Neko-Pan gave the gentlest extra wave, just for good measure — opened wide and grabbed the biggest, plushiest mochi-toy of the whole bunch. It held tight. It held *tighter*. It rose up, up, up. The gyoza-girl squeezed her eyes shut, certain it would *ploop* like always.

But the claw did NOT let go.

*THUNK.* The prize tumbled down the chute.

The gyoza-girl squealed so loudly that two birds fell off a sign. She hugged the mochi-toy. Then she hugged Neko-Pan. Then she did something Neko-Pan absolutely loved: she turned to the next kid in line — a worried little **onigiri** (oh-nee-ghee-ree), a rice ball — and said, "Here. *You* try next. I'll wave for you!" And she copied Neko-Pan's wave exactly, paw and all.

Neko-Pan's custard heart went so warm it nearly melted right out the bottom.

"Ohhh," Neko-Pan breathed. "You're spreading it. You caught the luck and you're *passing it on*."

"That's how it works, right?" said the gyoza-girl, beaming.

"That," said Neko-Pan, "is EXACTLY how it works."

---

But here is where our story takes a wobble, because not every street stays bright forever.

Neko-Pan bounced around one last corner — and stopped. Everything was dark.

A whole stretch of Akihabara had gone quiet and gray. The giant cat sign had stopped meowing — which felt deeply, unfairly wrong, like a clock forgetting how to tick. The signs were black. The buildings loomed like sleepy giants who'd forgotten to brush their teeth. Even the air felt dim, the way a room feels when someone you love has left it.

A power flicker, the grown-ups would've called it. A whole block of light, snoozing all at once.

In the middle of the dark stood a wobbly old vending machine, sniffling. "It's my fault," the vending machine hiccuped. "I sneezed an electric sneeze and *bzzzt* — I knocked out the whole block. Now nobody will ever come down this street again. It's too dark. Too sad. Nobody likes a dark street."

A little crowd of nervous treats had gathered — a melonpan here, a dango trio there, a taiyaki flapping anxiously — all murmuring, *what do we do, what do we do.*

Neko-Pan looked at all the worried faces. Then Neko-Pan looked at one single paw. One small, raised, lucky paw.

"That's not very much luck for a whole dark street," Neko-Pan admitted. "Not by itself."

And then Neko-Pan grinned the biggest grin a bun has ever grinned.

"But it doesn't have to be by itself! Everybody — raise your paws! Or your fins! Or your dumpling-tops! Wave with me! When you share luck, it doesn't split in half — it MULTIPLIES!"

The treats looked at each other. The taiyaki shrugged a fin. The dango trio pivoted in a row like a tiny carousel. The melonpan yawned, then waved anyway, because waving is much easier than arguing and far cozier too.

"But what if it doesn't work?" whispered the smallest dango.

"Then we'll have waved together in the dark," said Neko-Pan, "and that's still a lovely thing to do. But I don't think it'll come to that. I've got a feeling. And my feelings are about ninety percent custard, which is the most reliable percent there is."

"On three!" Neko-Pan called. "Wave for luck — share the luck! One... two... THREE!"

And the whole crowd waved. Dozens of little paws and fins and frosted hands beckoning all at once, *come back, come back, glow again.*

For one breath, nothing happened.

Then — *tink.* A single sign flickered.

Then *tink-tink-tink-FWOOSH* — they all came roaring back to life at once, pink and blue and gold and electric-green, and the giant cat sign meowed *three* times in a row like it was making up for lost time, and the dark street wasn't dark anymore. It was the *brightest* street in all of Akihabara, brighter even than before, because every single one of those waves had stacked together like coins in a lucky jar.

The vending machine burst into happy tears that came out, sideways, as a free bottle of Ramune that fizzed all over everybody. Nobody minded. Fizz on a happy night is just bubbly confetti.

"You did it!" the vending machine cried. "You fixed the whole street!"

"*We* did it," said Neko-Pan, "and that's a hundred times luckier. One paw is a wave. A hundred paws is a *sunrise*." Neko-Pan twirled once, dizzy with joy. "See? My luck never ran out. It just kept bumping into *more* of it. That's the trick nobody believes until they try it: you can't give luck away. You can only *spread it thinner and brighter,* like butter on warm toast, until the whole loaf glows."

---

By the time the moon climbed up over Akihabara, the whole street was humming and glowing and full of friends. Mister Daikon's noodles were the talk of the town. The gyoza-girl was teaching a whole line of kids how to wave. And everywhere Neko-Pan looked, treats were waving at each other — paw to fin, dumpling to bun — passing little glimmers of luck back and forth until the air practically sparkled.

Neko-Pan curled up on a warm window ledge, custard heart full to the brim, and gave one last sleepy wave to the great big glowing town.

"Funny thing about luck," Neko-Pan yawned. "I gave it all away tonight... and I've never felt so full."

The collar bell went *ting* — softly, this time. A goodnight sort of *ting*.

And somewhere far below, a hundred little lights waved back.

Wave goodnight to the moon, and let the bright dreams find you. Goodnight.
