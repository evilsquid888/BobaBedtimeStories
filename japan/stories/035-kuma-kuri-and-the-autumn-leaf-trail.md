# Story 35: Kuma Kuri and the Autumn Leaf Trail

**Main Character:** Kuma the Kuri (roasted chestnut / kuri kinton sweet)
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A mountain temple path blazing with red and gold autumn leaves
**Theme:** Change can be the most beautiful thing of all
**Japanese Spotlight:** もみじ (momiji) = autumn maple leaves

---

Kuma the Kuri was hiding inside a teakettle, and he was not coming out.

"Kuma," called a gentle voice through the spout, "the kettle is for tea. You are a chestnut. These are different jobs."

"I know," said Kuma, his small mahogany voice echoing all warm and round inside the kettle. "But it's cozy in here. And it's *summer* in here. Out there, summer is leaving, and I am not invited to say goodbye properly, and also my toes are cold."

"Chestnuts don't have toes," the voice pointed out kindly.

"Then why are they cold?" Kuma demanded, which was a very good question, and nobody had an answer for it, which is the best kind of question to ask when you are sad and hiding in a kettle and would like to win an argument before breakfast.

Outside, the mountain was changing. The big maple trees that had been green and humming all summer were quietly trading their leaves for new colors, the way you might trade a wet swimsuit for a soft dry sweater. And Kuma — small, sturdy, fuzzy-capped Kuma, with his autumn-leaf scarf and his cozy roasted-warm glow — did not like change one single bit.

---

The gentle voice belonged to Maru Matcha, who had come up the mountain temple path with her little bamboo whisk tucked behind her like a wand. She sat down beside the teakettle, folded her frothy matcha-cream swirl of hair against the breeze, and said nothing at all for a while. Maru was very good at saying nothing. It was, she always claimed, one of the most useful things a friend could say.

Finally Kuma's round chestnut face peeked out of the spout.

"Summer was *perfect*," he said. "Warm grass. Long evenings. Cicadas singing the same song over and over until your brain went pleasantly mushy. And now it's all going away, and I didn't get to keep any of it." A tiny tear, warm as roasting steam, rolled down his glossy shell. "Things shouldn't change, Maru. The good things should just... stay good. Forever."

"Mm," said Maru, in her calm, half-moon-eyed way. "Whisk slow. Feel calm." She held out one small green hand. "Walk with me a little, Kuri-chan. I want to show you something. If it's terrible, you may go straight back into the kettle, and I will personally bring you a blanket and a snack and apologize to the kettle on your behalf."

Kuma considered this. It was, he had to admit, an extremely fair offer.

He climbed out.

---

The temple path wound up the mountain in gentle stone steps, worn smooth by a thousand years of slippered feet. And the moment Kuma stepped onto it, he stopped so suddenly that he very nearly tipped over onto his fuzzy cap.

Because the whole world had turned to fire — the soft, friendly, crackly kind of fire, the kind you toast marshmallows over, not the kind anyone needed to worry about.

The maple trees arched over the path in a glowing tunnel, and every single leaf had become a flame that didn't burn. Some were deep crimson, like the inside of a sweet red-bean bun. Some were burnt-orange, like Kuma's own roasted skin. Some were buttery gold, and some were a startled pink, as if they'd blushed at being looked at. A breeze wandered through, and a thousand leaves let go at once and came spinning down, twirling and tumbling and somersaulting, so that Kuma stood in a slow, sparkling snowfall made entirely of color.

"These," said Maru softly, catching one on the tip of her whisk, "are **もみじ** (momiji) = autumn maple leaves. The mountain wears them only for a little while. That's part of why they're so beautiful." She turned the little crimson leaf so it caught the light. "Beautiful things you can keep forever stop feeling beautiful. You start walking right past them. But momiji — momiji you *notice*. Because you know they're saying goodbye."

Kuma stared up at the burning, glowing, spinning tunnel of leaves. His chestnut heart did something strange. It ached a little. And it glowed a little. And, to his enormous surprise, the two feelings turned out to be friends.

"Oh," he whispered. "Oh, they're showing off."

Maru's mouth curved into her tiny, deadpan smile. "The whole mountain is showing off. It does this every single year. It can't help it. Summer leaves quietly. But autumn? Autumn likes to make an *entrance*."

---

They walked deeper along the trail, and the more Kuma looked, the more he found.

He found a **石灯籠** (ishidōrō) = stone lantern standing patient and mossy by the path, with three crimson leaves resting on its little roof like a tiny hat someone had knitted just for it. "It looks dressed up," Kuma said, and the lantern, being a lantern, modestly said nothing, but Kuma was fairly sure it was pleased.

He found a puddle from last night's rain, and floating on it were a dozen gold leaves arranged in a perfect, accidental swirl — a soup of pure autumn. And then — *crunch.*

Kuma froze. He looked down. He had stepped on a fallen leaf, and it had made the most satisfying sound he had ever heard in his entire roasted life.

"Maru," he breathed. "*Maru.* Did you hear that?"

He took another step. *Crackle.* And another. *CRUNCH.* A whole pile of dry leaves had drifted across the path, and Kuma waded straight into the middle of them and stomped, and the leaves went *crick-crack-crunch-CRUNCH* under his sturdy little feet, and it was, without any competition whatsoever, the funniest and best sound in the world.

"Crispy outside!" Kuma cheered, doing a wobbly chestnut twirl. "Soft heart inside! That's *me!* I'm crunchy too! The whole mountain is crunchy like me!"

He went absolutely wild. He stomped. He shuffled. He found the biggest leaf pile of all, took a running start on his short little legs — which is to say he took a slow, determined waddle — and flopped backward into it with a glorious *FWUMP*, sending leaves exploding up into the air in a fountain of red and gold that rained back down all over his fuzzy cap.

Maru sat on a step and watched, and she laughed her quiet matcha laugh, which sounded a bit like a teaspoon stirring honey.

---

Higher up the trail, where the steps grew steeper, they met an old, old tree — the grandest maple on the whole mountain, with a trunk so wide it would have taken six chestnuts holding hands to hug all the way around it. Its branches reached so high they seemed to brush the clouds, and every leaf upon it had turned the most astonishing shade of scarlet, so that the whole tree looked like a slow, silent bonfire reaching for the sky.

A little wooden sign was tied to its trunk with a faded ribbon. Kuma squinted up at it.

"It says this tree is three hundred years old," he read. He looked up into the blazing branches, suddenly feeling very small and very young. "Three hundred autumns. That's three hundred times it's done... all of *this.*"

"And it isn't tired of it yet," said Maru. "Watch."

A great gust came swooping down the mountain, and the old maple shivered and *let go* — not a few leaves, not a handful, but a whole sky's worth all at once, thousands and thousands of scarlet leaves lifting off the branches and pouring down the trail in a roaring, rustling, glittering river of red. Kuma squealed and threw both little arms up, and the leaves swept right over him and past him and around him, tickling his fuzzy cap, swirling in his scarf, burying his sturdy feet, until for one perfect moment he wasn't quite sure where Kuma ended and the autumn began.

When the gust finally settled, Kuma was up to his cap in scarlet, beaming so hard his roasted glow lit the leaves from inside like a little lantern.

"It gives them ALL away," he said in wonder, digging himself out one happy wiggle at a time. "Every single year. Three hundred years of giving everything away — and it never runs out. The next spring it just grows brand-new ones and does the whole thing all over again." He pressed a small hand to his shell. "How is it not the saddest tree on the mountain?"

"Because," said Maru, plucking a scarlet leaf from atop his cap and tucking it gently back into his scarf, "it learned the secret a very long time ago. You can let go of something *and* love it. The two go together, like crisp outsides and soft hearts." She tipped her bowl-head toward the towering tree. "It isn't grieving up there, Kuri-chan. It's *celebrating.* Every falling leaf is a tiny firework. The whole tree is one long, slow, three-hundred-year hanabi show, and we just happened to walk by during the grand finale."

Kuma looked up at the great scarlet tree for a long, quiet moment. Then he bowed to it, very low, the way you might bow to a wise old teacher — which, of course, is exactly what it was.

"Thank you," he told the tree softly. And a single leaf came spiraling down and landed right on the tip of his nose, which Kuma decided was the tree saying *you're welcome* in the only language trees know.

---

"You know," Kuma said, lying in his leaf pile, looking up through the burning branches at the soft grey sky, "I think I had it backward."

"Mm?" said Maru.

"I thought change took the good things away." A gold leaf landed gently on his nose, and he went a little cross-eyed looking at it. "But summer didn't *take* anything. It just handed everything over to autumn and said, 'Here, your turn, do something amazing.' And autumn did." He gestured at the entire glowing mountain with one small chestnut arm. "Summer could never do *this*. Summer's far too busy being warm. It takes a chilly day and a brave tree to make something this beautiful."

He sat up, scattering leaves, his roasted-warm glow shining brighter than it had all season.

"And winter's coming next, isn't it? After autumn."

"It is," said Maru.

Kuma thought about snow. He thought about steam rising off hot drinks. He thought about being roasted golden on a little cart while the cold wind blew, and warm hands cupping around him, and someone's happy face when they peeled him open to find his soft sweet heart inside. He thought about how a chestnut isn't really *for* summer at all — a chestnut is for the cold, cozy days, when people most need a small warm thing to hold.

"I think," he said slowly, "winter might be *my* turn to show off."

"Now," said Maru, whisking the air gently, "*now* you understand momiji."

---

They walked back down the temple path together as the afternoon went soft and grey-gold around them. The leaves kept letting go above their heads, twirling down one and two and three at a time, and Kuma no longer felt sad to see them fall. Each one was a tiny brave goodbye, and goodbyes, he had decided, could be beautiful if you waved back at them properly.

When they reached the bottom, the old teakettle was still sitting there in the grass, waiting.

Kuma patted its warm copper side. "Thank you," he told it kindly, "for keeping summer safe for me. But I don't need to hide in there anymore." He picked up the single reddest leaf he could find — crisp and perfect and the exact color of his own roasted shell — and tucked it carefully into the folds of his autumn-leaf scarf, right next to his heart.

"To remember," he said, "that the most beautiful thing of all is the thing that's brave enough to change."

And the mountain, all aflame and all aglow, crackled softly in the evening breeze, as if it agreed.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, the leaves outside your window are learning how to let go, and they are not afraid, because they know something secret: every ending is just a doorway holding the next beautiful thing. You are growing and changing too, a little more each day, and that is not something to be sad about — it is the most wonderful magic there is. So snuggle deep, breathe slow like Maru's gentle whisk, and let the soft crackle of the autumn night tuck itself around your dreams. Tomorrow will be new, and new is lovely. Goodnight.
