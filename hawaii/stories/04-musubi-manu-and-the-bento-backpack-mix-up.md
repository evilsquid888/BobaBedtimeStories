# Story 4: Musubi Manu and the Bento Backpack Mix-Up

**Main Character:** Musubi Manu
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A neighborhood plate-lunch shop and the streets of Kaimuki, Oahu
**Theme:** Being dependable is its own kind of brave.
**Hawaiian Spotlight:** ohana (oh-HAH-nah) = family

---

Musubi Manu had exactly one job that morning, and he intended to do it perfectly.

The job was this: deliver six lunches to six friends before the lunch bell rang, and bring back zero complaints. Manu loved a list with zero complaints at the end of it. He stood in the doorway of Auntie's plate-lunch shop, his neat rice-block body squared up, his pink spam blanket smoothed flat, and his crisp black nori seatbelt buckled snug around his middle. On his back sat the most important thing he owned: his little bento backpack, fat and warm and stuffed with six lunches.

"Wrapped up and ready!" he announced to nobody, which is the most reliable kind of person to announce things to. Nobody never argues, never forgets the plan, and never, ever loses a backpack. Manu admired that about Nobody.

He patted the backpack twice. He checked his list twice. He checked that he had checked his list twice, because between you and me, dear reader, there is no cozier feeling in all the world than a list that has been checked one time too many. Then, because the morning was bright and the smell of teriyaki was practically lifting him off the ground, Musubi Manu marched out into the sunny streets of **Kaimuki** with the confidence of a tidy fellow who has never, ever lost anything.

His friends called him the dependable one. Need somebody to remember which friend was allergic to sesame, or which one needed his lunch cut into exactly four squares because three squares was, in his opinion, simply uncivilized? You called Manu. He had never let anyone down, not once, the way a freshly folded blanket is proud of its corners.

He was about to lose something.

---

The first delivery went beautifully, which is exactly how disasters like to begin.

Manu hopped down the sidewalk, past the shave-ice stand and the little flower shop, all the way to the bus bench where his friend Lani the Loco Moco was waiting, sprawled out and yawning like the sidewalk was a giant pillow.

"Howzit, Manu," Lani mumbled, his fried-egg hat tilted sleepily over one eye. "You bring the goods?"

"One musubi plate, extra crispy, just how you like." Manu swung the backpack off his shoulders, unzipped the top pocket, and handed over a warm bundle. "Eat slow. You always eat too fast and then nap on the bench."

"That's the dream, brah," sighed Lani, and he settled in for both, gravy and all.

Manu zipped the pocket, slung the backpack on, and ticked off number one on his list with a tiny, satisfying scribble. One down, five to go. He felt unstoppable. He felt magnificent. He felt, in fact, like the single most organized musubi ever to roll out of a rice cooker.

The trouble was the bench was crowded. Somebody had left an umbrella, a single slipper, and a paper bag of mangoes all in one messy heap, and Manu's tidy soul could not bear to even *look* at a heap. And in the wiggle and shuffle of standing up — careful not to step on Lani's gravy, mind you — Manu set his bento backpack down for one tiny second right on top of the heap. Just one second. To re-buckle his nori seatbelt, which had gone slightly crooked during the wiggle, and a crooked seatbelt simply would not do. Not on a delivery day. Not on *any* day.

You already know what happened. Manu stood up, grabbed a strap, slung it on his back, and marched off feeling wonderfully organized.

He had grabbed the paper bag of mangoes.

---

He didn't notice. That's the thing about being so sure of yourself — sometimes you forget to look. Manu sailed three whole blocks before he reached the banyan tree where Lilikoi Lani — no relation, just two Lanis, it happens — was bouncing on a root, waiting.

"Manu! Manu! Lunch lunch lunch!" zipped the little purple fruit, leaving a tangy-sweet scent trail in loops around his head. "What you bring me? Sour? Sweet? Surprise?"

"Today's surprise," said Manu proudly, "is that there is no surprise. It's exactly what you ordered." He swung the backpack around, unzipped it — and froze.

Mangoes. Six mangoes. Smiling up at him with that smug, round, mango-ish way mangoes have.

"Ooh!" said Lilikoi, peering in. "Bold choice. Very chunky. Very orange. Did everybody decide to eat fruit for lunch and forget to tell me? Because honestly, I respect it."

"This isn't — these aren't —" Manu's whole rice-block body went cold, then went a sort of pale steamed-rice white. His nori seatbelt suddenly felt three sizes too tight. He patted the bag. He flipped it over. He shook it gently, then less gently, then peeked inside again as if six lunches might be cleverly hiding underneath a single mango. They were not. The mangoes just sat there being mangoes, which is the one thing mangoes are truly excellent at.

Somewhere back across Kaimuki, sitting all alone on a sunny bus bench, was his real bento backpack — fat, warm, and stuffed with everybody's lunch.

"I lost it," he whispered. "I lost the lunches."

This was, for a fellow like Musubi Manu, roughly the worst sentence in the world.

---

Now, here is where Manu surprised himself.

A different musubi might have flopped down on the curb and turned into a sad little rice puddle right there on the sidewalk. And I will be honest with you — Manu *wanted* to. His list had a hole in it now, a big mango-shaped hole, and a list with a hole in it felt to Manu like a song with the last note missing.

But he had a job, and the job had not changed one bit just because everything had gone sideways. So instead of flopping, Manu did the bravest thing a tidy fellow can do: he took a deep breath, squared up his shoulders, smoothed his spam blanket flat, and re-buckled his seatbelt good and snug, the way some people tie their shoes tight before a race.

"Lilikoi," he said, "I have to **wikiwiki** (VEE-kee-VEE-kee) = hurry. I have to retrace my whole route and find that backpack before the lunch bell. You in?"

"Am I in?" Lilikoi did three backflips. "Sour first, sweet always — let's GO!"

And off they zipped, the tidy square fellow and the bouncy purple fruit, back through the bright streets of Kaimuki on a wikiwiki rescue mission.

They zipped past the bakery, where the sugar smell nearly knocked them both sideways. Lilikoi bounced off three mailboxes and a fence post out of sheer excitement, calling out "wikiwiki, wikiwiki!" the whole way, because she liked the word and it bounced as nicely as she did.

The first clue waited at the flower shop. Honu Haupia, the gentle coconut-pudding sea turtle, was gliding slowly past the door, her snow-white shell jiggling like soft fresh snow with every unhurried flipper-stroke.

"Manu," she said in her calm, ancient way. "You wear a face like a lost canoe."

"I lost the lunches, Auntie Honu." It tumbled out of him. "I had one job."

Honu blinked her gentle ancient eyes. "Slow flippers, far journeys," she said. "A lost thing is not gone. It is only somewhere you have not looked yet. Go back to where your hands were sure. That is where you set it down."

"Where my hands were sure," Manu repeated. And he knew at once. The bench. The crooked seatbelt. The one tiny second.

---

But when they wikiwiki'd back to the bus bench, the bench was empty. No umbrella. No slipper. No fat warm backpack. Just Lani the Loco Moco, snoring softly with rice on his chin.

"Lani!" Manu shook him gently. "The bag I set down — the heap of stuff — where did it go?"

Lani cracked one yolky eye. "Mmf. Auntie from the flower shop took the umbrella. Mac grabbed his slipper. And Manapua came bouncing by and scooped up a backpack, said it smelled like teriyaki and lunch and he was *starving*—"

"Manapua has the lunches," Manu groaned, both hands on his rice-block cheeks, "and Manapua is the hungriest friend on the *entire island*."

"Soft on the outside, full of love inside," Lani agreed sleepily, scraping up his last grain of rice. "Also full of appetite. That bun once ate a whole tray of malasadas and asked for the menu. Better wikiwiki, brah."

So they did. They wikiwiki'd like the lunch bell was breathing down their necks — which, to be fair, it sort of was. They followed that warm teriyaki smell like a pair of hungry bloodhounds, three streets over, past the shave-ice stand (where Manu did *not* stop, even though it was very hard), around the banyan tree, through a cloud of plumeria, all the way to the little neighborhood park. And there, sitting in the grass with the bento backpack open wide in his lap — and one lunch already half-unwrapped in his soft pillowy hands — was Manapua Mano.

"MANAPUA, WAIT!" Manu hollered, which was the loudest he had ever been about anything.

---

Manapua jumped so high a little wisp of steam puffed out his swirl-knot.

"Manu!" he beamed. "There you are! I found your backpack on the bench and I thought, *somebody's gonna lose this,* so I kept it safe. I was *just* about to deliver them all. After one tiny snack. Maybe two."

Manu looked at the open backpack. He looked at the half-unwrapped lunch. He looked at Manapua's big hopeful, hungry, completely honest face — and something melted in his chest, soft as warm rice.

"You kept them safe," Manu said slowly. "You weren't taking them. You were guarding them."

"Of course!" Manapua looked genuinely shocked. "It's everybody's lunch. **Ohana** (oh-HAH-nah) = family. Nobody's lunch gets left behind! ...I was *gonna* guard that last bite, though, real careful. With my mouth."

And Musubi Manu laughed — a big, wobbly, relieved laugh that rattled his nori seatbelt loose, and for once he let it stay crooked.

---

They delivered the rest together, the whole bouncy parade of them marching through Kaimuki like the world's smallest, most delicious holiday. Poke Pua got her lunch and immediately rearranged everything in it "just so," because she could not help herself and nobody expected her to. Mac the Macadamia poked half out of his glossy shell to take his, whispering a shy "small but mighty, thanks, Manu," before popping right back in. Pua the Pineapple accepted hers like a queen accepting an entire kingdom, adjusting her leaf-crown and declaring it "acceptable, sweetheart, *barely*," which from Pua was practically a hug.

And the half-unwrapped lunch — the one Manapua had so very nearly snacked on? Manu folded the wrapper back up neat as you please, smoothed the corners, marched it right back to the bus bench, and presented it to a very surprised and very delighted Lani the Loco Moco, who had already finished his first lunch and was thrilled beyond words to receive a second. Lani said it tasted twice as good for having gone on an adventure, and then promptly fell back asleep mid-bite, which is the highest compliment a Loco Moco can give.

Zero complaints. The lunch bell rang at the exact same second the last lunch landed in the last pair of hands, the way things sometimes line up when an ohana is pulling together.

Back at Auntie's shop, Manu finally sat down, wrung out and warm. "I thought losing the bag meant I failed," he said. "I'm supposed to be the dependable one. The one who never drops anything."

Honu Haupia, who had glided all the way over to hear the ending, smiled her slow turtle smile. "Dependable does not mean never falling, little one. It means getting up and going back for what matters. You wikiwiki'd across the whole town for six lunches that were never even lost — only loved by somebody else for a while." She nodded. "Being dependable *is* its own kind of brave."

Manu thought about that. Then he re-buckled his nori seatbelt — snug, straight, just right — and grinned.

"Wrapped up and ready," he said softly. "For next time."

## Goodnight Blessing
May your backpack always be a little fuller than you remember, and your friends always a little kinder than you fear. When something feels lost, may you find the bravery to simply go back and look — slow flippers, far journeys. Remember that you do not have to carry every lunch alone, because ohana means nobody, and nothing you love, ever gets left behind. Snuggle in now, tidy and warm and safe, all your worries folded up neat as nori. You did your best today, and your best was more than enough. Goodnight.
