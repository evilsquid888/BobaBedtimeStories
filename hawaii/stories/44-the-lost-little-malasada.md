# Story 44: The Lost Little Malasada

**Main Character:** Malia the Malasada
**Guest Characters:** Musubi Manu, Manapua Mano
**Setting:** A crowded county fair and food-truck festival in Honolulu
**Theme:** When you're lost, kind helpers are everywhere.
**Hawaiian Spotlight:** huli (HOO-lee) = to turn / search / flip

---

Malia the Malasada had never seen so many lights, smells, and silly hats in her whole puffy life. The county fair sparkled like somebody had sneezed glitter all over Honolulu. Ferris wheels spun. Food trucks honked. A man in a coconut suit juggled three pineapples and dropped all of them — *plonk, plonk, plonk* — and bowed anyway, which made Malia laugh so hard a little shower of sugar crystals tumbled off her golden cheeks.

"Stay close, you two!" called Musubi Manu, marching ahead with his tiny bento backpack and his crisp nori seatbelt. "I have a map, a snack schedule, and a backup snack schedule."

"I have a *hungry*," announced Manapua Mano, bouncing so high his steam-knot wobbled. "Does the fair have a bun stand? It should. Every place should."

"There's a poke truck shaped like a *whale*," Manu read off his map. "And a stage with hula dancing, and a pie-eating contest at eight, and—"

"Pie-eating?!" Mano's eyes turned into two perfect circles. "Manu. Manu. That's a *me* contest. I was born for the pie-eating contest."

"You were *steamed* for the pie-eating contest," Malia teased, and the three of them laughed so loudly that a nearby seagull jumped.

And right then — *boom-boom-BOOM* — a marching band of singing musubi rolled by between Malia and her friends like a sudden river of rice. Drums! Cymbals! Tiny rice-marchers high-stepping in perfect rows! Malia clapped along, spinning to watch them pass.

By the time the last little drummer high-stepped by, she turned back around.

Manu and Mano were gone.

Malia was, very suddenly, all alone.

---

Now, a smaller doughnut might have panicked. Malia's lilikoi-custard heart did give one nervous little squeeze. But Malia took a breath that smelled of kettle corn and plumeria, and she remembered what her bakery-grandma always said: *Fresh, warm, and never alone.*

"Okay, Malia," she whispered to herself. "We're not lost. We're just... un-found. I'll **huli** (HOO-lee) — turn and search — until I flip this whole fair upside down and shake my friends right out of it!"

She tried very hard to march bravely. The trouble was that Malia was about as tall as a soup can, and everyone at the fair was a giant compared to her. Knees! Knees everywhere! Big stomping shoes and swishing skirts and a stroller that nearly rolled right over her sugar.

"Excuse me — pardon — coming through — *yikes!*" Malia bounced sideways and bonked gently into a warm, soft leg.

She looked up. And up. And *up*.

It was an auntie selling **leis** (LAY-z) = flower garlands, her arms draped in ropes of orange 'ilima and white pikake. She had the kindest crinkly eyes Malia had ever seen.

"Aloha, little one," the auntie said, crouching down with a soft *creak*. "You look like you lost your 'ohana."

"I did!" Malia's eyes went big and shiny. "There was a band. And rice. And then *poof* — no friends."

The auntie didn't laugh, and she didn't fuss. She just nodded, like losing your friends at a fair was the most normal thing in the world. "Then we will help you huli for them. The first rule of being lost," she said, slipping a tiny pikake bracelet around Malia's wrist, "is to find a helper. And keiki — you already found one. Stand here a moment. Tall people can see far."

She lifted Malia up onto a sturdy wooden crate so she could see over the sea of knees.

---

From up high, the fair was even more enormous — a glowing, blinking, sizzling jungle of food trucks. Malia squinted. "I don't see them. But I smell... hmm." She sniffed the air with her custard-button nose. "Soy sauce! Manu loves soy sauce. He carries little packets in his backpack just in case of *emergencies*."

"A boy with a snack plan," the auntie chuckled. "Follow your nose, then. And if you get stuck, find another helper. There are more kind people here than there are malasadas in the whole island — and that is a *lot*."

Malia hopped down, waved goodbye, and rolled off toward the soy-sauce smell. The bracelet bounced on her wrist like a tiny cheering crowd.

She huli-ed left. She huli-ed right. She wove between flip-flops and ducked under a picnic table where a friendly poi dog wagged its tail and walked her safely to the other side, nudging her along with its cold nose. "Mahalo, doggie!" Malia called. The dog gave one happy *boof* and trotted off.

Along the way, the island kept right on helping her. A keiki dropped his shave ice and started to cry, and a stranger bought him a new one before his tears even hit the ground. A grandpa helped a lady carry her huge box of malasadas — Malia's *cousins!* — and she gave him three for free, calling him a sweetheart. Everywhere Malia turned, somebody was being kind to somebody else, easy as breathing, like the whole island was knit together with invisible warm string.

*Maybe that's why nobody here is ever really lost*, Malia thought. *There's always somebody reaching out a hand.*

But the soy-sauce smell led only to a saimin truck — no Manu in sight. Malia's shoulders drooped. A single sugar crystal slid down her cheek like a tiny shiny tear.

"Whoa, whoa, no crying allowed — you'll get all soggy!" said a voice.

Malia turned. A girl was leaning out of the saimin truck window, hair in two big buns, a ladle in one hand. "You looking for somebody, yeah?"

"My friends," Malia hiccuped. "A square rice guy and a bouncy bun guy."

"Ho, easy. Climb up." The girl set Malia gently on the truck counter, right beside a basket of warm, jiggly fishcake. "From up here, I see the *whole* festival. You watch the people, I'll watch the trucks. We huli together." She handed Malia a paper umbrella, the kind that goes in fancy drinks. "For pointing. Detectives need a pointer."

Malia gripped her umbrella like a sword. She felt brave again. *That's the thing about helpers*, she thought. *Each one hands you a little more courage.*

They scanned and scanned. A clown floated by on stilts. A man won a giant stuffed honu and tripped over it. Two aunties argued cheerfully over the best malasada recipe, which made Malia feel oddly proud. The saimin girl narrated it all like a sports announcer, and Malia laughed until her sugar fizzled.

Then the saimin girl gasped and dropped her ladle. "Eh! By the shave-ice line — you see one square fella standing on his tippy-toes? Looks like he's looking for somebody too?"

Malia's whole body fizzed with hope. "MANU!"

---

But the festival was tricky. By the time Malia thanked the saimin girl and scrambled down, the crowd had shifted again, and the shave-ice line was just a long wiggle of strangers. No square Manu anywhere.

Malia stood very still in the middle of it all, the noise swirling around her like syrup. For one wobbly second, she felt smaller than a sesame seed.

Then she remembered the auntie's words. *Find a helper.*

So she looked — really looked — and saw a fair worker in a bright orange vest holding a clipboard and a microphone, standing by a big lighted sign that said LOST & FOUND.

"Oh!" Malia gasped. "A *finding* booth! For *lost* things! That's *me*!"

She marched right up. "Excuse me, sir," she said, lifting her paper umbrella politely. "I am one lost malasada, and I would like to be found, please."

The fair worker grinned the warmest grin. "Well now. We can do that." He crouched down. "What's your name, and who you looking for?"

"Malia! And I'm looking for Musubi Manu — he's square and tidy and very brave — and Manapua Mano, who is soft and bouncy and *always* hungry."

The fair worker lifted his microphone. His voice boomed across the entire festival, big as the sky:

*"ATTENTION, OHANA! We get one sweet little malasada named MALIA at the Lost and Found, looking for her friends! Calling all square rice boys and bouncy buns — your girl is HERE and she is SAFE!"*

The whole fair seemed to pause. And then, from somewhere far off, two voices shouted back at once:

"MALIA?!"

"MALIAAA! We been h-- *whoa!* -- huli-ing everywhere for you!"

---

Through the crowd they came — Musubi Manu running so fast his nori seatbelt flapped, and Manapua Mano bouncing like a runaway dumpling, leaving little puffs of steam behind him. They crashed into Malia in a big, warm, squishy hug that smelled of rice and pork and sugar all at once.

"We turned the festival inside out looking for you!" Manu panted. "I made a search grid! I asked *eleven* aunties!"

"I asked a churro stand if they'd seen you," Mano admitted, "but mostly I just bought a churro. I was *worried-eating*. It's a real thing." He patted his pillowy belly. "I worried-ate two."

"You worried-ate *four*," Manu corrected, "I counted." But he was smiling so wide his nori band crinkled, because being grumpy at jokes is impossible when you've just found someone you love.

Malia laughed so hard that a whole sparkly cloud of sugar puffed off her, raining down on her friends like confetti. "I wasn't really lost," she said, hugging them tighter. "I was *being found* — by lots and lots of helpers." She showed them her pikake bracelet, and her paper umbrella, and pointed back toward the lei auntie, the saimin girl, and the kind fair worker, who all waved.

Manapua Mano sniffled happily. "That's the best part of the island, yeah? You're never *really* alone."

"Never," rumbled the fair worker, handing each of them a warm cocoa. "On these islands, every stranger is just 'ohana you haven't met yet."

And Malia, snuggled safe between her two best friends, with cocoa warming her custard heart, decided that was the truest, coziest thing she'd heard all night.

Before they left, Malia ran back through the crowd one last time — but this time she wasn't lost, she was *thanking*. She gave the lei auntie a hug that left a sprinkle of sugar on her flowers. She waved her paper umbrella at the saimin girl, who saluted with her ladle. And she gave the fair worker the very last warm malasada from her own little pocket, which made him grin so big his orange vest practically glowed.

"You take care now, keiki," he said. "And next time you huli for someone — you be the helper, yeah?"

"I will," Malia promised, sugar shining on her cheeks. "I'll find every lost little snack on this whole island and walk them home."

They walked home together as the Ferris wheel turned slow and golden against the dark, and Malia held both her friends' hands so tight that not even a whole marching band of musubi could ever pull them apart again.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, if you ever feel un-found in a big, busy world, remember sweet Malia and her brave little huli. Take a breath, look around, and you will see them — the helpers, the kind eyes, the gentle hands waiting to walk you home. You are never as alone as you feel, for the whole world is full of 'ohana you simply haven't met yet. So close your eyes now, warm and sugar-dusted and safe, and let the soft night carry you. Goodnight.
