# Story 6: Saimin Sam and the Great Noodle Knot

**Main Character:** Saimin Sam (a steaming bowl of saimin noodle soup)
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A steamy little saimin stand near a Honolulu plantation town, on the night of a noodle slurp-off
**Theme:** Owning a mistake makes you bigger, not smaller.
**Hawaiian Spotlight:** kala mai (KAH-lah MY) = excuse me / I'm sorry

---

Tucked between a banyan tree and a wobbly bus stop, where warm steam curled into the night like sleepy little ghosts, sat the happiest saimin stand on the whole island — and inside the very biggest bowl on the counter lived Saimin Sam, who was, at this exact moment, showing off so hard he nearly slurped himself.

Sam was a wide blue bowl of golden broth, with curly wheat noodles that floated like ribbons of sunshine. A pink-and-white slice of fish cake made his smile. Two green-onion bits sat on his rim like eyebrows — extremely useful, since Sam had a great many expressions and used most of them per minute. And in the middle bobbed a soft-boiled egg cheek that blushed pink whenever he felt proud.

Tonight, that egg cheek was the pinkest pink you ever saw.

"Slurp-off champion," Sam hummed, swirling his noodles into a fancy loop. "That's gonna be me."

---

It was the night of the Great Slurp-Off, the slurpiest, steamiest night of the whole year, when noodle bowls from every corner of the plantation town gathered at the stand to find out who had the longest, loopiest, most beautifully *slurpable* noodles in all of Honolulu.

Here is how it worked: when Auntie rang her little brass bell, the hungriest slurper in town would lean over the winning bowl and pull the noodles up in one long, glorious *SHLOOORP*. The swirliest, slipperiest, most slurp-able bowl took the crown — which around here was a kiss of extra green onion right on top. To a soup bowl, that is basically royalty.

Sam wanted that crown so badly his broth bubbled.

"Long and loopy, smooth and soupy," he sang, swishing away. "Nobody slurps like Saimin Sam!"

The other bowls on the counter adored him. There was Wonton Winnie, plump and giggly, with dumplings that wiggled when she laughed and bobbed when she didn't. There was a quiet old ramen bowl, slow and steamy and wise, who had watched forty slurp-offs from the back of the counter and never once won a single one, but who clapped the loudest every time anyway. And there was a tiny udon bowl no bigger than a teacup, who bounced on her saucer with so much excitement that little drops of broth kept hopping over her rim.

"You're gonna win, Sam!" cheered the little udon bowl. "You always have the curliest noodles!"

"I know," said Sam, twirling. His eyebrows did the *I am magnificent* wiggle. "But curly isn't enough. They have to be *perfect.*"

"They look perfect already," said Wonton Winnie.

"Almost," said Sam. "Just one more swish."

---

The trouble started, as trouble so often does, with *just one more swish.*

"A little more swirl," Sam told himself, stirring. "A little more loop. To make them PERFECT."

*Swish. Swirl. Loop. Swish.*

But here is the secret about noodles that Saimin Sam, in all his showing off, had forgotten: the more you stir them, the more they tangle. And the more Sam swirled, the bigger his loops grew, until — *gloop* — two noodles crossed. Then four. Then, before he could say "saimin," forty.

"Hm," said Sam.

He stirred faster, trying to fix it. This was a bit like trying to put out a campfire with a bucket of extra campfire. The faster he swished, the worse it got, and the worse it got, the harder he panicked, and the harder he panicked the *faster he swished*, round and round and round.

By the time he finally stopped, every single noodle in his bowl had knotted itself into one enormous, soggy, hopeless tangle — a great golden noodle-ball the size of a beach hat, with his fish-cake smile stuck somewhere in the middle and one lonely green onion poking out the top like a tiny flag of surrender.

The whole counter went quiet.

"Whoa," whispered Wonton Winnie. "That's the biggest knot I ever saw."

"That's the biggest knot *anyone* ever saw," breathed the udon bowl, in a voice that was half horror and half admiration.

Saimin Sam's egg cheek went pale instead of pink.

---

*Ding!*

That was Auntie testing her brass bell. "Slurp-Off in ten minutes!" she called, wiping her hands on her apron. "Who's the swirliest bowl tonight?"

Ten minutes. TEN MINUTES. And Saimin Sam had noodles you couldn't slurp with a fishing net.

Now, in the corner of the saimin stand, the warm trade wind was drifting through the open window, jingling the little doorbell. And right then, a sneaky, slippery, easy little thought tiptoed into Sam's bowl.

*You could say the wind did it.*

Oh, how that thought sparkled. It would be so simple. "The wind blew in off the cane fields and tangled all my noodles!" Everyone would believe him. Nobody would ever know he'd swished and swirled them into a knot all by his proud, showing-off self. He'd still be the champion. His egg cheek could go right back to pink. The crown could still be his.

Sam opened his fish-cake mouth to say it.

"The wind—" he began.

But the words tasted wrong. They tasted like cold broth and yesterday's soggy noodles. Because Sam knew — he absolutely, completely knew — that the wind had not touched a single noodle. *He* had. His own swishing. His own swirling. His own can't-stop-fixing-it, must-be-perfect, look-at-me self.

He looked at the lonely little green onion poking up out of his giant knot, waving in the breeze, and he let all his steam out in one long, shaky sigh.

He thought about how saying *the wind did it* would feel for one short minute — and how it would feel for every long minute after, a cold lie sitting heavy at the bottom of his bowl forever.

---

"...didn't do it," Sam finished quietly.

He sat up as tall as a tangled bowl can sit. "**Kala mai** (KAH-lah MY) = excuse me, everybody. I'm sorry. The wind didn't tangle my noodles." His green-onion eyebrows drooped low. "*I* did. I kept swirling and swirling because I wanted them perfect, and I wanted to win, and I made the worst knot in the entire history of soup. All by myself."

For a long second, nobody said anything. The bell jingled. The steam curled. Somewhere, a fly buzzed.

Saimin Sam squeezed his eyes shut and waited to feel small. He waited to feel like the silliest bowl on the whole counter. He waited to shrink right down and disappear into his own broth.

But that is not what happened. Not even close.

Wonton Winnie scooted closer, her dumplings wobbling. "Brah," she said warmly. "You know how *hard* it is to say that out loud? My cousin once blamed a knot on a passing dog. A *dog.* There wasn't even a dog."

"I once blamed my soggy noodles on the moon," admitted the old ramen bowl.

"I blamed mine on Tuesday!" piped the udon bowl.

And then the whole stand was laughing — not *at* Sam, but *with* him, the warm kind of laugh that wraps around you like a blanket fresh from the dryer. Sam felt his egg cheek go pink again. Not embarrassed-pink. The good kind. The honest kind.

"Telling the truth when it's all tangly," said Wonton Winnie, "is harder than any slurp-off. You just won the bravest thing in the whole room."

---

"Okay," said Saimin Sam, sniffling a happy sniffle. "But I *still* have the world's biggest noodle knot. And the Slurp-Off starts in eight minutes."

That was when the most wonderful thing happened.

Not a single bowl went back to practicing their own noodles. Instead, every last one of them leaned in to help untangle *Sam's.*

"Pull there!" cried Winnie, tugging a loop with a dumpling.

"No — *under* there," said the old ramen bowl, gently lifting a strand.

"*Mmf,*" grunted the udon bowl, who had slipped right inside the middle of the knot and was pushing with all her tiny might. "It's like a noodle maze in here! I think I see the exit! Or my own face!"

They laughed the entire time. They laughed when the fish cake popped free and bonked the udon bowl right on the head. They laughed when a sprung noodle slapped Sam's eyebrow back into place, perfectly straight, as if it had never been gone. They laughed when the old ramen bowl pulled one wrong strand and the whole knot tightened with a *boing* and everybody groaned and then laughed even harder. They laughed so hard the broth in every bowl sloshed like a tiny ocean, and the steam danced, and the doorbell jingled along like applause, and even Auntie peeked over to see what all the giggling was about.

And here is the warm, glowing, honest thing: with every single loop they freed, Saimin Sam felt *bigger.* Not smaller. Bigger — as if his bowl was growing wider from the inside, filling up with something even warmer than broth.

Loop by loop, slurp by slurp, the great knot came undone at last. They draped Sam's freed noodles back into his golden broth, long and gleaming and curlier than ever — because, it turns out, a knot that has been loved untangled comes out the loopiest of all.

"Long and loopy, smooth and soupy," the whole stand sang together, "nobody slurps like Saimin Sam!"

---

The Great Slurp-Off, when it finally happened, was a glorious slurpy mess of broth and giggles and flying green onions. And do you know who won?

It does not matter even one little bit. Because by then, Saimin Sam had already won the only thing worth winning. He had felt that cold, slippery temptation to blame the wind — and he had spat it right out and told the warm, true thing instead. He had been sure that owning up would shrink him down to nothing.

Instead, he felt as wide and full and steaming as the biggest bowl on the whole counter.

"You know what's funny?" Sam said to the udon bowl, as the stand began to close and the steam went soft and sleepy. "I thought owning up would make me a smaller bowl."

"And?" the udon bowl asked, yawning a tiny yawn.

"I think," said Saimin Sam, his egg cheek glowing pink and proud, "I'm the biggest one on the counter now."

## Goodnight Blessing
Little keiki, everybody makes a tangle sometimes — a knot of noodles, a knot of mistakes, a knot that is nobody's fault but your very own. And there will always be a slippery little voice that whispers, *blame the wind.* But you are braver than that voice. When you say **kala mai** — I'm sorry, it was me — you do not get smaller. You get bigger, warmer, truer, like a wide bowl of soup on a cool island night. So rest easy, honest one. Whatever knots tomorrow may bring, you can always tell the truth and let your friends help you loop them loose. Steam gently. Dream softly. Goodnight.
