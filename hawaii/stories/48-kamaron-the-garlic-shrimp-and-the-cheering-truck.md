# Story 48: Kamaron the Garlic Shrimp and the Cheering Truck

**Main Character:** Kamaron the Garlic Shrimp
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A North Shore beach-games day beside the famous garlic-shrimp trucks
**Theme:** Cheering someone else across the finish line is its own kind of winning.
**Hawaiian Spotlight:** ho'omaika'i (hoh-oh-my-KAH-ee) = to congratulate / to praise

---

Kamaron the Garlic Shrimp had won eleven races before breakfast, and he was beginning to suspect that nobody had noticed.

He stood at the starting line of race number twelve, curled and uncurled his springy pink tail, and waited for the whistle. Kamaron was a sight to behold — a plump, glistening shrimp the warm sunset-orange of a perfectly fried prawn, dusted head to tail in golden-brown garlic crumbs that sparkled in the sun like glitter that smelled absolutely delicious. He had two bouncy antennae that swept straight back when he ran, ten busy little legs that blurred into a fan, and a tail so springy it could fling him down the beach like a flat stone skipped across the water.

"On your marks!" called the race judge, an old wobbly turtle who judged everything slowly and fairly.

The whistle blew. Kamaron *fwipped* his tail.

He won before most of the racers had finished sneezing off the starting line.

"Eleven and one!" he announced to the crowd, panting and proud. "I mean — twelve and zero!"

The crowd was looking the other way.

---

The North Shore Beach Games happened once a year, right beside the famous garlic-shrimp trucks, where the air was thick and buttery and the lines of hungry beachgoers wound clear down to the water. There were sandcastle contests and coconut bowling and a wiggling, giggling line of races down the hot golden sand. And Kamaron, the fastest snack on the whole beach, entered every single one.

And won every single one.

The trouble was the cheering.

Because the cheering — the *real* cheering, the kind that shook the palm trees and scattered the **manu** (MAH-noo) = birds right off the lifeguard tower — never came when Kamaron crossed the line first. It came much, much later. It came for the *last* finisher.

Take race number twelve. Kamaron had been resting in the shade, fanning himself with a ti leaf, for a good long while before the slowest racer of all — a little melting cone of shave ice — finally came puddling across the finish line, dead last, dripping rainbow all the way.

And the crowd went *bananas*.

"YOU DID IT! YOU DID IT!" they roared. They lifted the soggy little cone on their shoulders. They draped it in a flower lei. Somebody started a chant. Somebody else cried happy tears into a paper boat of shrimp.

Kamaron blinked his big shrimp eyes. "But," he said quietly, to nobody, "I won that one." He raised one little garlicky claw. "Twelve minutes ago. I'm — I'm right here."

A small girl walked past, licking garlic butter off her fingers. "You smell *amazing*," she told him kindly. Then she ran off to hug the puddle.

"Thank you," Kamaron called after her, a little too late. "I — I also *won*, by the way. The whole race. First place. There was a ribbon and everything." But she was already gone, and the ribbon, when Kamaron checked, had been quietly taken down and used to wrap a birthday present for somebody's grandmother. Which is a perfectly lovely thing to do with a ribbon, and Kamaron told himself he didn't mind one bit, and only mostly believed it.

---

By race number nineteen, Kamaron had a trophy collection so tall it cast a shade you could nap in, and a feeling in his middle he couldn't quite name. It wasn't hunger. It was emptier than hunger. It was the strange, hollow ache of standing at a finish line all by yourself while everyone else stands somewhere far more interesting.

He won the sack race. *(Empty.)* He won the three-legged race, which is genuinely impressive when you have ten legs and have to tie nine of them up. *(Empty.)* He won the watermelon roll, the limbo-under-the-surfboard, and the very-serious-walking race, in which the rule is you must look extremely serious. *(Empty, empty, and somehow the emptiest of all.)*

Every time, the same thing. He'd cross the line first to a polite little patter of claps, like rain that couldn't be bothered. Then he'd wait. And wait. And the moment the *slowest* racer struggled, wheezed, and flopped over the finish line — *that* was when the beach exploded.

Kamaron sat down on his tallest trophy and watched another roar erupt for somebody who'd come in thirtieth. Thirtieth! Out of thirty! That, Kamaron thought, was the *opposite* of winning. That was so far from winning it had practically circled all the way back around to losing and kept going. And yet there it was — the loudest cheer of the entire afternoon — for a coconut who was very proud simply to have remained, more or less, a coconut throughout.

"What," he muttered, "is everybody *cheering* for? They lost. I won. Winning is the whole entire point of a race. It says so. Right there. In the word *race*." He paused. "I think."

"You watch the wrong thing," said a calm voice beside him.

It was Musubi Manu, the tidy little rice-block fellow, who had not entered a single race because he found racing untidy and preferred to hand out water cups in perfect rows.

"What do you mean, *wrong thing*?" said Kamaron.

Manu handed him a water cup, lined up just so. "You watch the finish line," he said. "Every race, your eyes are glued to that ribbon. You want to be the first thing to touch it." He nodded out at the cheering crowd, at the rainbow puddle being hugged for the ninth time. "But look at *them*, Kamaron. They're not watching the line at all."

Kamaron looked.

And — oh. *Oh.*

They weren't. Not one of them. The whole crowd had its back to the finish ribbon. They were watching *each other*. Watching the slow ones puff and the tired ones grin and the little ones trip and get scooped up by bigger ones. They were watching faces, not finishes. They cheered when somebody who thought they couldn't, *did*.

"You cross the line alone," Manu said gently, stacking another cup. "So nobody gets to cross it *with* you. That's all. That's the whole sad little riddle of it." And then, because he was a tidy fellow with a route to walk, he picked up his tray of water cups and went off to line them up somewhere else, leaving Kamaron to chew it over by himself.

---

Kamaron sat with that for a long, quiet while, listening to a roar that was not for him and — for the very first time — not minding one bit.

Because he was beginning to understand something. The fun was never in the *front*. The fun was in the *middle*, in the warm jostling tangle of everybody, where you cheered and got cheered and nobody stood alone on a trophy in the shade.

Then a conch shell sounded across the sand for the final event of the day.

"The Grand Relay!" called the old turtle judge. "Last race! *Everybody* runs!"

The Grand Relay was the biggest, silliest race of the Beach Games — a great wobbling chain of friends, each one passing a coconut to the next, all the way down the beach to the garlic-shrimp truck at the finish. Everyone lined up: the dripping shave-ice cone, a hearty loco-moco bowl, a queenly pineapple, a tiny macadamia nut tucked deep in his shell, and right at the front, where he always put himself, springy and gleaming and faster than all of them put together — Kamaron.

"Kamaron's on the team!" somebody groaned. "Well, *they're* gonna win."

And Kamaron, for one tiny garlicky second, felt the old pull. *Win it. Fwip the tail. Be first. Touch the ribbon.* He could do it in his sleep. He could win it for them all and add one more trophy to the shade-casting pile.

But the pile cast such a lonely shade.

So when the whistle blew, Kamaron did the most surprising thing he had ever done.

He *stopped*.

---

He let the coconut come to him — and then he *didn't run with it*.

Instead, he turned around, faced his whole wobbling team, and started to *cheer*.

"GO, little nut, GO! Pass it! PASS IT! Pineapple — you majestic queen — RUN like you're late for your own coronation! Rainbow cone! Don't you melt on me now — you've GOT this!"

He bounced. He hollered. He flung his garlic crumbs into the air like confetti. He scooped up the sleepy loco moco when he stumbled and pointed him at the finish and *pushed*, gravy sloshing, egg wobbling, the whole bowl giggling all the way. He ran backward beside each friend in turn, shouting their name so loud the manu scattered off the lifeguard tower all over again, only this time the birds were cheering too. A lifeguard joined in. Then the lady selling shave ice. Then the entire line outside the garlic-shrimp truck, paper boats and all, until the loudest sound on the whole North Shore was a hundred voices roaring for the slowest team in the race.

And the most astonishing thing happened.

His friends ran *faster*. Not because Kamaron was fast — but because somebody, the fastest somebody on the whole beach, had decided *they* were the ones worth racing for. The shave-ice cone grinned so hard it nearly melted on purpose. The pineapple held her leaf-crown on with one hand and *flew*. And the tiny macadamia nut, who always came in last, who was used to being cheered only at the very end out of kindness — heard his name screamed at the very *start*, and the *middle*, and every single tumbling step.

"Almost there, little nut! ALMOST THERE!" Kamaron roared, galloping backward at his side. "**Ho'omaika'i** (hoh-oh-my-KAH-ee) = to congratulate — I'm gonna ho'omaika'i you so loud the whole island hears it!"

The little nut crossed the line.

The team had not come in first. They had come in dead, dead last.

And the beach *erupted*.

---

This time, Kamaron was right in the middle of it.

Not on a trophy. Not in the shade. Not alone. He was buried in the warm, sandy, garlicky, laughing pile of his friends, all of them tumbled together at the finish line, all of them shouting each other's names, and somewhere underneath it all was Kamaron, getting hugged and squished and ho'omaika'i'd from every direction at once.

"You gave up the win," puffed the little macadamia nut, peeking out of his shell at him. "Why?"

Kamaron grinned, his garlic crumbs all rumpled and glittering and absolutely covered in sand.

"Because I figured out which kind of winning counts," he said. "First across the line is the kind you do alone. But cheering somebody *else* across — that's the kind you do *together*." He wiggled all ten of his legs happily in the warm pile. "And turns out together is the only finish line worth crossing."

He had won eleven races before breakfast, and dozens more before dinner, and not one of them had felt like this — like being exactly, warmly, gloriously where he belonged.

The trophies cast a lonely shade.

But the pile of friends? The pile was the brightest, busiest, best-smelling place on the entire North Shore. And Kamaron, the fastest snack on the beach, had finally come in last, and found out it felt like first.

## Goodnight Blessing
May you always find the warm middle of the pile, where the cheering is loudest and nobody stands alone. When you are fast, may you slow down for a friend; when you are tired, may a friend slow down for you. Remember that the finest finish line is not the one you cross first, but the one you cross *together*, with somebody shouting your name. Tonight, let go of the race. You do not have to be first to be loved — you already are, just as you are, garlic crumbs and all. Snuggle down now, soft and warm and proud of the kindness you carried today. Ho'omaika'i, little one. You did so well. Goodnight.
