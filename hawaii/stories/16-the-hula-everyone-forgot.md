# Story 16: The Hula Everyone Forgot

**Main Character:** Honu Haupia
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** A grassy hula halau (school) near the beach at dusk
**Theme:** Stories can be told with your whole body, not just words.
**Hawaiian Spotlight:** hula (HOO-lah) = Hawaiian story-dance

---

The trouble started when Manapua Mano tried to dance with his eyes closed and bounced straight into a coconut tree.

"OW! I mean — TA-DA!" he squeaked, steam puffing from his swirl-knot as he wobbled in a circle. "Did everybody see my graceful tree hug?"

"We saw it, Mano," said Niu the Coconut from where he'd rolled, since the tree was, in fact, Niu's cousin. "The whole beach saw it."

The gang had gathered on the soft green grass of the **hula** (HOO-lah) = Hawaiian story-dance school, right where the lawn met the sand. The sky was turning the color of a ripe mango, and the waves whispered *shhh, shhh* like they were trying to settle everyone down for the night. It almost worked. It did not work on Manapua Mano.

"I want to learn a dance," he announced, "that is mostly snacks."

And that — wobbly tree hug and all — is exactly when Honu Haupia arrived.

She came gliding across the grass slow as a moonrise, her snow-white coconut-pudding shell jiggling gently with every step, each soft square of haupia catching the last gold light. Her ancient eyes were warm. She moved like she was still underwater, like the whole world was an ocean and she had all the time in the tide.

"Aloha, little ones," she said. "Tonight I will teach you a hula that almost no one remembers anymore. A dance so old that the islands themselves were young when it was new."

"Is it a SNACK dance?" asked Manapua Mano, hopefully.

"It is a *story*," said Honu Haupia, with a slow turtle smile. "Slow flippers, far journeys. Watch."

She lifted one soft flipper toward the darkening sea and swept it across the air in a long, gentle curve. Then she rocked her wobbly shell side to side, side to side, like a little white boat. She raised both flippers high and let them flutter down like falling rain.

The gang stared.

"...What did it say?" whispered Niu.

"That," said Honu Haupia, "is the part I have forgotten."

Now, here is a thing the warm narrator will tell you, just between us: there is nothing quite so delicious as a grown-up admitting they don't know something. The whole gang leaned in close.

"The hula tells a true thing about our islands," Honu Haupia explained, her voice slow and gentle as syrup. "Long ago, the **kupuna** (koo-POO-nah) = elders danced it to remember how our home came to be. Every sway, every step, every flutter — each one is a word. But the dance is so old that the *meaning* of the words drifted away, like a canoe with no paddle. I remember the steps. I have forgotten the story they tell."

"So it's a dance," said Niu slowly, "with the words rubbed off."

"Exactly so, keiki," Honu Haupia said. (*Keiki* — KAY-kee — means children, and yes, that's all of them, even the ones shaped like lunch.)

Manapua Mano gasped. "Then WE'LL figure it out! We're great at figuring things out!"

This was not strictly true. The last thing the gang had figured out was how to get Niu unstuck from a beach umbrella, and it had taken until lunch. But Honu Haupia only nodded, because elders know that being *willing* matters more than being *good*.

"Then let us begin," she said. "Slowly. Always slowly. The ocean is never in a hurry, and neither is a story worth telling."

And so the lesson began — and oh, my little reader, it was a *disaster*, in the very best and gigglest way.

Honu Haupia swept her flipper in that long, slow curve. The gang copied her.

Niu, being round, simply rolled. He rolled in a big slow circle and ended up facing the wrong way entirely, blinking at a hibiscus bush. "Am I doing it?"

"You are doing *a* thing," said Honu Haupia kindly.

Manapua Mano tried to sway side to side like a little boat. But Manapua Mano was a pillowy steamed bun, and pillowy steamed buns do not sway — they *bounce*. *Boing. Boing. Boing.* He bounced higher and higher until he popped a tiny puff of steam and landed in Niu's woven hat.

"I FOUND A SEAT!" he called from inside the hat.

"That is not part of the dance," said Honu Haupia.

"It should be."

But the funniest of all was the flutter — the part where Honu Haupia raised her flippers high and let them drift down soft as rain. The whole gang reached up high, oh-so-graceful, ready to float their arms down like falling water...

...and froze.

Because not a single one of them could agree on what came next, so they all just stood there, arms up, like a row of very confused candy reaching for cookies on a high shelf. The wind blew. A gecko walked by. Somebody's tummy growled. (It was Mano's.)

Honu Haupia laughed — a slow, deep, rumbly turtle laugh that wobbled her whole pudding shell. "We are getting it all wrong," she said warmly. "And that is wonderful."

"It IS?" said Niu, still upside down somewhere in the hibiscus.

"When I dance it perfectly," said Honu Haupia, "I dance it like a memory — careful, copying, alone. But just now? When Mano bounced and Niu rolled and everyone reached for the sky together? *That* felt alive." Her ancient eyes shone. "Perhaps the story was never meant to be remembered by one old turtle. Perhaps it was always meant to be danced by *everyone*."

She thought for a long, slow moment, the way only a haupia turtle can.

"Let us not try to remember the old words," she said at last. "Let us tell the story with our *whole bodies* — and let the meaning find us."

So that's what they did.

"The long curve," said Honu Haupia, sweeping her flipper. "What does it feel like?"

"The ocean!" said Manapua Mano. "Going *whooooosh* all the way to where the sky sits down!"

"Good. Now the rocking — side to side, side to side."

"A canoe!" said Niu, who had finally rolled himself right-side up. "Brave little voyagers paddling across the whole big sea to find a new home!"

Honu Haupia's whole shell jiggled with delight. "Yes. *Yes.* And the flutter — the arms drifting down soft as rain?"

Everyone went quiet. Even the waves seemed to hush.

"...The rain," said Mano softly, all his bounce gone gentle. "The first rain falling on a brand-new island. Making everything green. Making it home."

And do you know — the moment he said it, the dance *did* find them. Honu Haupia swept her flipper, and they all whooshed the ocean together. She rocked her shell, and they paddled their brave canoe across the dusk. She raised her flippers high, and down, down, down came the soft warm rain — Mano's arms, and Niu's stubby coconut nubs, and a whole row of swaying snacks, all telling the very same story without a single word between them.

The story of how the islands were found. The story of how home is something you *sail* toward, and *plant*, and *grow*. The story everyone forgot — told brand new, by a gang who couldn't dance a lick and did it anyway.

"We're TELLING it!" Manapua Mano whispered, amazed, mid-sway. "We're telling a whole story and my mouth isn't even moving! That's the best kind! It saves my mouth for snacks!"

Honu Haupia laughed her slow rumbly laugh, and the gang laughed too, and somehow they kept right on dancing through the laughing — wobbly and clumsy and bouncing and rolling and absolutely, perfectly *together* — while the sky went from mango to plum to deep soft velvet blue.

When at last they finished, arms drifting down one final time like the gentlest rain, nobody spoke for a moment. The sea whispered *shhh, shhh*. The first star blinked open over the water.

"Tutu Honu," said Niu quietly — *Tutu* is what you call a beloved grandparent — "is that how the old kupuna felt? When they danced it?"

Honu Haupia gazed out at the dark blue sea, the place all the islands came from, the place she'd been gliding toward her whole long gentle life.

"I think," she said softly, "they felt exactly like this. Tired in the legs. Full in the heart. And not the tiniest bit alone."

And the gang — wobbly, clumsy, snack-shaped, and warm — agreed that this was the very best way a story could possibly feel.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, the sleepiest stories are the ones told without a single word — a hug that says *I love you*, a yawn that says *it's time*, a hand held in the dark that says *I'm right here*. So sway slow like a canoe on a gentle sea, and let your dreams drift down soft as the first warm rain on a brand-new island. You don't have to dance it perfectly. You only have to dance it together, and you already do, every single day. Slow flippers, far journeys, sweet keiki — the whole big ocean of tomorrow is waiting, and it is not in any hurry at all. Goodnight.
