# Story 12: Poi Popo and the Old Island Stories

**Main Character:** Poi Popo
**Guest Characters:** Manapua Mano; Pua the Pineapple; Mac the Macadamia
**Setting:** Under a banyan tree at the family luau, evening
**Theme:** Where we come from is part of who we are.
**Hawaiian Spotlight:** **aloha aina** (ah-LOH-hah EYE-nah) = love of the land

---

The luau was winding down, which meant one thing to the keiki: Story O'Clock.

"SCARY stories!" said Manapua Mano, bouncing so hard steam shot out of his swirl-knot.

"PRINCESS stories," said Pua the Pineapple. "With a crown. Specifically, my crown."

Mac the Macadamia peeked out of his shell, said nothing, and clacked shut again.

Across the grass, slow as a purple cloud, came Poi Popo, flower lei swaying.

"Tonight," she said, "the oldest story of all. Where we come from."

---

The banyan was the grandparent of trees, roots dropping from its branches like extra legs.

Poi Popo settled among the roots with a soft pomf. The keiki tucked in like ducklings.

"Popo," whispered Mac, "is it a true story?"

"The truest," said Poi Popo. "It begins long ago, before poi, before even me."

"How old ARE you?" blurted Manapua.

"Older than this tree," said Poi Popo. Manapua's steam went WHOA.

---

Then a cool wind came whispering through the banyan branches. WHOOSH.

It lifted Poi Popo's lei clean off and tumbled it into the dark beyond the torches.

"My own popo wove that lei," said Poi Popo softly. "I wear it every luau."

The keiki looked at the dark. The dark looked very wide.

"I'm not scared," said Pua. "I'm just crown-conserving." She hid behind Manapua.

Clik. Mac's shell opened. He hopped off the root. "I'll find it." Nobody was more surprised than Mac.

---

First place: the **lo'i** (LOH-ee) = taro patch, cool and muddy, leaves like big green hearts.

Manapua bounced in to help and sank to his swirl-knot. "I'm a mud bun now. It's fine."

Pua tugged him out by the swirl, and the mud said SHLORP, and let go.

No lei. Only taro leaves nodding, as if they knew.

---

Second place: the shore, where the **moana** (moh-AH-nah) = ocean breathed against the reef.

Mac hopped along the wet sand, shell open, listening. A wave tickled his feet. He squeaked.

"Found it!" cried Pua, holding up a strand of seaweed. She wore it anyway. "Royal. Obviously."

No lei. But the dark, Mac noticed, was full of soft sounds. A gecko. A far ukulele.

"That's not the dark being scary," said Mac. "That's the dark being cozy."

---

Third place: one droopy little taro, standing alone at the edge of the lo'i.

And there, hung on its biggest leaf like a prize, was Poi Popo's lei.

"The droopiest plant in the whole patch," breathed Pua, "caught it."

Mac lifted it down with one tiny arm. "Mahalo, brother," he said, not sure why.

---

Back under the banyan, Mac set the lei on Poi Popo's bowed grey head.

"You found it on the taro," she said. "Then you found the story too."

"The first taro was a droopy little sprout named Haloa," said Poi Popo. "He had a little brother."

"The taro held the soil. The brother pulled the weeds. They took care of each other."

"From that taro came every taro. And from taro came me."

"That is **aloha aina** (ah-LOH-hah EYE-nah) = love of the land. Love the land, and you love your family."

"If you're made of taro," said Manapua, "and taro is our brother, are you our BROTHER?"

Poi Popo laughed until she wobbled like pudding. "I am your popo, you steamed silly bun."

---

"Wait," said Pua. "When we eat poi at the luau, are we eating our GRANDMOTHER?"

"You are taking her with you," said Poi Popo. "Why do you think I am always smiling?"

The keiki tumbled into her soft lap. Pua's crown drooped, which on a pineapple means sleepy.

Mac reached one tiny arm out and touched a banyan root. "Goodnight, brother," he whispered.

"From the land," said Poi Popo, "with aloha." And the last torch settled into embers.
