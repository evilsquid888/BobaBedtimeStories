# Story 12: Poi Popo and the Old Island Stories

**Main Character:** Poi Popo
**Guest Characters:** None
**Setting:** Under a banyan tree at the family luau, evening
**Theme:** Where we come from is part of who we are.
**Hawaiian Spotlight:** aloha aina (ah-LOH-hah EYE-nah) = love of the land

---

The luau was winding down, which meant exactly one thing to the keiki: it was Story O'Clock, and absolutely nobody could agree on what that meant.

"It means SCARY stories," said Manapua Mano, bouncing so hard a wisp of steam shot out his swirl-knot. "The kind where a giant musubi chases you and you scream and then you eat the giant musubi anyway!"

"It means PRINCESS stories," argued Pua the Pineapple, tilting her leaf-crown so it caught the last gold light. "With a crown. Specifically, my crown."

"It means snack stories," said Lani the Loco Moco from somewhere deep inside a yawn. "Where everybody takes a nap halfway through. Best part."

And right in the middle of all this glorious bickering, moving like a slow purple cloud across the grass, came Poi Popo — the oldest, softest, most lavender-grey mound of pounded taro on the whole island, flower lei swaying, smelling of warm earth and aloha.

"Tonight," she said gently, "I will tell you the oldest story of all. Come. Under the banyan tree."

The keiki went very, very quiet. When *Popo* said *oldest*, she meant it.

The banyan tree was the grandparent of all the trees — so old its roots dropped down from the branches like extra legs, so wide that the whole family could tuck underneath it like ducklings under a wing. Poi Popo settled herself among the roots with a soft *pomf*, and the little ones gathered close: Manapua Mano still bouncing, Pua trying to look regal and mostly looking nervous, and tiny Mac the Macadamia peeking out of his shell just far enough to listen.

"Popo," whispered Mac, who almost never spoke first, "is it a true story?"

Poi Popo's ancient eyes crinkled like the surface of a calm pond. "The truest, keiki. It is a story about where we come from. And there is no story more important than that one."

She lifted one soft, slow flipper of taro paste toward the sky, where the first stars were blinking awake over the sea.

"Long ago," she began, "before there were luaus, before there was poi, before there was even *me* — and I am very, very old —"

"How old?" Manapua Mano blurted.

"Older than the banyan tree."

"WHOA."

"— there were two brothers," Poi Popo went on. "Sky and Earth had a child, and that child was the very first taro plant. A tiny, droopy, sad little sprout. He could not run. He could not laugh. He could only stand in the mud and reach his leaves toward the sun." She paused. "His name was Haloa."

"That's a sad name for a sad plant," sniffed Pua.

"Ah, but wait, sweetheart," said Poi Popo, and her voice went warm as a banked fire. "Because that little taro plant had a younger brother. A human child. And the human child grew up beside the taro in the very same garden, in the very same mud, drinking the very same rain. The taro held the soil together so the boy's family had ground to stand on. The boy pulled the weeds so the taro had room to breathe. They took care of each other. Brother and brother. Land and family. The very same thing."

Mac crept all the way out of his shell. "The plant and the boy were... 'ohana?" he asked. *'Ohana* meant family — the kind you never, ever leave behind.

"The very first 'ohana," Poi Popo agreed. "And from that taro plant came every taro plant after it. And from those came —" she gave herself a gentle, jiggly pat — "*me*. Poi. And from poi comes the strength in your little bodies, so you can bounce —" she nodded at Manapua "— and wear crowns —" a nod at Pua "— and pop in and out of shells." A nod at Mac, who blushed cream-pink and ducked halfway back in, pleased.

"That," said Poi Popo, "is **aloha aina** (ah-LOH-hah EYE-nah) = love of the land. When you love the land, you are loving your family. Because the land *is* your oldest brother. It fed your popo, and her popo, and her popo before that, all the way back to a droopy little sprout in the mud."

For a moment the keiki were silent, full of wonder.

Then Manapua Mano raised one pillowy hand. "Popo. I have a very serious question."

"Yes, keiki?"

"If the taro is our brother... and you are made of taro... does that mean *you're* our brother?"

Poi Popo laughed — a slow, rolling laugh that shook her whole soft body like pudding in a gentle earthquake. "I am your *popo*, you steamed silly bun. Brother, grandmother, cousin, garden — when you love the land enough, all those words mean the same thing. They mean *home*."

"I knew that," said Manapua, who had not known that.

"Wait, wait, wait," said Pua, sitting up so fast her crown nearly toppled. "If we're *all* family with the taro, and you're made of taro, and *poi* is made of you —" she squinted, doing very serious pineapple math — "then when we eat poi at the luau, are we eating our *grandmother*?"

The whole circle of keiki gasped.

Poi Popo only chuckled, that slow rolling chuckle that made her whole soft body wobble like a happy little earthquake. "Oh, keiki. When you eat the poi, you are not losing your popo. You are *taking her with you.* You carry me in your strong little bodies, and I get to come along on every adventure you ever have. That is the most wonderful gift a grandmother could ask for. Why do you think I am always smiling?"

"So getting eaten is..." Mac began shyly.

"The proudest day of a poi's life," said Poi Popo warmly. "Like a seed finally getting to grow. Like a star finally getting to shine. I would *bounce* if I could bounce."

"I'll bounce for you, Popo!" cried Manapua, and he bounced so high he bonked a low banyan branch and a shower of little leaves came drifting down over everyone like confetti. The keiki dissolved into giggles, and even the gecko in the tree chirped what sounded suspiciously like a laugh.

When the giggling settled, Poi Popo brushed a leaf gently off the top of her head. "Now. Where was I? Ah. The first taro and the first boy."

She told them how, in the old days, families would walk down into the cool wet **lo'i** (LOH-ee) = taro patch with their bare feet sinking in the mud, and how the children would learn to plant the little taro starts in tidy rows, and how an auntie would sing while she worked so the taro would grow up hearing music.

"You *sang* to the plants?" Pua asked, delighted.

"Of course," said Poi Popo. "How else would they feel loved? A plant grows tall on sunshine and rain — but it grows *strong* on aloha. The same as a keiki." She tilted her grey head. "Your popos sang to you when you were tiny sprouts, too. You just don't remember. The land remembers for you."

"What did they sing?" whispered Mac.

So Poi Popo hummed an old, slow island tune, low as the tide, soft as warm rain — a song older than the banyan tree, a song her own popo had hummed into the lo'i a hundred years before. And the keiki felt something settle deep in their chests, warm and round and certain, like a stone that had finally found exactly where it belonged.

But just then a cool little wind came whispering through the banyan branches, and Pua the Pineapple shivered and looked over her shoulder into the dark beyond the tree. "Popo," she said, trying to sound brave and braving it about halfway, "what's *out there*? In the dark? Past the luau lights?"

The other keiki edged closer together. The night beyond the banyan suddenly seemed very wide.

Now, a different sort of grandparent might have made a spooky story of it. But Poi Popo only smiled and held out her soft arms, and the keiki tumbled in against her like little ducklings finding the warm middle of the nest.

"Out there," she said, "is the same land that loves you. Listen."

So they listened.

And under her quiet, the night stopped being scary and started being *full*. They heard the **moana** (moh-AH-nah) = ocean breathing in and out against the reef, slow as Honu Haupia's flippers. They heard the wind combing through the taro patches down in the valley — *their* brothers, all of them, whispering goodnight. They heard a gecko chirp, and a far-off ukulele, and the soft *fffp* of the last luau torch settling into embers.

"That's not the dark being scary," murmured Mac, astonished. "That's the dark being... cozy."

"The land never sleeps angry," said Poi Popo. "The land just tucks itself in. Same as you."

Pua's crown drooped a little — not from sadness, but from sleepiness, which on a pineapple looks remarkably similar. "Popo," she said, "I'm sorry I wanted my story to be about my crown."

"Why be sorry, sweetheart?"

"Because the taro brother story was better. And he didn't even *have* a crown. He was just... droopy. And he held the whole world together anyway."

Poi Popo pressed a soft kiss to the top of Pua's leaf-crown. "That, my dear, is the most aloha aina thing you have ever said. The smallest, droopiest, most ordinary thing in the garden held up the sky. That is the secret of this whole island. The quiet ones —" a wink at Mac — "and the bouncy ones —" a wink at Manapua, who beamed — "and the proud ones learning to be kind —" a gentle squeeze of Pua — "all of you are made of the same mud and the same rain and the same brother-plant standing in the sun. You are *one*."

"'Ohana," whispered all three keiki at once.

"'Ohana," said Poi Popo. "And land. And love. The very same thing."

Down at the luau, the grown-ups were beginning the slow, happy work of stacking ti leaves and dousing torches and carrying sleepy little ones to bed. But under the banyan tree, in the warm purple hush, the keiki had one last thing to do.

They each reached out — Mac with a tiny shy flipper, Manapua with a soft steamy hand, Pua with the very tip of her crown — and touched the ancient roots of the banyan tree that dropped down all around them like the legs of a giant, patient grandfather.

"Goodnight, brother," they said to the tree.

"Goodnight, land," they said to the valley.

"Goodnight, Popo," they said, snuggling into the softest, oldest, most lavender-grey lap on the entire island.

And Poi Popo, who was older than the banyan tree, who carried the whole long story of the taro inside her gentle grey body, who had told this very story to *their* popos when *they* were small, gathered her keiki close beneath the stars.

"From the land," she whispered, "with aloha."

The torches burned low. The ocean breathed slow. And the first taro brother, somewhere out in the dark valley, leaned his big green leaves toward the moon and held the whole sleeping island gently in his roots — the way he always had, the way he always would.

## Goodnight Blessing
Little one, you are made of rain and starlight and the patient old land that loves you. Somewhere in the dark, the taro leans toward the moon and holds the whole island safe, just for you. The ocean is breathing slow, the banyan is tucking in its roots, and every grandparent who ever told a story is whispering the same soft words across the years: you are home, you are loved, you are 'ohana. So close your sparkly eyes and let the warm earth hold you, from the land, with aloha. Goodnight.
