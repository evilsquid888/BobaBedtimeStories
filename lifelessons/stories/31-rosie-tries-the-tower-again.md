# 31 — Rosie Tries the Tower Again

**Life skill:** Respond to a frustrating task by pausing and trying one manageable change.
**Magic sentence:** "Can we try one small change?"
**Coaches:** Boba 🇹🇼 + Tato the Patacon 🇨🇷
**Setting:** the garden treehouse platform (practice), then the wooden toy maker's workshop (real outing).

---

## The Story

Rosie wanted to build a tall wooden tower, with a window near the top for a tiny carved owl.

Boba bounced gently beside the treehouse's acorn cup and wooden blocks.

"What if it keeps falling?" Rosie asked. Tato planted his sturdy feet as if personally negotiating with gravity.

Boba arrived, a glossy pearl bouncing gently.

Tato arrived, a sturdy golden plantain patty.

Cat the penguin napped on a rope-basket cushion.

Boba watched a pretend tower wobble. He paused and asked Tato, **"Can we try one small change?"**

"Frustration is a feeling, not an instruction to throw," Tato explained. "We can make the next try smaller."

2. Name the feeling.
3. Pause before touching the pieces.

4. Choose one small change.
5. Try again or choose a break.

Mom took Rosie to the toy maker's workshop, where smooth wooden blocks waited on soft building mats.

Cat claimed the carved-animal display bench, settling beside an owl considerably more alert than the visiting penguin.

Guagua chose some blocks too. Their towers grew beside each other, above a tray of tiny wooden windows.

Rosie's top block slipped, and her tower tumbled onto the mat. Step one: she stopped reaching for pieces.

Step two: "I feel frustrated," she told Mom. She had wanted a tall tower before the owl moved in.

Step three: she paused, keeping the blocks on the mat. **"Can we try one small change?"**

Guagua suggested rebuilding the whole tower differently, showing her several blocks at once. That felt like too much.

Rosie's straw drooped. She felt overwhelmed. That's okay; helpful suggestions could still need a smaller starting place.

Rosie took a Rose breath.

Smell the rose: in, two, three, four.

Blow out the candle: out, two, three, four.

"Please help me choose just one thing," Rosie said, looking at the two blocks nearest her cup.

Mom and Guagua showed her a wide bottom block. Rosie compared it with the narrow one she had used.

Step four: Rosie chose the wider base, leaving the other changes for later instead of changing everything at once.

Step five: she tried again. The shorter tower stood steadily, with enough room for the little carved owl.

Tato nodded. Boba rocked proudly. "Strong downstairs. The owl's swimming pool can be an extension."

Cat opened an eye beside the display owl. "My neighbor appears to own two houses. I request one pillow."

Rosie giggled. Her tower was smaller than planned, but her hands were calm and the owl had a window.

Tori met Rosie beside the workshop's carved-door display, where she could see the finished tower on its mat.

"You made one useful change. You've got this, little sip," she said, admiring the broad bottom block.

Rosie felt proud. She could build something good without solving every wobble all at once.

**加油** (jiāyóu) = go for it.

"One step at a time, one sip at a time," said Rosie.

## 🌹 Rosie's Steps (Recap)

1. Stop when you feel frustrated.
2. Name the feeling.
3. Pause before touching the pieces.
4. Choose one small change.
5. Try again or choose a break.

**If something surprising happens:** Take a Rose breath or a comfortable pause, then say: "Please help me choose just one thing."

## 💬 Practice Together (for grown-ups)

- Role-play the situation using a wide wooden building block or a safe pretend substitute; read the five steps together.
- Swap roles so your child can see you model **"Can we try one small change?"** and hear the other person's answer.
- Introduce this story's small surprise; practice "Please help me choose just one thing." and allow a comfortable breath or pause.
- On a real outing, stay nearby, offer choices, and praise the specific step your child tried.
